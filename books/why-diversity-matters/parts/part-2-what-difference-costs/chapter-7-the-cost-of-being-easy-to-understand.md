# **Chapter 7**

## **The Cost of Being Easy to Understand**

A friend once told me about getting lost on a hiking trail. The trail was well marked, the weather was good, the map seemed straightforward, and several people were hiking together. For the first hour everything felt normal.

Then one member of the group became uneasy. Nothing was obviously wrong — no missing signs, no dramatic change in terrain, no clear evidence that they had left the trail. Just a growing sense that something didn't fit.

A bend in the path seemed unfamiliar. The elevation felt different than expected. The landmarks appeared slightly out of place.

The concerns were difficult to explain. Each observation sounded minor by itself, the map still appeared to support the route, the trail still looked like a trail. The explanation for staying the course was simple; the explanation for turning around was complicated.

So the group kept walking.

An hour later they discovered they had taken a wrong turn. A storm months earlier had damaged part of the trail, and the route no longer matched the map.

The uneasy hiker had been noticing dozens of small inconsistencies. The problem was not that the observations were wrong — the problem was that they were difficult to communicate.

"I have a bad feeling about this" is rarely persuasive, especially when everyone else can point to something concrete. The map says we're right. The sign says we're right. The trail looks right.

One explanation compresses easily. The other does not.

I've thought about that story often because it seems to describe a larger pattern. Some realities are easier to explain than others. Some concerns arrive neatly packaged; others arrive as fragments — a collection of small observations, a vague sense that something does not fit, a suspicion that the explanation everyone accepts may be incomplete.

Human beings tend to trust information that is easy to process. This is not a moral failure; it is a practical necessity. The world contains more information than we can evaluate directly, so we rely on summaries, categories, narratives, rules of thumb. Without them we would be overwhelmed.

The difficulty is that reality does not arrive in equally convenient forms. Some truths fit neatly into existing explanations; others resist compression. They require context, patience, translation, additional attention.

The result is that systems often reward information that is easy to understand rather than information that is most accurate. A confident explanation frequently outperforms a tentative one. A simple story often outcompetes a complicated reality. A familiar pattern feels more trustworthy than an unfamiliar one.

This happens in ways that are so ordinary we rarely notice them. The person who can explain a concern in thirty seconds is often heard more clearly than the person who needs thirty minutes. The problem that fits existing categories receives attention more quickly than the problem that challenges those categories. The risk everyone already understands feels more urgent than the risk nobody has encountered before.

The difference is not always correctness.

The difference is legibility.

Some realities travel more easily than others. This creates a challenge for people whose experiences do not fit familiar patterns.

A person living with chronic illness may spend years trying to describe symptoms that sound disconnected to everyone else. A caregiver may notice subtle changes long before anyone else sees them. A newcomer may recognize assumptions that longtime members have stopped noticing. A customer may encounter problems that never appear in internal reports. An employee may sense a risk before they can fully explain it.

The pattern appears repeatedly. The people closest to a problem are often carrying information that is difficult to translate.

The information exists. The challenge is making it visible.

This is one reason diversity matters — not because every perspective is equally correct, not because disagreement is inherently valuable, but because different experiences reveal different signals. Different lives make different details visible.

The challenge is that visibility alone is not enough. The signal must survive the journey. It must compete with explanations that are simpler, cleaner, more familiar, more persuasive.

That competition is not always fair. Systems tend to favor information that arrives in forms they already recognize.

The irony is that many important discoveries begin as weak signals — a pattern that does not quite fit, a concern that sounds difficult to explain, a possibility that feels unlikely, a question nobody else is asking.

The first person to notice a problem is often unable to prove it. The first person to notice a change is often unable to explain it fully. The first person to recognize a blind spot may not yet have the language to describe what they are seeing.

By the time the evidence becomes obvious, the opportunity to learn early has often passed.

This is one reason healthy systems create space for uncertainty — not because uncertainty is comfortable, but because certainty is frequently premature. A system that only trusts information once it becomes obvious will discover many things too late.

The older I get, the less interested I become in who is right during moments like these. I find myself asking different questions: which realities are hardest to explain, which concerns require the most translation, whose observations are easiest to dismiss, what signals are we overlooking because they arrive in unfamiliar forms?

The answers are rarely obvious. Yet many important forms of learning begin there — not with certainty, but with a faint signal that something does not fit.

A signal that is easy to ignore.

Until reality makes it impossible.
