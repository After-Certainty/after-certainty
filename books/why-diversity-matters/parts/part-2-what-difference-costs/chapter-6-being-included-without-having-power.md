# **Chapter 6**

## **Being Included Without Having Power**

A friend once told me about a town meeting she attended for several years. The meetings were open to the public — anyone could attend, anyone could speak — and the organizers often reminded everyone that community participation mattered. On paper, the process seemed remarkably inclusive.

Residents came. Questions were asked, concerns were raised, ideas were discussed. My friend attended regularly, as did many of her neighbors. Over time she began noticing a pattern.

The same issues appeared repeatedly. People would raise concerns; the concerns would be acknowledged, and the discussion would move on. Months later the same concerns would return — again they would be acknowledged, again they would disappear.

Nothing dramatic happened. Nobody was silenced, removed, or told they could not participate. The process remained polite, open, inclusive.

Yet somehow very little changed.

One evening after a meeting she found herself struggling to explain why she felt frustrated. Then she realized something.

The town was listening.

The town simply was not learning.

At first the distinction sounded strange. Listening and learning often feel like the same thing.

They are not.

A person can hear an observation without allowing it to change their understanding. A group can collect feedback without allowing it to influence decisions. An institution can create opportunities for participation while remaining remarkably resistant to influence.

The difference matters because many systems measure inclusion through visibility: who attended, who participated, who was represented? Those questions are important, but they are also incomplete.

A different question often reveals more: what changed because they were there?

The answer is frequently uncomfortable. Sometimes the answer is very little.

Human beings have a tendency to confuse access with influence. The confusion is understandable — influence is difficult to measure, attendance is easy. A seat at the table is visible; the ability to shape what happens next is often invisible.

As a result, many systems become quite good at creating participation. The harder challenge is creating responsiveness.

A suggestion enters the conversation. Does anything happen? A concern is raised. Does anything change? A perspective becomes visible. Does it alter how the group understands the problem?

These questions reveal whether participation is connected to learning. Without that connection, inclusion can become strangely performative.

People are present. Their experiences are visible, their perspectives acknowledged — yet the underlying assumptions remain largely untouched.

This can be frustrating for everyone involved. The people making decisions often feel they have listened, and in many cases they have. The people providing input often feel ignored, and in many cases they have not been ignored exactly.

Something subtler has happened.

The information entered the system. It simply failed to influence it.

One reason this occurs is that influence is rarely distributed evenly. Every group develops informal maps of credibility — whose concerns deserve attention, whose ideas are likely to be useful, whose objections can be safely discounted.

These maps are not usually written down. Most people could not fully explain them, yet they shape decisions constantly.

The result is that two people may make the same observation and receive very different responses — not because the observation differs, but because the listener's expectations differ.

An idea arrives attached to a person. The person arrives attached to a story, and the story influences how seriously the idea is taken.

This process is often invisible to the people participating in it. The assumptions feel natural, the reactions feel justified, the outcomes feel reasonable.

Yet over time certain patterns emerge. Some voices consistently shape decisions; others consistently inform discussions without affecting outcomes.

Both groups may technically be included. Only one group possesses meaningful influence.

This is one reason diversity alone cannot solve every problem. A diverse group may still learn very little if its pathways of influence remain narrow.

The perspectives exist. The system simply does not know how to use them — or perhaps it knows how and chooses not to.

The result looks similar either way. Learning stalls, the same assumptions continue guiding decisions, the same blind spots persist, the same surprises keep appearing.

The irony is that many organizations become aware of this only after something goes wrong. A problem emerges, a warning surfaces, someone points out that concerns had been raised months or years earlier.

The information existed. The perspective existed. The participation existed.

What was missing was influence.

The older I get, the less interested I become in whether people were invited into the room. That question still matters, but another question increasingly captures my attention.

What happens after they arrive? Can they change anything, alter assumptions, challenge expectations, influence how the group understands reality?

A system that cannot answer yes to those questions may still be inclusive in appearance. Whether it is capable of learning is another matter entirely.

The challenge is not merely making people visible.

The challenge is allowing what they see to matter.
