# **Chapter 4**

## **Why Some Choices Aren't Really Choices**

A friend once told me she hated networking events. Not disliked. Hated.

This surprised me because she was good at them. If you had watched her from across the room, you would have assumed she enjoyed them. She was funny. Comfortable. Easy to talk to—the sort of person who could walk into a room full of strangers and somehow leave with new friends, the kind of person people imagine has never struggled with social situations.

I asked her why she hated them. She laughed. Then she said something I still think about: "It's easy for people to say no when they know they'll be invited somewhere else."

At first, I didn't understand. The statement felt larger than the conversation we were having. She explained.

For some people, an invitation is just an invitation. You go if you want to go. You stay home if you don't. You leave early if you're tired. You decline because you'd rather spend the evening somewhere else. The stakes are low. There will be another event, another introduction, another opportunity, another chance. The invitation is one possibility among many.

For other people, the same invitation carries a different weight. The room may contain potential clients, potential employers, potential friends, potential mentors, potential partners, potential futures.

Missing one event may not matter. But missing enough of them might. The invitation is no longer just an invitation. It becomes a calculation—a small wager against uncertainty. What if this is the room where something changes? What if this is the conversation that leads somewhere? What if everyone else is making connections while I'm at home?

The event remains the same. The experience does not.

I thought about that conversation for a long time because it seemed to explain something much larger than networking. The older I get, the more I notice how often people assume that choices are experienced equally.

Someone leaves a job. Someone stays. Someone speaks up. Someone remains silent. Someone takes a risk. Someone avoids one. We observe the decision and assume we understand it. After all, we can see the available options. What we cannot see are the costs attached to them.

We see the fork in the road. We do not see what each traveler is carrying.

A person with savings experiences unemployment differently than a person without savings. A healthy person experiences risk differently than someone dependent on insurance. A teenager experiences authority differently than a parent. A parent experiences uncertainty differently than a teenager. A person with a strong support network experiences failure differently than someone facing it alone.

The choices may appear identical from a distance. Up close, they rarely are.

We like to imagine that we evaluate situations objectively. But much of what feels obvious to us is simply familiar. Much of what feels reasonable is shaped by circumstances we stopped noticing long ago.

A person who has always had a soft place to land may struggle to understand someone who acts as though falling matters. A person who has repeatedly fallen may struggle to understand someone who treats risk casually. Neither necessarily lacks empathy. Neither necessarily lacks intelligence. They are standing in different landscapes.

This is one of the reasons people often talk past each other. One person sees courage. Another sees recklessness. One person sees caution. Another sees fear. One person sees opportunity. Another sees danger.

The disagreement may appear moral. It may appear political. It may appear personal. Sometimes it is none of those things. Sometimes people are responding rationally to different conditions.

The challenge is that conditions are often invisible. The easiest things to see are actions. The hardest things to see are constraints.

A person declines a promotion—we assume they lack ambition. A person remains in a difficult relationship—we assume they lack courage. A person avoids a confrontation—we assume they lack conviction. A person refuses an opportunity—we assume they do not recognize its value.

Sometimes these explanations are correct. Often they are not.

Human beings are remarkably talented at inventing stories about behavior. We are much less talented at recognizing what information is missing.

The stories become especially dangerous when they harden into systems. Organizations do this. Schools do. Families do. Communities do. Entire societies do.

People who succeed under one set of conditions begin designing systems as though those conditions were universal. The assumptions become invisible precisely because they are shared.

The result is a strange kind of blindness—not blindness to people, but blindness to circumstances. The person making the decision remains visible. The terrain shaping the decision disappears.

This is one of the reasons conversations about diversity so often become frustrating. People assume diversity is primarily about representation: who is present, who is absent, who gets a seat at the table. Those questions matter. But another question matters too: who can see parts of reality that others cannot?

Different lives create different visibility. Different constraints reveal different risks. Different dependencies expose different vulnerabilities. People notice what they must navigate.

The person closest to a cliff usually understands something about cliffs. The person who has never approached one may not think about them at all. Neither perspective is complete. But they are not interchangeable.

A system composed entirely of people who share the same terrain will often mistake their experience for reality itself. This is not because they are malicious. It is not because they lack compassion. It is because familiarity quietly transforms into certainty.

The world begins to look simpler than it is. The map starts replacing the landscape. And eventually people forget there was ever a difference between the two.

The challenge is not simply learning to listen to other people. The challenge is learning that other people may be seeing parts of the world that our own circumstances never required us to notice.

That possibility is uncomfortable. It suggests that some of what feels obvious to us may only feel obvious because of where we happen to be standing.

I still think about my friend's comment: "It's easy for people to say no when they know they'll be invited somewhere else."

The sentence sounded small when she said it. Almost casual. But some observations arrive quietly. They enter the room as anecdotes and leave as lenses.

Once I understood what she meant, I began hearing versions of that sentence everywhere—not in networking events, but in families, in schools, in politics, in friendships, in conversations about opportunity, responsibility, risk, and freedom.

Again and again, I found the same pattern hiding underneath. The choice was visible. The cost was not.
