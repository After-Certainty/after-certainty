# **Chapter 5**

## **Why Words Don't Land the Same Way**

A friend once told me about a conversation with his father that neither of them forgot. The disagreement started with a sentence so ordinary that neither could later remember the exact words—something about moving away, or changing jobs, or choosing a different path than the one his father had imagined. The details faded over time. The feeling remained.

What my friend remembered was that his father said something that sounded, to him, like disappointment. What his father remembered was offering advice. The same conversation produced two entirely different stories. One person walked away feeling judged. The other walked away feeling misunderstood.

Years later they talked about it again. Each was surprised by the other's account. The father genuinely believed he had been supportive. The son genuinely believed he had been criticized. Neither was lying. Neither was manipulating. Neither was trying to hurt the other.

Yet somehow they had occupied different conversations.

I have become increasingly fascinated by misunderstandings like these—not the dramatic ones, not the arguments where people are obviously attacking one another, but the quieter ones. The conversations where everyone involved appears sincere. The conversations where good intentions fail. The conversations where two people leave with entirely different memories of what was said.

Most of us grow up believing that communication works something like transportation. I have an idea. I put the idea into words. The words travel to you. You receive the idea. The process may not be perfect, but we imagine the basic transfer remains intact.

Reality is stranger. Words do not arrive alone. They arrive carrying history. A sentence enters a conversation accompanied by everything that came before it: past experiences, prior relationships, old disappointments, earlier successes. The words themselves may be identical. The baggage attached to them is not.

This becomes easier to see in families. A stranger can say something that barely registers. A parent can say the same thing and leave a mark that lasts for years—not because the sentence changed, but because the relationship changed. The words arrived carrying additional weight.

The same thing happens elsewhere. A joke. A compliment. A warning. A question. A suggestion. What one person experiences as harmless curiosity, another may experience as suspicion. What one person experiences as encouragement, another may experience as pressure. What one person experiences as honesty, another may experience as dismissal.

Neither interpretation necessarily reveals the whole truth. Yet neither emerges from nowhere. Each is connected to a larger story.

This is one reason communication is more difficult than it appears. People often imagine that misunderstandings happen because someone failed to listen. Sometimes that is true. But listening perfectly would not eliminate the problem. The challenge is deeper. The same words enter different worlds.

Consider the phrase: "You can always come back."

To one person it may sound comforting—a door remains open, an option remains available. To another it may sound like doubt, an assumption that they will fail, a prediction disguised as reassurance. The speaker may intend one meaning. The listener may hear another. Neither experience is entirely contained in the sentence itself.

Meaning emerges from the interaction between words and lives.

This becomes particularly important when people come from different backgrounds—different communities, different risks, different assumptions, different histories. The same phrase may activate completely different memories. A statement that feels neutral to one person may connect to years of experience for another.

The difficulty is that these connections are often invisible. We hear the words we spoke. Other people hear the words they received. The difference can be enormous.

And because the process is largely hidden, both sides often become confused. "I didn't mean it that way." "I know. But that's how it sounded."

Conversations frequently stall here. One person defends their intent. The other defends their experience. Both may be correct. Intent matters. Experience matters too. Treating either as irrelevant usually makes understanding harder.

This creates a challenge for diverse groups. Not because diversity creates misunderstanding—misunderstanding exists everywhere. Diversity simply makes certain misunderstandings easier to notice.

People who share similar experiences often interpret language similarly. People with different experiences reveal assumptions that would otherwise remain hidden. The result can feel frustrating. A phrase that seemed obvious suddenly becomes complicated. A word that seemed harmless becomes contested. A conversation that once felt straightforward requires additional care.

Many people experience this as a burden. I increasingly suspect it is a form of learning. The complication is not always a sign that communication has failed. Sometimes it is a sign that reality is larger than we assumed.

The misunderstanding reveals information. It exposes differences in experience that were previously invisible. It shows us where our assumptions stop working.

This does not mean every interpretation is equally valid. It does not mean communication is impossible. It does mean that understanding requires more than transmitting information. It requires curiosity about what other people might be hearing.

The goal is not perfect agreement. The goal is not perfect safety. The goal is not eliminating misunderstanding entirely.

The goal is something more modest: to become aware that other people may be hearing our words through experiences we do not share.

That possibility changes a conversation. It introduces humility. It reminds us that meaning is not something we send. Meaning is something we build together.

The older I get, the less interested I am in determining who was technically correct in conversations like the one between my friend and his father. I find myself asking a different question: what was each person protecting? What history entered the room with them? What fears were attached to the words? What hopes were attached to them?

The answers are rarely contained in the sentence itself. The sentence is only where the story becomes visible. The story was already there. The words simply gave it a place to land.
