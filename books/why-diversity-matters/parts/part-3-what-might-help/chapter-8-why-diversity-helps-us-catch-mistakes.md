# **Chapter 8**

## **Why Diversity Helps Us Catch Mistakes**

Several years ago, I watched a group of people try to move a couch through a doorway. It should not have been memorable — it was a couch, it was a doorway, the challenge seemed straightforward.

Yet for nearly ten minutes everyone involved remained convinced the couch would not fit. Push, rotate, back up, try again — the same conclusion emerged repeatedly. It doesn't fit. The doorway is too small. The couch is too large. The evidence seemed obvious.

Then someone who had been standing quietly near the wall asked a question.

"What if you stand it up?"

The room went silent. A few moments later the couch passed through the doorway.

The dimensions had not changed. The doorway had not changed. The couch had not changed.

Only the perspective had changed.

What fascinates me about stories like this is how often they occur — not because people are foolish, but because reality is larger than any single viewpoint. Every person sees part of the situation; no one sees all of it.

Most of the time this limitation doesn't matter very much. The missing information is small, the assumptions are harmless, the consequences are manageable.

Occasionally the missing piece turns out to matter.

A great deal.

When that happens we often tell ourselves a story about intelligence — someone was smarter, more experienced, more knowledgeable. Sometimes that's true. Often something else happened: someone was standing somewhere different.

The difference matters.

We tend to imagine mistakes as failures of competence. Many are failures of visibility. The information existed, the perspective existed, the warning existed, the possibility existed — the group simply never encountered it, or encountered it too late.

History contains countless examples: accidents that seemed impossible until someone reconstructed what happened; policies that appeared sensible until they encountered people living under different conditions; products that worked perfectly for their designers and poorly for everyone else; medical assumptions that held until researchers examined populations they had previously overlooked.

Again and again the pattern repeats. The mistake was not always a lack of information. Sometimes it was a lack of perspective.

This is one reason diversity is often misunderstood. People hear the word and imagine a social goal, a demographic goal, a political goal. Those conversations matter, but there is another reason diversity matters that receives less attention.

Diversity increases the chances that someone notices what everyone else missed.

The value is not difference itself. Difference can be noisy, frustrating, slow. The value emerges because different experiences illuminate different parts of reality.

A person who has never encountered a particular problem may not think to look for it. Why would they? Human beings notice what their lives require them to notice.

The parent notices things the teenager does not. The teenager notices things the parent does not. The newcomer notices things longtime residents stopped seeing years ago. The longtime resident notices things newcomers cannot yet understand.

Each perspective reveals something. Each perspective conceals something.

The challenge is that groups often mistake shared perspective for complete perspective. Agreement feels reassuring, similarity feels efficient. People who see the world similarly tend to communicate more easily — they require less translation, less explanation, less patience. The process feels smoother.

The danger is that smoothness can become deceptive. A group may be functioning efficiently while collectively overlooking something important.

The absence of friction is not always evidence of understanding. Sometimes it is evidence that everyone shares the same assumptions.

Learning often begins when those assumptions encounter resistance — a question, a concern, an observation, a story that doesn't fit.

The interruption can feel inconvenient, distracting, like someone is slowing progress. Occasionally that is exactly what is happening. More often than we like to admit, the interruption is information — a signal arriving from a place the group cannot currently see.

This is one reason systems that learn well often appear less certain than systems that learn poorly. They make more room for disagreement, more room for questions, more room for experiences that do not fit existing explanations.

The goal is not endless debate. The goal is not treating every opinion as equally correct. The goal is increasing the chances that reality can enter the conversation before reality enters the consequences.

The distinction matters.

A problem discovered early is a discussion. The same problem discovered late becomes a crisis.

Most people can remember a moment when someone warned them about something they did not yet understand — a parent, a friend, a teacher, a stranger. At the time the warning seemed unnecessary. Later it became obvious.

The information was available. The perspective was not.

Understanding arrived only after experience changed what was visible. Diversity allows some of that learning to happen sooner — not perfectly, not completely, but sooner.

We gain access to experiences we have not personally lived. We encounter risks before they become our risks. We hear warnings before consequences force us to listen.

The older I get, the less interested I become in finding the smartest person in the room. Intelligence matters, expertise matters, experience matters — but I find myself asking a different question.

Who can see something the rest of us cannot?

The answer is often surprising. It is not always the loudest person, not always the most educated, not always the most powerful. Sometimes it is simply the person standing in a place no one else has stood.

The couch fit through the doorway because someone saw a possibility others had missed. Most important mistakes are discovered the same way — not because reality changed, but because someone finally looked at it from somewhere else.
