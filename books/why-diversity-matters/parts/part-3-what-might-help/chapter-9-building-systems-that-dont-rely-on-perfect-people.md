# **Chapter 9**

## **Building Systems That Don't Rely on Perfect People**

A friend once told me about getting locked out of her house. She had lived there for years, knew where the key was supposed to be, knew which door she normally used, knew the routine so well that she rarely thought about it.

One afternoon she came home carrying groceries. The key wasn't where she expected. She checked her pockets — nothing. She checked her bag — nothing. She walked around the house; all the doors were locked.

The mistake itself was unremarkable. People forget things, lose things, become distracted.

The interesting part came later. When she told the story, she never described herself as careless or irresponsible. She never talked about the character flaws that produced the problem. Instead, she talked about the spare key hidden at a neighbor's house.

The mistake happened. The system recovered.

The more I thought about that story, the more it reminded me of how we often discuss failure. When something goes wrong, our first instinct is frequently to look for a person — who made the mistake, who missed the warning, who should have known better?

The questions are understandable. Sometimes they are important. Yet they can obscure a different question: why did the system require perfection in the first place?

Human beings forget things. We misunderstand one another, overlook details, become distracted, jump to conclusions, grow tired, carry assumptions we do not recognize.

These are not unusual failures. They are ordinary features of being human.

The surprising thing is not that mistakes occur.

The surprising thing is how many systems continue behaving as though mistakes are optional.

This becomes especially important when discussing diversity. Many conversations focus on helping people become more aware, more empathetic, more informed. Those efforts matter, but awareness has limits.

Even the most thoughtful person cannot see everything. Even the most experienced person carries blind spots. Even the most inclusive group will occasionally misunderstand what it is hearing.

The challenge is not finding perfect people. The challenge is building systems that remain capable of learning when imperfect people inevitably make imperfect decisions.

Consider the difference between a warning and a feedback loop. A warning depends on someone noticing a problem; a feedback loop depends on the system eventually discovering the problem whether anyone noticed it or not.

The distinction matters. One relies on individual perception; the other relies on learning.

Strong systems rarely assume that every important insight will arrive at the right moment from the right person. They assume important things will be missed, then they build mechanisms that increase the chances of discovery.

A pilot follows a checklist — not because pilots are incapable, but because capable people forget things. Scientists publish methods alongside conclusions — not because scientists are dishonest, but because honest people make mistakes. Democratic systems divide authority — not because leaders are always corrupt, but because good leaders can still be wrong.

The pattern appears repeatedly. Resilient systems do not emerge from trust alone; they emerge from a recognition of human limitation.

This is one reason diversity matters in ways that are easy to overlook. The value is not merely that different people notice different things. The value is that diversity creates additional opportunities for error detection — additional chances for assumptions to encounter reality, additional pathways through which a system can learn.

Yet diversity alone is not enough. A warning that cannot influence decisions is merely information. A perspective that never alters behavior is simply observation.

Learning requires mechanisms that connect discovery to change. Otherwise the same mistakes return, again and again.

Many organizations eventually discover this the hard way. After a failure, someone reveals that concerns had been raised months earlier.

The information existed. The perspective existed. The warning existed.

What was missing was a reliable path from awareness to action.

The lesson is often misunderstood. People conclude that they should have listened harder. Sometimes that is true, but listening harder is not a system.

Listening harder is a hope.

Systems become stronger when they assume important things will occasionally be overlooked and then create ways to recover when that happens.

The older I get, the less interested I become in identifying the smartest person in the room. I care much more about whether the room can learn.

Can assumptions be challenged? Can concerns surface? Can mistakes be corrected? Can new information alter old conclusions? Can the system recover when someone gets something wrong?

Those questions tell us far more about future success than any individual credential.

A system that depends on perfect people is fragile. Sooner or later reality will expose its weakness.

A system that expects imperfection has a chance to become resilient.

The goal is not eliminating human error. The goal is building communities, organizations, and institutions that can continue learning despite it.

Because learning is not what happens when people get everything right.

Learning is what happens after they discover they were wrong.
