# **Chapter 2**

## **When Everyone Agrees — and Everyone Is Wrong**

One winter, a friend told me about growing up in a small town where everyone seemed to know the weather before it arrived. Not because they watched forecasts. Because they watched each other.

Someone's grandfather would mention a storm coming in. A neighbor would repeat it. The cashier at the grocery store would mention hearing the same thing. By evening the prediction had become common knowledge. The storm was coming. Everyone knew it.

Sometimes they were right. Sometimes they were wrong.

The interesting part was not the forecast. The interesting part was how certainty spread. The prediction felt more believable each time it returned from a different direction. By the time four people had repeated it, it no longer felt like information. It felt like confirmation.

Nobody needed evidence. The agreement itself became evidence.

I've thought about that story often because it describes something human beings do remarkably well. We mistake repetition for verification—not because we are foolish, but because agreement is comforting. Agreement reduces uncertainty. Agreement makes the world easier to navigate.

If everyone around us believes something, we gain a powerful shortcut. We no longer need to investigate the claim ourselves. The group has already done the work. Or so it appears.

Most of us learn this lesson early. A child notices what makes other children laugh. A teenager notices what everyone else seems to know. Adults do the same thing, though we often use more sophisticated language: common sense, best practice, professional judgment, tradition, culture, institutional knowledge.

The names change. The mechanism often remains the same. A belief gains strength because many people hold it. The fact that many people learned it from one another becomes difficult to see.

What looks like independent confirmation may actually be a single idea echoing through a network. A room full of mirrors can create the illusion of endless depth. The image remains the same.

This becomes especially dangerous when people share similar experiences. Not because similar experiences are bad—because they create similar assumptions. People who have traveled the same roads tend to notice the same landmarks. They also tend to miss the same things.

This is one reason blind spots are so difficult to detect. A blind spot is not simply something you cannot see. It is often something nobody around you can see.

If everyone overlooks the same detail, the omission disappears. It begins to feel natural. The missing piece becomes part of the background—like the hum of an air conditioner, like a smell you stop noticing after a few minutes, like an accent you no longer hear because everyone around you speaks the same way.

The absence becomes ordinary.

This is why some mistakes survive for so long. Not because nobody looked. Because everyone looked from roughly the same place.

History is full of examples: medical research conducted primarily on certain populations and then generalized to everyone else; products designed by people who assumed users would behave as they did; policies created by leaders who never encountered the problems those policies produced.

The pattern appears so frequently that it begins to look less like failure and more like a feature of human perception. We see from where we stand. There is no shame in that.

The difficulty begins when we forget that we are standing somewhere. Perspective has a strange habit of disguising itself as reality. The water feels invisible to the fish. The atmosphere feels invisible to the bird. The assumptions surrounding us often feel invisible too—especially when everyone around us shares them.

One of the most unsettling experiences a person can have is discovering that a belief they considered obvious is not obvious at all. A conversation. A friendship. A book. A move to a new place. A chance encounter with someone whose life unfolded differently.

Suddenly the certainty weakens—not because reality changed, but because another part of reality became visible.

This can feel threatening. Many people experience it as disagreement. Sometimes it is. But often something more interesting is happening. The world is becoming larger. Additional information is arriving. A map that once seemed complete begins revealing its edges.

The temptation is to defend the old map. Sometimes we should. Not every challenge is correct. Not every dissenting voice reveals a hidden truth. Difference alone guarantees nothing.

But neither does agreement.

Consensus is not evidence. Popularity is not verification. Shared confidence is not understanding.

Groups can be wise. Groups can also be synchronized. The two are not the same thing.

This is one reason diversity matters. A diverse group does not guarantee better decisions. What it often provides is something more basic: additional opportunities to notice—different assumptions, different experiences, different questions, different reasons to doubt what everyone else takes for granted.

The value is not that disagreement makes us smarter. The value is that disagreement sometimes reveals what agreement concealed.

Learning often begins there—not when everyone reaches the same conclusion, but when someone notices that everyone reached the same conclusion for the same reason.

That realization can be uncomfortable. It interrupts certainty. It complicates decisions. It slows things down. It asks us to examine assumptions we would rather leave untouched.

Yet many of the most expensive mistakes begin with a simpler story. Everyone agreed. Everyone was thoughtful. Everyone acted in good faith. Everyone looked.

And everyone happened to be looking in the same direction.
