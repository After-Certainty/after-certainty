# **Chapter 1**

## **Why "Having Diverse People" Isn't Enough**

A friend once told me about a community garden in her neighborhood. The garden had been built on an empty lot. Volunteers maintained it. Families visited on weekends. Children helped plant vegetables each spring.

By most measures it was a success. People cared about it. People participated. People felt proud of it.

Each year a small group of volunteers would meet to decide what to plant—tomatoes, peppers, beans, squash. The discussions were friendly. Suggestions were welcomed. Everyone was encouraged to contribute.

One year a woman who had joined recently suggested planting something different. Not replacing the existing crops—just adding a few varieties the group had never grown before.

The response was polite. People nodded. Someone mentioned that the current approach worked well. Another person pointed out that everyone already knew how to care for the existing plants. The conversation moved on.

No argument followed. No one rejected the idea outright. The suggestion simply disappeared.

Months later a drought arrived. Several of the garden's most common crops struggled. The harvest was smaller than expected.

During a conversation about what had happened, someone remembered the earlier suggestion. The plants the woman had mentioned happened to be more tolerant of the conditions the garden had encountered.

The observation was uncomfortable—not because anyone had behaved badly. Nobody had. The group had been welcoming. The group had been respectful. The group had listened.

Yet somehow an idea with value had entered the conversation and left without changing anything.

I think about that story often because it reveals a distinction we rarely discuss. Being heard is not the same thing as being influential. And influence is not simply a matter of authority. It is often a matter of expectation.

Human beings are constantly forming ideas about where valuable information is likely to come from: who understands the situation, who has experience, who has good judgment, who usually has useful ideas.

These assumptions are not irrational. Most of the time they help us navigate an impossibly complicated world. The problem is that every shortcut leaves something out—including shortcuts about people.

The longer we know someone, the easier it becomes to summarize them: the quiet person, the confident person, the experienced person, the newcomer, the expert, the organizer, the helper, the skeptic.

The categories make social life manageable. They also create blind spots. Once we have decided who someone is, it becomes surprisingly easy to overlook parts of them that do not fit the summary.

The challenge is not hostility. The challenge is familiarity. We begin seeing our description of a person instead of the person themselves.

This is one reason diversity is often misunderstood. Many discussions focus on who is present—who was invited, who received a seat at the table. Those questions matter. But another question matters too: what happens when someone says something unexpected? What happens when insight arrives from a place we were not expecting to find it?

That moment reveals whether a group is merely diverse or genuinely capable of learning.

A group can include many different people while remaining remarkably narrow in what it notices. The reason is simple: diversity does not automatically challenge assumptions. People must be willing to reconsider where value comes from, where expertise comes from, where insight comes from, where understanding comes from.

That is harder than it sounds. Most of us like to believe we judge ideas on their merits. Sometimes we do. Yet ideas rarely arrive detached from people. An idea arrives attached to a voice, a history, a reputation, a set of expectations.

The same observation can land differently depending on who makes it. The same warning can receive different levels of attention. The same question can be interpreted as insightful, disruptive, naïve, or profound depending on what listeners already believe about the person asking it.

These judgments happen quickly—often unconsciously. They feel natural because they are built from real experiences. Yet they remain judgments. And judgments can be wrong.

The result is that groups sometimes miss exactly what they claim to value—not because the perspective was absent, but because the perspective never became visible as valuable.

There is an important difference. Many organizations try to solve this problem by increasing representation. That can help. But representation alone cannot solve a deeper issue.

A group may contain perspectives capable of preventing mistakes, revealing opportunities, or exposing assumptions. Those perspectives only matter if the group remains open to being surprised.

That is the part we discuss less often. Learning requires more than diversity. It requires humility—the willingness to believe that useful insight may arrive from someone we have already summarized, someone we think we understand, someone we have unconsciously stopped being curious about.

The community garden did not fail because it lacked good people. It did not fail because anyone intended exclusion. It failed, briefly, because everyone already had a model of how things worked.

The model included crops, weather, soil, experience. What it did not fully include was the possibility that an unfamiliar voice might be seeing something important.

That possibility is easy to overlook. It is also where many of the benefits of diversity begin—not with different people, but with the realization that different people may know things we do not expect them to know.

The challenge is not simply inviting them into the conversation. The challenge is remaining curious enough to discover what we have not yet learned from them.
