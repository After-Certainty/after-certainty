# **Chapter 3**

## **Bias Isn't Always What We Think It Is**

A friend once told me about a neighbor she had known for years. They waved when they saw each other, talked occasionally, exchanged small favors—the sort of relationship many people have with the people living nearby.

One summer the neighbor began spending long hours outside. He planted flowers. Repaired a fence. Built raised garden beds. Whenever she passed by, he seemed to be working on some new project.

Over time she developed a story about him: retired, handy, quiet—the kind of person who enjoyed staying busy. The story felt accurate. After all, it was built from observation.

Then one afternoon they ended up talking longer than usual. During the conversation she learned something she had never known. The man was not retired. He was recovering from a serious illness. The projects were part of his physical therapy. The raised beds existed because bending over remained difficult. Many of the things she had interpreted as hobbies were accommodations.

The story she had built collapsed almost instantly—not because it had been malicious, but because it had been incomplete.

What fascinated her was how natural the original story had felt. Nothing about it seemed unreasonable. The evidence appeared to support it. The conclusions fit the available information. Yet the picture had still been wrong.

I've thought about that story often because it captures something human beings do remarkably well. We create summaries. Then we forget they are summaries.

Without summaries, daily life would become impossible. Every person we meet contains more complexity than we can fully understand. Every situation contains more detail than we can process. To navigate the world, we compress: the funny coworker, the strict parent, the reliable friend, the difficult customer, the expert, the newcomer.

The labels help us think. They help us predict. They help us coordinate. Most of the time they are useful.

The trouble begins when usefulness starts masquerading as understanding. A summary is not a person. A category is not a life. A pattern is not a complete explanation. Yet we routinely treat them as if they are.

This is one reason conversations about bias can become difficult. Many people hear the word and immediately think of hostility—discrimination, bigotry, prejudice. Those forms of bias exist. They matter. They cause real harm.

But there is another form that often receives less attention: the bias that emerges when we become too confident in our understanding, the bias of believing we already know who someone is, the bias of mistaking familiarity for knowledge.

Most of us have experienced this from both sides. We have looked at another person and quietly concluded: I understand them. At the same time, we have all experienced moments when someone looked at us and reached the wrong conclusion—not a completely wrong conclusion, but a partially wrong one, the most convincing kind, the conclusion built from real observations, the conclusion that misses something important.

Those misunderstandings often linger because they are difficult to detect. A person who dislikes you may be obvious. A person who misunderstands you can be much harder to recognize. The misunderstanding frequently sounds reasonable. It feels reasonable too. That is what makes it powerful.

Human beings are prediction machines. We are constantly trying to determine what kind of situation we are in, what kind of person we are dealing with, what kind of outcome to expect. The process is efficient. It is also dangerous—especially when our confidence exceeds our information.

The danger is not simply that we misunderstand people. The danger is that our understanding changes how we listen to them.

Once we have built a model of someone, new information begins arriving through that model. The helpful suggestion from one person sounds insightful. The same suggestion from another sounds surprising. The concern raised by one person sounds important. The same concern raised by another sounds pessimistic.

The observation is identical. The interpretation changes—not because we are evaluating the idea alone, but because we are evaluating the person attached to it.

This happens so naturally that we rarely notice it. The assumptions feel invisible because they arrive disguised as experience. After all, our conclusions came from somewhere. We observed things. We noticed patterns. We learned from previous interactions. The model is not imaginary. It is simply incomplete.

The problem is not that we create models. The problem is forgetting that they remain models.

This is where diversity becomes unexpectedly relevant. One of the most valuable things diverse experiences provide is not merely different perspectives on the world. They also challenge our assumptions about people.

The more kinds of people we encounter, the harder it becomes to believe that simple summaries are sufficient. Reality keeps escaping the categories. The person surprises us. Then surprises us again. And again.

Learning often begins there—not when we discover someone is different from us, but when we discover they are different from our explanation of them.

The older I get, the less interested I become in the question: who is biased? And more interested in another question: where have I become too certain about someone? Which person have I quietly summarized? Whose complexity have I compressed into a story that is easier for me to carry?

The answers are rarely comfortable. Yet many important forms of understanding begin with exactly that discomfort—the realization that another human being contains more than our description of them, and that reality, once again, is larger than the model we built to explain it.
