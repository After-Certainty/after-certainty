# **Chapter 4**

## **The Chair**

An empty chair can organize a room before anyone sits in it.

People entering a meeting glance at the table and choose their places. Some take the seats nearest the door. Some leave space between themselves and the head of the table. A person who arrives early may place a notebook beside one chair to reserve it for someone who has not yet appeared.

Conversation begins, but not everything begins.

People wait to see who will sit at the front. They postpone the difficult question. They speak more freely while the chair remains empty, then shorten their sentences when footsteps approach in the hallway.

Nothing about the wood or fabric explains this.

The chair may be identical to the others. Its authority comes from the relationships arranged around it: who is expected to occupy it, what decisions that person can make, what others believe the role permits, and how the room has learned to interpret the occupant’s behavior.

Once the person sits down, small actions acquire weight.

A sigh becomes a judgment.

A glance toward the clock becomes permission to stop.

A laugh tells the room that a remark is acceptable.

Silence may mean patience, disapproval, confusion, restraint, or simple fatigue. But when everyone is watching the same person, silence rarely remains empty.

The person in the chair does not become more perceptive merely by sitting there. They do not become less frightened, less vain, less generous, or less capable of error. They carry the same ordinary human capacities into a position where those capacities become unusually consequential.

This is one way leadership begins.

Not with superior humanity.

With concentrated attention.

---

By March 1783, officers in the Continental Army had reasons to be angry.

The Revolutionary War was nearing its end, but many had not received the pay Congress had promised. Questions about pensions and future compensation remained unresolved. The officers had served through years of danger, shortages, and separation from their families. Peace threatened to send them home carrying public gratitude but little financial security.

Anonymous writings circulated among them at the army’s cantonment near Newburgh, New York. The documents urged the officers to meet and consider stronger measures against Congress. Depending on whether the war continued, the army might refuse further service or use its collective pressure to force payment. George Washington regarded the developing crisis as a threat to military discipline and to the young republic’s civilian government.[^c4-newburgh-context]

Washington did not respond by pretending the officers had no grievance.

He shared much of their frustration. He had repeatedly pressed Congress over the army’s compensation, and he knew what the officers and soldiers had endured. But he also understood what it would mean for an armed force to use its leverage against the elected civil authority it had fought to establish.

He ordered that the unofficial meeting be replaced by a formal gathering on March 15.

The officers assembled in a large hall known as the Temple of Virtue. Washington was not expected to attend. Then he entered and addressed them.[^c4-temple-meeting]

The words he had prepared were serious and careful. He acknowledged their suffering, attacked the anonymous appeals, and asked the officers to consider whether they would damage the cause for which they had already sacrificed so much. He promised to continue advocating for them while urging them not to abandon the authority of Congress.[^c4-washington-address]

The speech did not immediately transform the room.

The officers had heard appeals to patience before. They knew Washington’s public language. They had spent years receiving orders, assurances, and promises while their own financial lives deteriorated.

Then Washington attempted to read a letter from a member of Congress.

He struggled with the words. He reached for a pair of spectacles that many of the officers had never seen him wear and asked their pardon, saying that he had grown gray in their service and was now growing blind.[^c4-spectacles]

The gesture was small.

No new policy had appeared. Congress had not suddenly issued the money. Washington had not produced evidence that the officers’ fears were unfounded. He had put on glasses.

But the room changed.

Accounts of the event describe officers weeping. The complaint before them had been directed toward distant political institutions and broken promises. In that moment, the man at the front no longer appeared only as commander in chief. His aging body became visible as part of the same years they believed the country had forgotten. The symbol of authority revealed the cost carried by the person occupying it.

The officers soon rejected the anonymous proposals and adopted resolutions affirming confidence in Washington and the civilian government. The immediate threat subsided, while Washington continued pressing Congress over compensation.[^c4-resolutions]

The scene is often told as evidence of Washington’s greatness.

It deserves some of that interpretation.

He chose to enter a hostile room rather than rely only on command. He acknowledged the legitimacy of the grievance while opposing the proposed remedy. His standing, restraint, and accumulated credibility mattered. Another person performing the same gesture might have appeared theatrical, manipulative, or irrelevant.

Yet the event becomes less understandable if we imagine the spectacles as a magical act produced by a singular leader.

The officers brought years of relationship into the room.

They had watched Washington remain with the army through defeats, winter camps, shortages, political criticism, and uncertainty. His authority did not come only from rank. It came from what the officers believed his conduct had shown over time. The spectacles carried meaning because the relationship already existed.

The room made the gesture consequential.

The gesture also changed the room.

Leadership occurred in the exchange.

---

We often imagine leadership as something a person possesses.

They have charisma.

They have vision.

They have courage.

They have command presence.

These descriptions may identify real qualities, but they can make leadership sound like a substance located inside one body. The leader enters, releases it into the room, and causes everyone else to follow.

The chair suggests a different account.

Leadership depends partly upon where attention has gathered.

A person can possess judgment without anyone treating their judgment as authoritative. They can speak clearly and remain unheard. Another person can offer an ordinary observation that reorganizes the entire group because everyone has already begun watching them.

Position matters.

So does history.

So does interpretation.

The leader’s response becomes a signal because other people use it to decide what the situation means.

When a senior executive calls a failure “a learning opportunity,” employees listen for more than the phrase. They watch whether anyone will be punished, whether the person who raised the problem remains respected, and whether the executive’s own decisions are included in the learning.

When a parent reacts calmly to a crash in another room, children decide whether to feel alarmed.

When a political leader describes a group as dangerous, supporters learn where suspicion is permitted.

When the person running a meeting admits that they do not understand, questions that had been withheld may suddenly enter the room.

Leadership does not consist only of issuing instructions. It shapes the conditions under which other people interpret their own agency.

A person in authority may never say, “Do not disagree with me.”

They may need only to respond defensively the first few times someone does.

They may never announce that speed matters more than safety.

They may simply praise every person who moves quickly and treat every delay as failure.

They may never tell people to hide uncertainty.

They may reward the performance of confidence until uncertainty disappears from public conversation.

The chair teaches without formally teaching.

People study its occupant because the occupant can distribute consequences.

---

This is why authority cannot be reduced to attention alone.

A widely admired person may influence how others think without possessing formal control. A manager may be disliked and still determine pay, promotion, access, and employment. A commander can translate interpretation into orders. A public official can convert judgment into law or force.

Power changes what a signal can do.

Washington’s words mattered partly because of accumulated trust, but he was also the commander of the army. The officers did not encounter him as another participant offering an opinion. His presence carried a history of formal authority, personal sacrifice, institutional legitimacy, and the possibility of sanction.

The leader’s humanity remains ordinary.

Their leverage does not.

This distinction matters because praise and blame often confuse human worth with consequence.

We elevate leaders when events go well, as though the successful use of leverage proves superior character. We degrade them when events go badly, as though every failure emerged from defects unique to the person at the center.

Both responses can hide the structure around the chair.

A leader may benefit from capable people, favorable timing, inherited trust, and systems that correct mistakes before those mistakes become visible. Another may occupy a damaged institution where every choice carries costs created long before their arrival.

But structure does not make the occupant irrelevant.

The person in the chair decides which warnings to hear, which voices to invite, how uncertainty will be treated, and whether authority will enlarge the group’s capacity or substitute for it.

The role magnifies what the person brings.

It also magnifies what they fail to notice.

---

On December 28, 1978, United Airlines Flight 173 approached Portland International Airport.

The aircraft’s landing gear did not appear to deploy normally. A loud sound and unusual vibration raised concern that the gear might not be safely locked. The captain placed the aircraft into a holding pattern while the crew assessed the situation and prepared the passengers for a possible emergency landing.[^c4-ua173-gear]

The original problem was serious.

A damaged landing gear could cause injury, fire, or loss of control upon landing. The crew had reason to investigate it carefully. They spent roughly an hour circling near the airport while trying to understand the indication, consulting procedures, and preparing the cabin.[^c4-ua173-hold]

As the aircraft remained aloft, another problem grew.

Fuel was running out.

The gauges were visible. The first officer and flight engineer made references to the fuel state. Yet the captain remained preoccupied with the landing gear problem and the preparations surrounding it. The urgency of the fuel situation did not become the controlling interpretation in the cockpit soon enough.[^c4-ua173-fuel]

The engines began to fail.

Flight 173 crashed in a wooded residential area outside Portland. Ten people died, including eight passengers and two crew members. The National Transportation Safety Board concluded that the probable cause was the captain’s failure to monitor and respond properly to the aircraft’s fuel state while concentrating on the landing gear malfunction. The Board also found that the other crew members did not fully grasp the fuel emergency or communicate their concern successfully to the captain.[^c4-ntsb-ua173]

The accident is not a simple story of one powerful person refusing to listen.

The captain did not intend to endanger the aircraft. He was dealing with an abnormal condition that required attention. The other crew members were trained professionals with access to important information. They spoke about the fuel, but the communication did not acquire sufficient force.

The cockpit had chairs, roles, procedures, instruments, and a chain of command.

Only one person was captain.

That arrangement existed for good reasons. Aircraft cannot be governed through endless deliberation during an emergency. Someone must hold final authority. Clear responsibility can make coordinated action possible when time is limited.

Yet authority can also pull attention toward itself.

The crew’s work becomes organized around the captain’s interpretation. If the captain frames the landing gear as the central danger, everyone else may begin treating their own information as secondary. A concern becomes something to mention rather than something that must interrupt the plan.

The chair can narrow the room.

The Federal Aviation Administration’s later account of Flight 173 emphasizes that the hierarchical cockpit culture of the period did not reliably support effective crew resource management. The accident became one of the events that helped drive a more collaborative approach in which subordinate crew members were expected to speak more assertively and captains were trained to use the knowledge distributed across the cockpit.[^c4-crm]

This did not eliminate the captain’s authority.

It changed what good authority was expected to do.

The captain still had to decide.

But the role was no longer understood only as the source of judgment. It also became responsible for creating a cockpit in which other people’s judgment could reach the decision.

---

Washington at Newburgh and the captain of Flight 173 might appear to belong to opposite stories.

One leader enters a divided room and prevents a crisis.

Another remains focused on the wrong crisis until an aircraft runs out of fuel.

But the deeper pattern is the same.

Other people are interpreting themselves through the person in authority.

At Newburgh, the officers watched Washington’s response to decide whether their grievance could remain inside the republic they had helped create. His gesture widened their interpretation. It connected their anger to a shared history and made restraint appear honorable rather than submissive.

On Flight 173, the crew organized around the captain’s concern. His focus helped determine which danger occupied the cockpit. The authority needed to coordinate action also made it harder for competing interpretations to displace the one already at the center.

Leadership magnifies ordinary judgment.

Sometimes the judgment is wise.

Sometimes it is mistaken.

Often it contains both strengths and blind spots.

The person need not be a hero or a tyrant for the consequences to become extraordinary.

That is what the chair does.

---

The language of leadership often celebrates decisiveness.

The leader sees what others cannot.

The leader makes the call.

The leader accepts responsibility.

These capacities matter. Groups can become trapped when no one will decide. Shared participation can become a way of distributing hesitation so thoroughly that no one remains accountable for the result.

But decisiveness becomes dangerous when it is confused with completeness.

A leader makes a decision from a partial view. Their role may provide wider access to information, but it also creates distance. People filter what moves upward. Bad news loses detail. Uncertainty becomes a recommendation. Resistance becomes a bullet point. The person at the center can begin believing that the information reaching the chair represents the whole organization.

The chair may offer a broad view of the table.

It cannot see every room.

Good leadership therefore depends partly on how the leader treats the limits of their own position.

Do they understand silence as agreement?

Do they invite dissent only after the decision has effectively been made?

Do they ask questions whose acceptable answers are already obvious?

Do they reward people who clarify the situation, even when clarification complicates the plan?

Do they allow someone with less rank to interrupt?

Do they distinguish being responsible for the decision from being the sole source of the decision?

The democratic question is not whether anyone should occupy the chair.

It is what happens to everyone else’s agency once someone does.

---

A leader can enlarge agency.

They can make information available that had been guarded. They can name uncertainty without turning it into paralysis. They can protect the person whose warning slows the group. They can give people real authority over the parts of the situation they understand best.

In such a room, attention still gathers around the leader, but it does not remain there.

The leader uses visibility to redirect attention toward the work, the evidence, the affected people, and the knowledge distributed across the group. Their authority helps others become more capable of acting.

A leader can also absorb agency.

Every difficult judgment moves upward. People learn to wait. They stop developing interpretations because the central person will provide one. Even highly capable participants begin behaving passively, not because passivity belongs to their character, but because the arrangement has taught them that initiative creates risk while deference creates safety.

The leader then sees the passivity and concludes that stronger direction is necessary.

Dependence becomes evidence for the authority that produced it.

This is how a temporary need for coordination can become a durable theory about people.

They need someone to tell them what to do.

They are not ready for responsibility.

They do not understand the broader situation.

They become emotional when stakes rise.

The person in the chair may believe these observations are neutral. The room may have learned them from the chair.

---

Leadership also exists without formal seating.

A group often knows who its real leader is even when the organizational chart says otherwise.

The person may be the one everyone watches after the manager announces a change. If they nod, the room relaxes. If they remain skeptical, confidence dissolves.

They may possess expertise no title can replace.

They may carry moral credibility earned by taking risks for others.

They may be the person who translates the official message into what everyone believes it actually means.

Informal leadership confirms that attention is part of the phenomenon. People can grant someone interpretive authority without granting them institutional power.

But informal leadership also reveals the limits of attention.

The trusted employee may influence the room and still lack the power to change the policy. The community elder may guide opinion while the government controls resources. The organizer may mobilize people while the employer controls whether they keep their jobs.

Influence and authority overlap without becoming identical.

A complete account of leadership must hold both.

People create leaders through attention, interpretation, trust, and response.

Institutions create leaders through titles, permissions, information, and force.

The person at the center acts through both arrangements.

---

This helps explain why removing a leader can change everything and fail to change enough.

A new person enters the chair.

Their tone differs. They invite questions. They treat mistakes differently. The room begins changing almost immediately because people are watching for signs of what the new leadership will permit.

Yet the policies remain.

The reporting structure remains.

The incentives, metrics, budget, hiring practices, and assumptions about who can be trusted remain. The new leader may want to distribute authority while inheriting systems built to concentrate it.

The chair carries the institution into the person.

The person also carries themselves into the institution.

Over time, their judgments become less visible because they harden into routine. What began as a preference becomes the standard meeting format. What began as anxiety becomes an approval requirement. What began as confidence in one team becomes discretion granted to that team and denied to another.

The leader’s influence outlives the moment of attention.

It enters the environment.

This is where leadership becomes architecture.

---

The empty chair at the head of the table is eventually filled.

The room changes. People listen for the opening sentence. They watch whether the person begins with a decision or a question. They notice who receives eye contact, whose interruption is tolerated, and whether the notebook in front of the leader contains a plan already settled.

The person may imagine they are merely conducting the meeting.

Everyone else is learning what kind of room this will be.

Will information travel upward intact?

Will doubt be punished?

Will responsibility spread?

Will the person in the chair remain necessary for every act of judgment?

These questions cannot be answered by character alone.

A generous leader can inherit a controlling system. A suspicious leader can turn a participatory structure into surveillance. The chair magnifies the person, but the person also begins redesigning the room.

A judgment becomes a rule.

A fear becomes a permission boundary.

A preference becomes a metric.

What began as one person’s interpretation becomes the condition under which many people must act.

The chair becomes a system.

And someone begins taking notes.
