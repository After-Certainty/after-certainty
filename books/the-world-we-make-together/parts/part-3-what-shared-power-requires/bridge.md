# **Part III — What Shared Power Requires**

A boundary can be easy to see and difficult to explain.

A fence runs between two yards. A painted line divides one lane from another. A sign marks where a trail ends and private property begins. The boundary appears to settle the question.

This side belongs here.

That side belongs there.

But boundaries do more than separate.

They organize who may enter, who must ask, who bears responsibility, and whose discomfort becomes relevant. They protect what people value. They can also make people outside the line easier to ignore.

The ballot established equal standing at the moment of decision. It did not erase the differences people carry away from the polling place. Citizens remain separated by geography, history, wealth, religion, race, work, risk, and the stories through which they understand what happened.

Formal equality cannot make those differences harmless.

It cannot ensure that people will recognize one another’s knowledge, remain present through conflict, or care about losses they do not personally experience. It cannot prevent a majority from treating the minority as an obstacle or a powerful minority from using its leverage to override the majority.

Shared power requires something more demanding than equal procedure.

It requires people to meet across boundaries without pretending the boundaries are imaginary.

This does not mean dissolving every difference into agreement. Some boundaries protect safety, identity, privacy, and legitimate authority. Some conflicts cannot be reconciled. Some people act dishonestly or use appeals to dialogue to delay accountability.

But no shared world can be sustained entirely through separation.

People must sometimes trust those who do not resemble them. They must listen to knowledge formed by lives they have not lived. They must negotiate without assuming that the other side’s position reveals the whole of its motive. They must remain capable of caring even when cooperation produces no equal reward.

These capacities are related, but they are not identical.

Trust allows people to risk action together before certainty arrives.

Love makes another person’s flourishing matter beyond the immediate exchange.

Trust asks whether we can place something important in one another’s hands.

Love asks whether what happens to the other remains important when we could walk away.

The next two chapters begin with ordinary objects that hold these questions quietly.

A fence identifies where one space ends and another begins. Yet every fence also contains a theory about the people on the other side.

A bowl passes from one person to another. Its contents can be measured, divided, exchanged, or shared. But the act of offering it can mean more than the value of what it contains.

Shared power depends upon institutions, procedures, and enforceable rights.

It also depends upon relationships capable of carrying what procedure cannot settle.

The ballot has been counted.

The boundary remains.

Someone is standing on the other side.
