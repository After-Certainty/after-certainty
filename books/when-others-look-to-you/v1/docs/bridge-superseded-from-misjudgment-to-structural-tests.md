# **Bridge — From Misjudgment to Structural Tests**

What came before named the problem: we misread leadership when we rely on outcomes,
confidence, and declared intent.

What follows moves from naming the problem to checking what actually happens.

One focus is harm. When influence grows, who absorbs the costs of error? Who is
protected? Who is exposed? **Harm** allocation is not a side effect. It is a core signal
of what leadership is becoming.

Another is **effectiveness** over time. Short-term wins can coexist with
long-term **decay**. Visible success can hide shrinking paths for **Correction**.

A third is **legitimacy**: why people keep following, and what kind of permission
they extend when they do. **Legitimacy** can be example-based, procedural, office-based,
or sacralized, and each form carries different **vitality** risks.

This stretch of the book also tracks transfer: legitimacy does not change only
during one leader’s tenure; it can travel across leadership handoffs. Across
those transitions, the next leader often inherits what last worked—**Leadership Reproduces Itself**.

Throughout, keep one plain tension in view: tasks (what must ship, what gets
measured) versus relationships (who is held in view when costs land). Neither
word is moral by itself. The balance between them shapes whether harm and
**Correction** stay real.

Being looked to does not mean everyone does what they are told. Selective
followership can work as **Correction** in the **vibrant group**, or it can become a
quiet fracture in the **decaying group**.

Across these chapters, track one continuity thread: crisis pressure often
compresses judgment and makes displacement easier to justify. Under scale, that
thread comes back together across the lenses.
