# Expansion Plan: Chapter 4 — Circulation
## When Others Look to You — Developing the Book's Most Original Contribution

---

## The Governing Problem

Circulation is the book's most original contribution and currently its thinnest chapter. Chapters on Renewal and Erosion each get a full treatment with multiple dynamics, vignettes, and connections back to the framework. Circulation gets one chapter that has to do several things at once: define what circulation is, distinguish it from delegation and succession, introduce guest leadership, contrast how it works in vibrant versus decaying groups, and close with the practical check.

The result is that the concept lands intellectually but doesn't fully open up the way Renewal and Erosion do. The expansion doesn't require new ideas. Everything needed is already implied in the existing chapter. The work is excavation and development, not invention.

---

## What Is Currently Underdeveloped

**The permission structure idea** — the most original thing in the chapter — moves past too quickly. What does a permission structure look like when it's open? What are the subtle signals that tell someone whether stepping in is safe before they try it?

**The cost of not circulating** — the chapter doesn't fully develop what a group loses structurally when circulation is captured over time. What atrophies? How does its absence become invisible until pressure reveals it?

**The relationship between circulation and legitimacy** — this connection gets picked up in Chapter 7 but belongs at least partially in Chapter 4. When someone steps in temporarily and is credited, something happens to what the group believes about who is allowed to lead.

**The guest leadership concept** — cited but moves through quickly. Given the direct citation of Cockburn's work, this deserves more development that situates it firmly inside the larger framework.

**The vignette load** — "Who Gets Credit" is the best vignette in the book but it's carrying almost all the experiential weight for the entire chapter. An earlier, smaller vignette is needed to make the permission question concrete before the conceptual sections develop it.

### 1. What Circulation Is — and What It Isn't
*Keep with minor revision*

The opening distinction between circulation and delegation/succession/rotation is strong and should stay.

**One addition:** After establishing what circulation isn't, name permission explicitly as the thing those other mechanisms miss. Currently permission is introduced a paragraph later almost in passing. It deserves to be named at the top as the chapter's central concern.

---

### 2. The Permission Question
*New section*

This is the chapter's most original idea and it currently has no dedicated space.

**Key ideas to develop:**

Permission is rarely stated explicitly. Nobody announces that stepping in is dangerous. The signals are behavioral and accumulate over time — who gets credited when they step in, whether the person who held the center temporarily is welcomed back into the decision ring, whether the step-in is described afterward as useful or as a problem that had to be managed.

Permission is learned before it's tested. People read the structure before they act inside it. They watch what happened to the last person who stepped in. That watching shapes what they attempt — or don't attempt — before they ever face a moment that would call for it.

Permission can be open on paper and closed in practice. A formal deputizing structure, a clear chain of command, an explicit invitation to lead in someone's absence — none of these guarantee that the permission is real. The test is what happens after, not what was said before.

**Draft language:**

> Permission is not usually announced. It accumulates in what the group does after someone steps in — whether they are credited, whether they are welcome in the same conversations as before, whether the step-in is described as useful or as something that had to be managed. People learn to read that accumulation before they test it. They watch what happened to the last person who held the center for a stretch, and they calibrate accordingly. By the time a moment arrives that calls for someone to step in, the permission question has usually already been answered — not by any formal declaration, but by what the group has been teaching about the cost of leading without being asked.

### 3. Reading the Room Before You Step In
*New vignette*

Position this vignette immediately after the Permission Question section. It should be brief — roughly half the length of "Who Gets Credit" — and should show the moment before stepping in, not the aftermath. Someone reading the permission structure before they decide whether to act.

**Draft vignette:**

> **The Hesitation**
>
> The project lead is out sick the morning the client calls with a scope question that cannot wait. One person on the team knows the answer — has been closest to that part of the work for two months, has heard every relevant conversation. She also knows what happened six weeks ago when someone else jumped in on a similar call without being asked. That person was thanked afterward in a way that felt like a warning. She drafts a response, reads it twice, and sends it to the project lead's personal phone instead. The client waits three hours for an answer that was already written.
>
> Nobody made a rule about this. The rule was already there.

**Note:** This vignette makes the permission question felt before the prose explains it. That's the interpretive register doing its job.

### 5. What Atrophies Without Circulation
*New section*

This is the section the chapter most needs and currently doesn't have. Renewal and Erosion both develop what changes structurally in a group over time. Circulation needs the same treatment on the negative side.

**Key ideas to develop:**

**Judgment atrophies at the edges.** When only certain people are permitted to lead, everyone else's capacity for consequential judgment goes unpracticed. This isn't about skill — people may be highly capable — it's about the habit of acting on judgment under conditions that matter. That habit requires practice inside real situations. Watching someone else lead doesn't build it.

**The group loses its early warning capacity.** Circulation is partly how groups develop people who can recognize trouble from inside the work. When only one path for leading exists, the people closest to shifting conditions — who would be the first to notice something going wrong — never develop the authority to act on what they see. The warning exists but has no route.

**Recovery becomes harder under pressure.** A group that has never circulated leadership has no practiced bench. When pressure arrives and the center needs support, or when the center fails, there is no one who has held the role before and knows what it requires. The group discovers this at exactly the moment when the cost of discovering it is highest.

**What gets inherited changes.** A group that hasn't circulated doesn't just produce a thin bench — it produces a next generation of leaders who have learned that authority is scarce, that stepping in uninvited is dangerous, and that waiting for permission is how you survive. That lesson travels forward through Leadership Reproduces Itself.

**Draft language:**

> Capture doesn't look like failure from inside the group. The work still moves. Decisions still get made. The center still holds. What disappears is harder to see because it was never fully visible — the judgment people on the edges were developing, the early warnings that had a route before they lost it, the practiced capacity to hold the center for a stretch when the work demanded it. None of that announces its absence. It shows up only when pressure arrives and the group reaches for what isn't there.

### 6. Circulation and Legitimacy
*New section*

This connection is currently split across Chapter 4 and Chapter 7 in a way that makes both chapters thinner than they need to be. This section doesn't need to develop the full legitimacy framework — that belongs in Chapter 7 — but it should establish that circulation has a legitimacy dimension, not just an effectiveness one.

**Key observation:** When someone steps in temporarily and is visibly credited, something changes in what the group believes about who is allowed to lead. That belief is a form of legitimacy — informal, distributed, earned through witnessed action rather than assigned through title.

**The inverse:** When stepping in consistently goes uncredited or is treated as overreach, the group learns that legitimacy belongs only to certain people, that it doesn't transfer through action, that authority is positional rather than earned in the moment.

**Draft language:**

> There is a legitimacy question inside circulation that sits beneath the effectiveness one. When someone steps in and is credited — visibly, by name, in the room where it happened — the group learns something about who is allowed to lead. Not from a policy, but from what they witnessed. That witnessing is how informal permission spreads: not through announcement but through accumulated example. The group that circulates leadership is also, quietly, expanding its own sense of who counts as a leader. The group that captures it is narrowing that sense with each uncredited step-in, each person thanked in a way that felt like a warning, each moment where the room closed around someone who held the center and then stepped back.

### 7. Circulation in the Vibrant Group
*Keep with moderate revision*

The existing content is good.

**Two additions:**

First, connect explicitly to the Permission Question section — in the vibrant group, permission is open not because it was declared but because the examples that accumulated made it readable as open.

Second, the closing observation — "Renewal at the center and circulation through the bench reinforce each other" — arrives too quickly. Give it one more sentence of development before moving on.

### 8. Circulation in the Decaying Group
*Keep with moderate revision*

This is one of the stronger passages in the chapter. The observation that "the people invited to 'step up' are the ones who have shown they will not disturb the pattern" is exactly right.

**One addition:** Connect explicitly to the Atrophy section. In the decaying group, what travels through captured circulation isn't just a template for how leadership is performed — it's a practiced incapacity. People don't just learn the wrong lesson; they don't practice the right one.

**Keep:** The closing passage about coordination outside the official chain being treated as bypass. It's strong as-is.

### 9. Who Gets Credit
*Keep vignette as-is*

This is the best vignette in the book. Do not revise it. The two contrasting scenes do exactly what a vignette should do. The closing line — "Same competence can read as threat or as gift, depending on whether circulation is open or captured" — is one of the best lines in the manuscript.

### 10. Leadership Reproduces Itself
*Keep with minor revision*

**One addition:** After "Circulation teaches people whether they are allowed to lead before they ever hold the role" — add a sentence connecting to the Atrophy section. What gets practiced or not practiced during that pre-role period shapes not just permission but capacity. The lesson isn't only about whether stepping in is safe. It's about whether the muscle gets built at all.

---

### 11. Closing Passage
*Revise toward interpretive register*

This is a Tier 1 revision from the register plan. Convert bullets to prose, shift from "ask" to "watch," end with an image that opens outward rather than a verdict that closes down.

**The closing line — "Circulation copies permission before it ever copies a title" — is excellent.** Build toward it. It should be the chapter's absolute final note, not something that appears mid-section before a checklist.
