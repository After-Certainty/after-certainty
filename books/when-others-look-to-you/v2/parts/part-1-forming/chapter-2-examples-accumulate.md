# **Chapter 2 — Examples Accumulate**

**Attention Finds a Focus.** The room has oriented. People are
watching, even if no one says so directly. What happens next is small. A
response. A reaction. A choice made in a few seconds. It does not feel
like a defining moment. It rarely does.

But it is.

When **Attention Finds a Focus**, behavior is no longer private.
Before that moment, actions are just actions. They belong to the person
taking them. They can be interpreted in different ways, or not at all.

After that moment, actions are signals. They are seen, interpreted, and
repeated.

A deadline is missed. Someone explains why. The explanation is partial.
It leaves things out. It softens what actually happened. You respond
quickly. “That’s fine—let’s just move forward.” The conversation
continues. The meeting stays on track. Nothing escalates.

It feels like a small decision. A practical one. But something else has
happened.

The group has learned what happens when something goes wrong. They have
learned that incomplete explanations are acceptable. They have learned
that accuracy can be traded for speed. They have learned that moving
forward matters more than understanding.

No one writes this down. No one discusses it. But it is understood.

**Examples Accumulate.**

The rule is not formed through instruction. It is formed through
observation.[^c2-norm-focus]

People are constantly asking, without saying it out loud:

- What is safe here?

- What matters here?

- What happens if something goes wrong?

They do not wait for policies to answer these questions. They watch how
you respond.

A sharp correction signals that mistakes matter. A calm question signals
that understanding matters. A quick dismissal signals that speed matters
more than either.

None of this is announced. All of it is learned.

This is why behavior moves faster than policy. Policy can be written. It
can be discussed. It can be enforced.

But behavior is immediate. A reaction in a moment carries more weight
than a rule written later. And when they conflict, behavior wins.

The effect compounds quickly. One moment becomes a pattern. The pattern
becomes an expectation. The expectation becomes a boundary.

Soon, people stop testing the edges. They stay within what has already
been shown. They bring the kind of information that has been received
well before. They avoid the kind that has not. They match the pace that
has been set.

Not because they were told to. Because they learned.

The person being looked to does not need to intend this. In many cases,
they don’t.

They are trying to keep things moving. Avoid unnecessary friction.
Maintain focus.

Each of these makes sense in the moment. But the group is not
interpreting the intent. It is interpreting the example.

And the example does not stay isolated. It repeats. There is no neutral
response. Silence signals something. Speed signals something. Curiosity
signals something. Certainty signals something.

Even the absence of reaction is interpreted. People are not waiting for
clarity. They are constructing it.

This is how leadership begins to take shape. Not through decisions
alone. Through patterns of response.

The group is learning how to behave, not from what is said, but from
what is shown.

And once learned, these habits are difficult to undo. Because they are
no longer external. They are internal.

The next time something goes wrong, the group does not wait to see what
will happen. They already have a sense. They have seen it before.

> Under pressure, people learn from what they watch—not from what was announced.

From here, something else begins to happen. The same person is looked to
again. The same responses are reinforced again.

And over time, the moment stabilizes. Not formally. Not intentionally.
But reliably.

**Leadership Coalesces.**

[^c2-norm-focus]: Robert B. Cialdini, Raymond R. Reno, and Carl A. Kallgren,
  "A Focus Theory of Normative Conduct," *Journal of Personality and Social
  Psychology* 58, no. 6 (1990): 1015–1026.
