# **Trust Protects Authority**

> *Why does authority often outlive performance?*

By the time Walter's concerns became difficult to ignore, questioning David Marsh had begun to feel uncomfortable inside Calder.

Not because David demanded loyalty, prohibited criticism, or punished dissent. Something subtler was happening. David's history was beginning to function as evidence—not merely as a record of past performance, but as a reason current questions felt disrespectful.

When Walter raised concerns about processes or decisions associated with David's leadership, many people heard criticism of the person who had built the organization, of the mission itself, of a story they valued. The conversation quietly shifted. People no longer asked whether the evidence supported David's current judgment. They asked how David could possibly be wrong—given everything he had built.

Renata often responded this way. Not because she believed David incapable of mistakes, but because she worried about what would be lost if people became too willing to question the organization's foundations. Walter worried that avoiding criticism already had.

Authority often begins as a response to demonstrated competence—a reasonable process. The challenge emerges later. Authority does not disappear when performance changes. The history that established legitimacy can make legitimacy difficult to revise—and over time, legitimacy itself can function as evidence, substituting for evaluation of current performance.

Sociologist Max Weber described legitimacy as one foundation of authority.[^c7-weber-legitimacy] People obey not merely because power exists, but because they believe the authority is justified. Legitimacy accumulates faster than it dissolves. Trust grows through years of successful performance; questions may emerge much later. The authority remains. The conditions change.

Organizations such as WeWork showed how quickly confidence in leadership can expand during success, and how difficult it becomes to separate trust in a mission from trust in the people associated with it.[^c7-wiedeman-wework] Long-tenured leaders in many institutions experience the same shift: past success becomes the answer to present questions.

Authority solves real coordination problems. The difficulty is that trust can gradually transform it into something beyond ordinary evaluation—when questions require justification, criticism requires courage, and correction requires permission.

At Calder, David remained widely respected, and much of that respect remained deserved. Yet Walter noticed that David's past had become the answer to questions about Calder's present. When concerns emerged, people discussed the trust David had earned rather than the evidence currently available.

Legitimacy was substituting for evaluation.

That is often the moment when authority begins outliving the conditions that originally justified it.

### Core Principle

**Legitimacy Can Outlive Performance**

Past legitimacy can function as evidence—making current performance harder to evaluate fairly.

[^c7-weber-legitimacy]: Weber, *Economy and Society: An Outline of Interpretive Sociology*, ed. Guenther Roth and Claus Wittich (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1978). Weber analyzed how legitimacy—traditional, charismatic, and legal-rational—grounds authority and can persist after the conditions that first justified it have changed.

[^c7-wiedeman-wework]: Wiedeman, *The Cult of We: WeWork, Adam Neumann, and the Great Startup Delusion* (New York: Little, Brown, 2021). Wiedeman reported how investor and employee confidence in WeWork expanded rapidly during its growth phase, making later scrutiny of leadership difficult to separate from trust in the company's mission.
