# **Why Trust Needs Revision**

> *How can trust remain connected to reality?*

The debate at Calder eventually centered on a surprisingly specific issue. For decades, David Marsh had personally approved every new clinic director—a practice that began when the organization was small, when David knew every employee and every clinic, and when the approval process helped preserve a consistent standard of care as Calder grew. Many people viewed it as one of the reasons the organization had succeeded. Renata certainly did. To her, the process represented care, stewardship, and the belief that leadership positions should be entrusted carefully rather than assigned casually.

Walter saw something different. What had once been a safeguard was becoming a bottleneck. The organization had grown far beyond the conditions that originally justified the process. Approvals took longer. Decisions became delayed. Regional leaders increasingly lacked authority to respond to local needs. The practice that once helped the organization adapt was beginning to slow its ability to do so.

Neither perspective felt unreasonable. Renata was not defending the process because she opposed improvement. Walter was not challenging it because he rejected Calder's history. Both believed they were protecting something valuable. The disagreement centered on what, exactly, that valuable thing was.

As the discussion continued, Olivia found herself paying less attention to the argument itself and more attention to what seemed to motivate it. Walter was protecting correction. Renata was protecting continuity. Olivia had avoided taking sides—not from neutrality alone, but from fear that choosing Walter's path would make her disloyal and choosing Renata's would make her complicit in drift.

For months, the conversation remained unresolved. Eventually, the organization chose a different approach. Rather than deciding which side was correct, Calder created a pilot program. Several regions were allowed to approve clinic directors locally. The traditional approval process remained in place elsewhere. The organization agreed to monitor outcomes—hiring quality, patient outcomes, staff retention, leadership performance.

The goal was not to prove one side right. The goal was to learn.

The results were not dramatic. Regional clinics maintained comparable standards. Patient outcomes remained stable. Several regions filled leadership positions more quickly and responded more effectively to local challenges. Not every concern disappeared. Not every question was settled. Yet something important had happened: the organization had returned to feedback. Instead of debating the process in the abstract, people had created an opportunity to learn from reality.

The experience changed Olivia's understanding of the conflict. Walter and Renata were protecting different parts of the same mission. Neither approach seemed sufficient by itself. An organization that preserves everything eventually loses the ability to adapt. An organization that revises everything eventually loses the ability to endure. Healthy stewardship requires both.

The question was never whether Calder should preserve its traditions or revise them. The question was how it could learn which traditions served the mission and which merely reflected the circumstances in which the mission had first emerged.

That distinction is more difficult than it appears. Many organizations have abandoned valuable practices while claiming to preserve their purpose. Many have preserved obsolete practices while claiming to protect their legacy. No leader, institution, or community can determine that distinction alone. It cannot be settled by authority, loyalty, or criticism alone—only through continued participation, feedback, and learning.[^c9-dewey-experience-education]

This principle appears in many of the systems that remain resilient over time. Aviation safety improves because incidents, anomalies, and near misses become opportunities for investigation rather than reasons for denial.[^c9-reason-safety] Science advances because claims remain subject to revision when new evidence emerges. Peer review, replication, and criticism exist not because trust is absent, but because trust remains accountable to reality.[^c9-merton-science]

Elinor Ostrom studied how communities and institutions sustain cooperation over time.[^c9-ostrom-commons] Her work showed that durable systems depend on feedback mechanisms—rules that can be revised when outcomes reveal their limits. Open-source communities often improve through a similar process: ideas compete, revisions occur, and outcomes remain visible enough for learning to continue.[^c9-raymond-open-source]

These systems do not survive because they eliminate trust. They survive because trust remains correctable—answerable to the people who depend on it and to the outcomes it produces.[^c9-oneill-trustworthiness]

At Calder, the pilot program did not solve every problem. No process ever does. What it demonstrated was something more important: trust remained capable of learning. The organization did not preserve its history by freezing it. It preserved its history by allowing it to remain responsive to reality.

That is the difference between trust and drift. Drift protects trust from feedback. Healthy trust remains willing to learn from it.

### Core Principle

**Healthy Trust Remains Correctable**

Healthy trust remains capable of learning which practices must endure and which must evolve.

[^c9-dewey-experience-education]: Dewey, *Experience and Education* (New York: Macmillan, 1938). Dewey argued that learning requires feedback, reflection, and the willingness to revise practice when outcomes demand it.

[^c9-reason-safety]: Reason, *Managing the Risks of Organizational Accidents* (Aldershot, UK: Ashgate, 1997). Reason analyzed how aviation and other high-hazard systems treat incidents and near misses as sources of learning rather than threats to institutional trust.

[^c9-merton-science]: Merton, "The Normative Structure of Science," in *The Sociology of Science*, ed. Norman W. Storer (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1973), 267–278. First published 1942. Merton described peer review, replication, and organized skepticism as mechanisms that keep scientific trust accountable to evidence.

[^c9-ostrom-commons]: Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1990). Ostrom showed how adaptive institutions depend on local feedback and revisable rules rather than fixed authority alone.

[^c9-raymond-open-source]: Raymond, *The Cathedral and the Bazaar* (Sebastopol, CA: O'Reilly Media, 1999). Raymond described how open-source communities sustain improvement through visible revision, public critique, and competing implementations.

[^c9-oneill-trustworthiness]: O'Neill, *A Question of Trust* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2002). O'Neill argued that trustworthiness requires accountability and answerability—not blind faith, but trust that remains open to scrutiny.
