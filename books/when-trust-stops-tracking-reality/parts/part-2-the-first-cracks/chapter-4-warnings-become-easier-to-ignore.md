# **Warnings Become Easier to Ignore**

> *Why do warning signs become harder to see?*

Real warning signs almost never announce themselves. They arrive as something easier to dismiss—a handful of patient complaints, a process that misfires once, clinics handling the same situation differently despite shared procedures. Only later does anyone recognize what those fragments meant.

When Walter Pruitt, who had joined Calder during its period of rapid expansion, began noticing such concerns, the organization was still succeeding—outcomes mostly positive, the mission intact, trust largely deserved. He remembered when problems were easier to see because fewer layers existed between decisions and outcomes. That backdrop made weak signals hard to see. None of the issues seemed significant on its own. Walter did not treat them as proof Calder was failing. He treated them as signals worth investigating.

Most colleagues heard something milder. Complaints seemed isolated. Failures looked temporary. Outcomes remained overwhelmingly positive. Against years of successful care and a mission people believed in, each fragment felt small. The existing story seemed more convincing than the new evidence.

Fragments rarely ask people to overturn trust. They ask to be filed away as exceptions—and when trust has been earned, filing them away often feels like patience rather than denial. Patience and dismissal can look remarkably similar while warning signs are still weak. The difference usually appears only later.

The pattern appears in intimate relationships too. A spouse may notice recurring friction but file it away as a bad week. A long friendship may show small shifts in availability that feel too minor to name against years of loyalty. The signals are present. They simply do not look like warnings yet.

Before the Challenger disaster, engineers raised concerns about O-ring performance under unusually cold conditions.[^c4-vaughan-challenger] The concerns were real, but they looked like technical caveats against successful launches—not alarms demanding a halt.

Before the Boeing 737 MAX accidents, concerns emerged about design decisions, pilot assumptions, and safety processes.[^c4-grady-boeing-max] Each competed with decades of engineering credibility rather than with an obvious crisis.

Before Theranos collapsed, journalists, employees, and experts raised questions that struggled against a narrative of innovation and success.[^c4-carreyrou-bad-blood] The questions existed early. They simply did not look like the beginning of collapse.

At Calder, nothing yet demanded a different conclusion—but fragments had appeared, and the organization was treating them as exceptions rather than signals.

Trust had once helped people learn from success. Now it was helping them explain away the first signs that success might no longer tell the whole story.

### Core Principle

**Trust Can Silence Weak Signals**

Weak signals rarely look like warnings—and trust often absorbs them before stronger evidence arrives.

[^c4-vaughan-challenger]: Vaughan, *The Challenger Launch Decision: Risky Technology, Culture, and Deviance at NASA* (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1996). Vaughan documented how organizations normalize deviance—gradually accepting small anomalies until warning signs no longer register as warnings.

[^c4-grady-boeing-max]: Grady, *Flying Blind* (2022). Grady showed how early warnings about the 737 MAX competed against Boeing's accumulated reputation for engineering excellence.

[^c4-carreyrou-bad-blood]: Carreyrou, *Bad Blood: Secrets and Lies in a Silicon Valley Startup* (New York: Knopf, 2018). Carreyrou reported how Theranos employees and journalists raised concerns that struggled to compete with the company's narrative of innovation.
