# When Trust Stops Tracking Reality — Bibliography Guide

Working notes for source selection during drafting. Export bibliography lives in [`../back-matter/bibliography.md`](../back-matter/bibliography.md).

---

## Scope

This book is not a survey of trust literature. It is about:

> how trust drifts away from feedback,
> how institutions stop learning,
> and how correction becomes difficult.

Every source should directly support that argument. Do not force citations into chapters where the Calder story and real anchors carry the prose alone.

---

## Citation discipline

Write observation-driven prose first; use citations to anchor key claims and intellectual lineage—not to drive the argument.

**Target density:** About 8–12 intellectual-spine sources appearing repeatedly across the manuscript, plus real-world anchor citations where named examples appear (Apple, Boeing, Theranos, WeWork, etc.). Optional sources appear once where they naturally fit.

**Footnote timing:** Add Pandoc footnotes (`[^c4-vaughan-challenger]`) at structural pivots during the citation pass, not while drafting anchor stories.

**Series overlap:** Luhmann, Dewey, Scott, Weber, Ostrom, O'Neill, Fricker, and Vaughan appear elsewhere in the portfolio. Reuse citation formats from sibling books where possible.

---

## Intellectual spine (six authors)

If the bibliography must feel maximally focused, these six authors map almost perfectly onto the book's structure:

| Chapter theme | Primary source |
|---------------|----------------|
| Trust forms | Luhmann — *Trust and Power* |
| Warning signals | Vaughan — *The Challenger Launch Decision* |
| Learning stops | Dewey — *Experience and Education* |
| Scale and abstraction | Scott — *Seeing Like a State* |
| Legitimacy persists | Weber — *Economy and Society* |
| Trust recovers | Ostrom — *Governing the Commons* |

Weick, Fricker, and O'Neill extend the spine where interpretation, credibility, and trustworthiness require explicit grounding. They are core to the export bibliography but secondary to the six above.

## Core sources by unit

### Introduction — The Company Everyone Wanted to Work For

**Primary**

- **Luhmann — *Trust and Power*** — Trust reduces complexity; action without constant verification; why trust forms.
- **O'Neill — *A Question of Trust*** — Trustworthiness and scrutiny; healthy trust is not blind faith.

---

### Chapter 1 — Trust Creates Shortcuts

**Pattern:** Trust Reduces Verification

**Primary**

- **Luhmann — *Trust and Power*** — Trust as complexity reduction; verification is costly; cooperation under uncertainty.

---

### Chapter 2 — Success Creates Momentum

**Pattern:** Past Success Creates Future Trust

**Primary**

- **Isaacson — *Steve Jobs*** — Apple; reputation built on successive product success.
- **Lowenstein — *Buffett*** — Investor trust extending from decades of performance.
- **Cole — "What Really Happened to Toyota?"** — Generational reliability reputation.
- **Grady — *Flying Blind*** — Boeing engineering credibility before the 737 MAX crisis.

---

### Chapter 3 — Trust Becomes Identity

**Pattern:** Trust Can Become Part of Who We Are

**Primary**

- **Freeh, Sporkin, & Sullivan — Penn State investigative report (2012)** — Institutional identity; criticism experienced as attack on community.
- Prose anchors: sports fandom, religious belonging, family identity (Calder carries the narrative).

---

### Chapter 4 — Warnings Become Easier to Ignore

**Pattern:** Trust Can Silence Weak Signals

**Primary**

- **Vaughan — *The Challenger Launch Decision*** — Probably the single most important source in the book. Normalization of deviance; warning signs absorbed before strong signals register.
- **Grady — *Flying Blind*** — Boeing 737 MAX; warnings competing against engineering reputation.
- **Carreyrou — *Bad Blood*** — Theranos; early questions struggling against an innovation narrative.

---

### Chapter 5 — Participation Stops Updating Beliefs

**Pattern:** Trust Can Interrupt Learning

**Primary**

- **Dewey — *Experience and Education*** — Learning requires feedback; participation updates beliefs; what breaks when experience becomes confirmation.
- **Raine — "Reinventing the self" (IJCAM 2021)** — NXIVM-specific; participation within reinventive/self-help institutions.
- **Keep & Vander Nat — MLM historical analysis** — Multi-level marketing; setbacks interpreted as insufficient commitment.

**Optional**

- **Janis — *Groupthink*** — Could strengthen group-level confirmation dynamics.
- **Berman — *Don't Call It a Cult*** — Investigative account; how NXIVM looked from inside versus outside.
- **Lalich — *Bounded Choice*** — Cross-cult mechanism for participation reinforcing commitment (not NXIVM-specific).

---

### Chapter 6 — Trust Scales Beyond Evidence

**Pattern:** Abstraction Can Hide Reality

**Primary**

- **Scott — *Seeing Like a State*** — Large systems become abstract; distance weakens feedback; institutions lose contact with local reality.

---

### Chapter 7 — Trust Protects Authority

**Pattern:** Legitimacy Can Outlive Performance

**Primary**

- **Weber — *Economy and Society*** — Authority, legitimacy, and why people continue trusting leaders after conditions change.
- **Wiedeman — *The Cult of We*** — WeWork; confidence in leadership expanding faster than scrutiny.

**Optional**

- **Jackall — *Moral Mazes*** — Could strengthen how authority protects itself inside organizations.

---

### Chapter 8 — Trust Shapes Interpretation

**Pattern:** Trust Influences What We Notice

**Primary**

- **Weick — *Sensemaking in Organizations*** — Organizations interpret evidence through existing narratives; people notice what fits the story they already believe.
- **Fricker — *Epistemic Injustice*** — Who gets believed; how credibility is distributed; why some warning signals struggle to gain attention.
- **Druckman et al. — misinformation and health framework** — COVID-era divergent interpretation of shared events.
- **Douglas et al. — "Understanding Conspiracy Theories"** — Conspiracy communities learning different evidence priorities over time.

---

### Chapter 9 — Why Trust Needs Revision

**Pattern:** Healthy Trust Remains Correctable

**Primary**

- **Dewey — *Experience and Education*** — Feedback, learning, and revisability.
- **Ostrom — *Governing the Commons*** — Adaptive institutions; self-correction; feedback mechanisms that prevent drift.
- **O'Neill — *A Question of Trust*** — Accountability; why healthy trust requires scrutiny rather than blind faith.
- **Reason — *Managing the Risks of Organizational Accidents*** — Aviation safety; incidents and near misses as learning.
- **Merton — "The Normative Structure of Science"** — Peer review, replication, organized skepticism.
- **Raymond — *The Cathedral and the Bazaar*** — Open-source revision through visible critique and competition.

---

### Conclusion — The Founder and the Mission

**Primary**

- **Ostrom — *Governing the Commons*** — Institutions that learn; stewardship over mechanism.

**Optional**

- **Arendt — *Between Past and Future*** — Could strengthen the preservation/correction tension (what must endure vs. what must evolve).
