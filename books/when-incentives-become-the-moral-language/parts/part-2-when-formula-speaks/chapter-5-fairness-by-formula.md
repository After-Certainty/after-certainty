# Chapter 5

## Fairness by Formula

Central question: when does fairness become something you compute rather than something you own?

### Before Formula, Fairness Was Argued

Layoffs, promotions, discipline, and compensation decisions have always been imperfect. Bias, politics, and favoritism were never absent. But in smaller organizations or slower governance environments, decisions were often experienced as authored. Someone made the call, explained it, and could be challenged on why they thought it was fair.

That form of fairness was messy and often inconsistent. It was still moral in a specific sense: it required visible judgment about what was owed in a case, by someone who could be held accountable for the reasoning.

Modern firms still speak this language in values statements: respect, integrity, opportunity, dignity. The language implies that people, not only systems, own the tradeoffs.

As organizations scaled across regions, legal regimes, and workforce categories, authored fairness became harder to defend in public institutional terms.

### Why Discretion Became a Liability

Discretion did not become suspect because managers stopped caring. It became suspect because variability under scrutiny looks like discrimination, favoritism, or arbitrariness.

A single workforce decision can now trigger internal escalation, legal review, social media amplification, investor questions, and regulator attention. Two managers making slightly different calls in similar cases may reflect context-sensitive judgment. In legal and reputational terms, the same difference can look like unequal treatment.

Under those conditions, organizations learn that "human judgment" is a weak defense. "Documented process" is a stronger one.

The shift is especially sharp in high-pressure restructuring. When leaders must reduce costs quickly, discretionary case-by-case reasoning is difficult to scale across large populations with credible consistency. Even managers who know local context are often told to implement centrally defined criteria.

So fairness is translated from judgment to method.

### How Workforce Fairness Became Procedural

Three mechanisms carry most of that translation.

First, performance scoring systems. Ratings, distributions, and calibration sessions convert heterogeneous work into comparable categories.[^c5-ref-1-peter-cappelli-and-anna] These systems are often presented as anti-bias infrastructure, and they can reduce some forms of arbitrary decision-making. They also create the appearance that the score is the person.

Second, redundancy and restructuring matrices. Large layoffs are commonly executed through criteria sets that combine role criticality, cost center constraints, geography, and performance bands.[^c5-ref-2-us-worker-adjustment-and] The matrix is operationally useful because it is reproducible. It can be shown to counsel, boards, and regulators. It can be defended as consistent.

Third, compliance layers and scripted process. Notice periods, severance formulas, legal language review, and standardized communication kits ensure that decisions survive procedural challenge. In this frame, fairness is demonstrated by procedural adherence: timelines met, templates followed, documentation complete.

None of these mechanisms claims that harm is eliminated. They claim harm is administered according to an auditable standard.

That distinction matters. A "fair" layoff in procedural terms may still feel morally unowned to everyone involved.

### What Formula Solves and What It Cannot

Formula solves a real coordination problem. It lets global firms run consequential decisions at scale without collapsing under inconsistency claims. It enables comparability and legal defensibility where discretionary narratives would be impossible to arbitrate.

Formula cannot answer every fairness question that people actually ask.

It can tell you whether two people were evaluated under the same framework. It cannot tell you whether the framework itself captured the value of their contribution. It can tell you whether severance followed policy. It cannot tell you whether policy reflected proportional burden-sharing across leadership and staff. It can show that criteria were applied consistently. It cannot show that the criteria were morally adequate.

This is where fairness-by-formula becomes a substitute language: it narrows fairness to what the system can count and defend.

### Moral Residue: Harm Without Ownership

Managers and HR practitioners feel this narrowing directly. They often know details the model cannot represent: caregiving burdens, role complexity that was never scored well, team-level fragility, context that made performance dip temporarily but recoverable. When matrix outputs conflict with that judgment, they are expected to execute the output, not defend the exception.

Employees feel the same gap from the other side. A rating category becomes destiny. A matrix row becomes a terminated role. A person asks why this happened and receives a process explanation that may be accurate but does not feel like an account of what was owed.

Organizational justice research has long shown that procedural fairness matters for legitimacy. It has also shown that people evaluate fairness through interactional and informational dimensions: whether they were treated with dignity and whether explanations felt sincere and specific.[^c5-ref-3-jerald-greenberg-organizational-justice] Process helps; process alone is not enough.

When institutions cannot provide authored explanations without increasing exposure, they default to procedural language. Trust can erode even as compliance improves.

### Fairness and Defensibility Diverge

Companies rarely say, "we outsourced moral responsibility to a model." They say, "we followed a rigorous and equitable process." That statement usually tells the truth about governance posture. It does not settle the moral question employees are asking.

At system scale, this language is stabilizing. It reduces legal risk, enables execution speed, and allows boards to claim consistency across large populations.

At human scale, costs remain visible:

- people reduced to abstractions in score distributions;
- managers implementing decisions they would not author independently;
- teams reading neutrality as distance rather than justice.

Fairness and formula are not identical. Fairness asks what is owed in this case. Formula asks whether this case fits the same rule as adjacent cases.

When formula becomes the dominant moral language of workforce decisions, care for people does not disappear. It moves inward, into private discomfort and unofficial conversations that the institution cannot absorb without weakening the very defense it depends on.

Work continues. Process improves. Fairness becomes increasingly procedural.
