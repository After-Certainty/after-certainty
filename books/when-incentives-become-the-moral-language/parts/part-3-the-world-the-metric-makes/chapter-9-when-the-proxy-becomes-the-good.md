# Chapter 9

## When the Proxy Becomes the Good

By the end of a single ordinary week, a person moving through contemporary institutions can touch eight different surfaces without leaving the routines of work, care, and citizenship.

A whiteboard in a hospital corridor still shows an expected discharge date beside a name. A phone feed refreshes with items chosen from a record of what held attention yesterday. A journal shelf in a university office sorts venues by the influence metrics hiring committees recognize. A poster in a company lobby displays a net-zero curve toward a date the market can repeat. A spreadsheet on a manager's laptop lists employees beside ratings and selection status. A newsroom homepage moves a municipal investigation down as a faster story climbs. A campaign dashboard updates overnight tracking on a housing message test. A report-card screen waits for comments that must align with grades the state can aggregate.

None of these objects is exotic. Each solved a coordination problem someone actually had. Each made a moral aim legible enough to travel: care, connection, truth, responsibility, fairness, public consequence, representation, formation. Each also began, somewhere in its history, as a translation rather than a destination.

The pattern has a sequence. It appears in every domain already visited here, though no domain announces it.

### The Week the Institution Forgot

A value is difficult to coordinate. Healing must move across payers and shifts. Connection must scale across billions of users. Truth must be judged across fields no committee can master. Responsibility for harm must be carried across supply chains and decades. Fairness must be administered across workforces too large for authored decisions. Public consequence must be weighed against economic survival. Representation must speak for people who are not loud in today's data. Formation must be nurtured across institutions that answer to different anxieties.

A proxy makes the value legible. Criteria replace readiness. Engagement replaces editorial judgment. Publication replaces contribution. Targets replace causal ownership. Procedure replaces authored fairness. Attention replaces importance. Polling replaces deliberation. Scores replace formation.

Rewards and consequences attach to the proxy. Reimbursement follows length of stay. Distribution follows attention. Careers follow citation counts. Capital follows disclosure quality. Retention follows ratings. Placement follows traffic. Viability follows message tests. School reputations follow proficiency bands.

People reorganize behavior around the proxy. Clinicians speak in criteria. Creators speak in hooks. Researchers speak in throughput. Sustainability teams speak in trajectories. Managers speak in matrices. Editors speak in performance. Campaigns speak in toplines. Teachers speak in data.

The institution forgets the proxy was a translation. The sentence everyone can repeat begins to sound like the value itself. *Patient met discharge criteria.* *Users want this.* *She has an excellent record.* *We are on track.* *We followed a rigorous process.* *The story performed.* *We listened to the people.* *Scores improved.*

What the proxy cannot express loses public standing. Doubt without a field. Grief without a category. Formation without a rubric. Harm outside the boundary. Fairness beyond the matrix. Public consequence before measurable interest. Representation that does not test well. The absent column becomes private knowledge.

That is the mechanism. It does not require villains. It does not require cynicism. It requires scale, scrutiny, and the entirely reasonable desire to defend decisions in language that survives people who were not in the room.

### Three Consequences

Once the mechanism is in motion, three consequences follow.

Judgment moves inward. It is still practiced by nurses, editors, researchers, managers, moderators, teachers, sustainability officers, and campaign staff. It is increasingly expressed in private because institutions cannot absorb it publicly without reopening the risks metrics were meant to contain. The nurse's *I'm not sure she was ready* belongs to the hallway. The teacher's notebook belongs to the desk. The director's memo about offset caveats belongs to the internal file. Judgment becomes a hidden subsidy that helps the official account succeed.

Burnout, distrust, quiet harm, and the sense that something essential is being performed rather than practiced are not random personal failures. They are structural traces of a gap between what was decided in auditable language and what was owed in human terms. The institution can close the file. The person often cannot. Both descriptions can be true at once.

Institutions lose the ability to hear what they displaced. To admit that a metric failed morally would require admitting that the metric was doing moral work. That admission would weaken the only language leadership can defend at scale. Moral knowledge that cannot enter in its own form tends to return only in metrics the institution already knows how to count.

### When the Proxy Becomes the Good

The final turn is the most subtle. The proxy does not only replace the value operationally. It begins to replace the value morally.

Good care becomes care that met the criteria. Good journalism becomes journalism that reached the audience. Good research becomes research that produced recognized output. Good leadership becomes leadership that moved the numbers. Good education becomes education that improved the score. The measure does not need to claim perfection. It becomes the good because it is the only account of the good the institution can repeat consistently.

This is when incentives become moral language in the full sense—not merely as coordination tools, but as the vocabulary in which institutions praise, blame, reward, and understand themselves. The map is treated as the territory. The translation is treated as the aim. The column is mistaken for the whole form.

Campbell's warning returns here: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to function reliably as a measure of what it was meant to measure.[^c9-campbell] The deeper problem is moral, not technical. Institutions begin answering the third question with evidence prepared for the first two. Was the procedure followed? Yes. Was the outcome defensible? Perhaps. Was it good? The last question no longer fits the form.

### What the Pattern Does Not Claim

Naming the pattern does not mean metrics are evil, markets are false, or modern institutions should dismantle their instruments. Scale requires proxies. Hospitals cannot coordinate through intuition alone. Platforms cannot sort billions of posts by hand. Universities cannot read every scholar. Democracies cannot govern solely through deliberative intimacy. Schools cannot escape accountability for inequity.

The argument is narrower. Proxies are necessary. Substitution is optional until it stops feeling optional. The danger is not translation. The danger is forgetting translation occurred.

Neither does the pattern imply that judgment, left alone, would govern justly. Judgment can be biased, cruel, habitual, and blind. Metrics can reveal what judgment normalized. Both truths have been held throughout because both are true. The choice is not between human wisdom and inhuman numbers. It is between keeping the distinction alive and allowing the proxy to become the only speech the institution can hear.

### The Surfaces Still in Use

Return to the objects. They have not disappeared. The whiteboard still coordinates shifts. The feed still connects people across distance. The journal shelf still signals rigor. The target still concentrates effort. The matrix still reduces arbitrary cruelty in some layoffs. The homepage still helps editors learn when they are inaccessible. The poll still reveals ignorance. The report card still tells parents something real.

Each surface remains useful. Each remains incomplete. The work is not to destroy the surfaces. It is to remember what they are: portable simplifications of values that were harder to carry, not the values themselves.

People already do this work unofficially. They speak in hallways, margins, notebooks, and memos the institution cannot metabolize. They protect investigations, students, patients, and colleagues in the space between what the system counts and what they know is owed. That unofficial work is not a substitute for institutional reform when reform is possible. It is evidence that the official language is incomplete—and that judgment has not died. It has been displaced.

The question that follows is who pays when displacement succeeds: which bodies carry the remainder, which communities absorb harm the target does not name, which workers keep systems running while presenting themselves as complete. Moral exhaustion is not always a private failure. It is often what happens when competent people discover they are maintaining, in unofficial form, the judgments their institutions can no longer speak—and are then asked to recover through attitude what structure continues to remove.

### Core Principle

**A Proxy Becomes Moral Language When the Institution Forgets It Is a Translation**

Incentives enter as answers to real coordination problems. They become moral language when the institution can no longer speak the aim except through the measure. The whiteboard, the feed, the shelf, the poster, the matrix, the homepage, the poll, the grade—each began as a way of making value travel. Together they teach a world in which what can be counted is what counts. Remembering the translation does not end the pattern. It changes what we see when we touch the next surface.
