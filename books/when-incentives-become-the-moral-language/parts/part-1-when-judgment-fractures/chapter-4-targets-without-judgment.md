# Chapter 4

## Targets Without Judgment

Central question: what does it mean to act morally when harm is global, delayed, and statistically distributed?

### The Original Aim: Reduce Harm

Climate policy and corporate responsibility programs begin with a moral claim that is not difficult to articulate: limit damage to people and places that cannot absorb it.

That claim is concrete even when its objects are large. Drought disrupts specific farms and specific families. Storm surge floods specific neighborhoods. Unsafe working conditions injure specific workers. The harm is real, even when the causal chain connecting corporate decisions to those outcomes runs through decades and continents.

For a long time, institutional language tried to follow that chain. Governments negotiated emissions limits. Companies published environmental commitments. NGOs pushed firms on practices that extended beyond legal requirements. The language was contested — argued across advocacy groups, industries, and political parties — but it was legible. You could ask whether a commitment would actually reduce the harm it named, whether the parties bearing costs were the same ones most exposed to risk, and whether the timeline was honest.

That kind of question-asking became harder to sustain as the underlying coordination problem scaled.

### Why Judgment Paralyzes Coordination at Scale

The difficulty is structural. Climate harm does not map cleanly onto organizational boundaries, annual reporting cycles, or the jurisdictional reach of any single regulator.

A factory that reduces its own emissions may shift production to a facility with weaker standards elsewhere. A net-zero pledge can be technically correct about operational carbon while leaving supply-chain emissions — often several times larger — unresolved. A carbon offset may represent reductions that are real, or permanent, or additional to what would have happened anyway, or none of those things, depending on methodological choices that specialists argue about and auditors rarely verify on the ground.[^c4-ref-2-world-bank-state]

This is not an argument that climate commitments are meaningless. It is an argument that moral responsibility under deep uncertainty is genuinely hard to own in institutional language. Causal chains are long. Benefits and costs are unevenly distributed across populations and time horizons. Uncertainty about physical outcomes is irreducible for decades.

In that environment, saying "in my judgment, this path is the responsible one" creates exposure. The judgment can always be challenged on its assumptions. Saying "we met the target" shifts the argument to compliance.

So institutions learned to lead with targets.

### How Targets Became the Survivable Language

Three mechanisms drove the shift from responsibility to seriousness.

First, emissions targets and science-aligned pledges. Net-zero commitments, science-based targets, and national caps translate moral urgency into dated numerical goals.[^c4-ref-1-united-nations-framework] They let institutions announce alignment with global benchmarks — the Paris Agreement, sectoral pathways, investor expectations — without resolving every downstream tradeoff their specific operations create. A target can be announced in good faith and still leave the hard questions open.

Second, carbon markets and offset mechanisms. Voluntary and compliance markets create instruments — credits, removals, avoidance claims — that let organizations report balance-sheet progress on emissions.[^c4-ref-2-world-bank-state] Whether a given offset represents actual, durable, additional harm reduction is a methodological question. Whether it satisfies a reporting threshold is a simpler one, and the simpler question is often what gets answered.

Third, ESG reporting frameworks. Disclosure regimes now require standardized metrics on emissions exposure, governance practices, and transition risk.[^c4-ref-3-international-sustainability-standards] Investors, ratings agencies, and boards use these reports to rank firms and allocate capital. The frameworks make comparison possible and create real pressure on laggards. They also reward disclosure quality in ways that are not always identical to harm reduction quality.

Together, these tools solve a genuine coordination problem. They make institutional behavior comparable across jurisdictions and sectors. They create shared language for capital markets. They establish checkpoints that would not otherwise exist.

They also make it possible to perform seriousness without settling the underlying moral question of who is responsible for what harm, to whom, and when.

### The Gap Between Accounting and Impact

Practitioners inside sustainability functions feel this gap regularly. Most entered the work because they cared about physical outcomes — emissions trajectories, community health, worker exposure, ecosystem integrity. Their expertise often lets them see exactly where the accounting diverges from the impact.

An offset purchase may be compliant and still direct resources to avoided-deforestation projects whose long-term durability is contested among scientists. A reported emissions reduction may be real at the facility level while the product manufactured there generates larger downstream emissions that fall into a different reporting category. A net-zero timeline may be technically plausible under optimistic assumptions about technology that does not yet exist at scale.

None of this is hidden. Specialists debate these gaps in conferences and working papers. The debates do not surface cleanly in institutional reporting because the reporting frameworks do not yet have a standard column for "here is what we do not know."

So the gap between what metrics show and what places experience stays largely internal. Teams carry it. Communities near still-polluting facilities carry it. Policymakers who understand the mechanics carry it. The institution continues to speak in the only language that survives its public accountability structures: target met, on track, disclosure complete.

### Seriousness as the Substitute for Responsibility

Responsibility requires owning specific tradeoffs: this decision increases harm to these people in exchange for these other outcomes, and we judge that exchange to be the best available option. That kind of speech requires public exposure of reasoning that can be challenged and proved wrong.

Seriousness requires demonstrating engagement with the recognized language of obligation — pledges, frameworks, disclosures, offsets — without necessarily accepting full causal ownership of outcomes. A serious institution publishes targets, files reports, participates in certification programs, and funds offset projects. It does these things in good faith. It may also still produce harm that its reporting structures are not designed to surface or assign.

This is the same substitution seen earlier in Part I: institutions converge on what can be counted, audited, and compared, while specific human costs travel as private knowledge inside the organization.

In climate governance the stakes are particularly high because the harm being managed is not only diffuse but cumulative and largely irreversible at the relevant timescales. What the accounting language cannot capture — long-horizon impact, locally concentrated harm, uncertainty about future physical conditions — is not a marginal remainder. It is much of the moral substance.

Action continues. Targets accumulate. Accountability for outcomes that cannot be expressed in reporting frameworks remains thin.
