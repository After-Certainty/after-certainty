# Chapter 2

## Engagement as a Theory of Value

Central question: when does attention stop being a signal and start being a definition of worth?

### The Original Claim: Connection and Access

Large technology platforms began with aims that were easy to say and genuinely meant. Connect people. Spread information. Lower the cost of communication. Give expression a wider stage.

Those aims carried moral weight. Connection implies that reaching each other is good. Access to information implies that people benefit from knowing more. Expression implies that speech deserves space before anyone has decided whether it deserves reward.

Early platforms made actual editorial choices in service of those aims. Engineers ranked content. Product teams surfaced posts. Moderation teams drew lines. The choices were imperfect and often contested, but the logic was traceable. If you wanted to know why a post appeared at the top of a feed, someone could explain in human terms why the decision made sense.

As platforms grew from millions of users to hundreds of millions, and then to billions operating across incompatible languages, legal regimes, and cultural contexts, that traceability started to create more problems than it solved.

### Why Judgment Became a Liability

Platforms did not set out to abandon editorial judgment. They discovered that maintaining it openly became impossible to defend.

Every ranking decision is, at scale, a value claim. When a post reaches a hundred million people because an algorithm surfaced it, someone has decided, implicitly, that this content is worth that attention. When a post is suppressed or demoted, someone has decided the opposite. Neither of those decisions can be explained to a global audience that holds incompatible values about speech, harm, authority, and truth.

Internal reasoning that worked fine in a small engineering team became dangerous when extracted into public controversy. Regulators in multiple countries demanded explanations. Journalists published internal memos. Congressional hearings became forums where former product managers described how ranking choices shaped political behavior.[^c2-zuckerberg-senate] The pattern was consistent: whatever explanation a platform offered, someone read it as evidence of bias or manipulation.

In that environment, offering a judgment meant defending a judgment. Platforms learned that explanation was not neutral. Every principled reason invited a principled countercharge.

So they stopped leading with judgment and shifted to a language that looked less like a position.

### How Engagement Replaced Editorial Language

The shift was not sudden and not cynical. Engagement signals were already being measured because they were genuinely informative. Click-through rates, watch time, shares, comments, reactions — these told product teams what people were actually doing, which was useful data for improving the experience.

The change was in how these signals were used.

Once editorial judgment became a legal and reputational liability, engagement metrics offered an alternative moral language. Instead of saying "we think this content is valuable," platforms could say "users engage with this content." Instead of saying "we think this content is harmful," they could point to lower engagement or trust signals. The behavioral data looked neutral because it described user actions rather than platform preferences.

This move had real advantages. It allowed coordination across teams that never met, in languages no individual spoke, at volumes no person could review. It let platforms face regulatory scrutiny and say, truthfully, that the system surfaced content users interacted with, not content editors selected.

Meta and Google described their ranking systems in these terms across multiple congressional testimonies: the algorithms reflect user relevance signals, not editorial preference.[^c2-pichai-house] Internal documents, including materials surfaced through the Facebook Papers, showed in more granular detail how engagement signals drove distribution decisions and where internal teams raised concerns about that relationship.[^c2-horwitz-seetharaman][^c2-haugen-senate]

What this language allowed was a particular kind of deflection. The system promoted what people engaged with. The people engaged. The engagement was real data. The platform was a mirror.

That claim is not fully false. It is also not the whole story.

### What Engagement Misses

Engagement measures behavioral response — whether someone clicked, paused, shared, or reacted. It does not measure whether the interaction improved understanding, strengthened connection, or advanced any of the original moral aims the platform named when it launched.

The signals that are easiest to elicit are not always the same as the signals that point toward meaning. Content that provokes anger, fear, or tribal solidarity tends to generate strong behavioral response. It earns engagement without necessarily earning trust, or producing the kind of connection anyone would have said they were building toward at the start.

Platforms know this. Internal research at several major platforms documented the relationship between engagement-optimized recommendations and increased exposure to divisive, emotionally activating content.[^c2-horwitz-seetharaman] The response was not always to change the optimization target; in several documented cases, proposals to reduce emotionally amplifying content were set aside because the engagement cost looked too large.

The argument was usually structural: users engage with what they engage with; changing the signal would change behavior, but in unpredictable directions; a platform that substitutes its own judgment about desirable behavior for the behavior users actually exhibit would be paternalistic, not neutral.

That argument is coherent. It is also a version of the same move the previous chapter described in healthcare: the system cannot absorb the moral cost without reopening risks it has already contained. So the cost moves elsewhere.

### How the Moral Residue Distributes

Platform workers often see the gap between stated aim and operational outcome more clearly than leadership can acknowledge publicly. Content moderators review material that engagement logic sends to wide audiences while being told they are not editorial decision-makers. Ranking engineers who raised concerns about amplification dynamics found those concerns weighed against user-growth targets. Former executives and researchers who left major platforms have described the experience in consistent terms: an organization that knew more than it said, and was structurally prevented from saying what it knew without destabilizing the defense it relied on.[^c2-haugen-senate]

This is not a story of villains. The structural logic is easy to follow. Once neutrality becomes the institutional position, admitting editorial culpability threatens the entire legal and reputational architecture built on that position. The individual people who see the gap clearly cannot fix it unilaterally. They carry the knowledge privately. The organization continues to speak in the only language it can defend.

Users experience something adjacent. They notice that what their feeds surface does not always match what they said they wanted, that the attention the platform gives them is calibrated to keep them present rather than informed. The experience is difficult to name because no one decided to manipulate them; a system optimized for behavioral response. The manipulation, such as it is, is structural.

That diffusion of responsibility is the distinctive moral pattern of this chapter. Healthcare concentrated the gap between system language and human cost at the discharge conversation. Platform governance distributes the gap across billions of interactions with no single identifiable moment where judgment was renounced.

### What Remains of the Original Aim

Technology platforms still speak about connection, access, and expression. Those aims appear in public statements, mission documents, and congressional testimony. They are not cynically deployed. Many people who work at these companies still pursue them.

But the operational language that governs daily decisions is engagement. What spreads is what the system treats as valuable. What holds attention gets more attention. What the algorithm surfaces defines, in practice, what the platform believes is worth surfacing — regardless of what the mission statement says.

Engagement began as a behavioral signal and became a theory of value. Not because anyone announced the transition, but because the alternative — public editorial judgment across incompatible audiences — was no longer survivable.

That shift is the pattern this chapter names. Platforms continue to function. Content continues to flow. Attention continues to accumulate. But moral responsibility for what the attention economy builds has no institutional address.

In Chapter 3, the same pattern appears in academic publishing — where visibility metrics replace the slower, harder-to-defend standards that truth-seeking institutions once used to coordinate disagreement.
