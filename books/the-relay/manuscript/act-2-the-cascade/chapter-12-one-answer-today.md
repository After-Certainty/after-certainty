# Chapter 12 — One Answer, Today

Lena had put twenty-six people on the *Wren,* and the *Wren* had not come out the other side.

She had not owned the boat or piloted it or signed for it. She had done something worse, the thing only she could have done: she had *cleared the route.* At Tellan Transfer there'd been two hulls loading for the north and a hundred frightened people trying to choose between them, and the choosing had no information in it, only rumor and fear and the price of a berth, and Lena had stood on the loading floor and done the one thing she was good at. She'd read it. The *Marn* was bound up the Coll spur — she'd marked it the bad bet in a breath: a pilot lying about his fuel, a route that the canyon silence told her was already going quiet, a hull she wouldn't have put a crate of salt on. The *Wren* was taking the long rim hook around the Vesper dark, the slow careful way, a sound boat with an honest captain and a route Lena herself had crossed pieces of and judged still live. So she'd told people. Not loudly. The way couriers tell things — a word at an elbow, *not that one, this one,* the quiet authority of someone who knew. Twenty-six people had taken the *Wren* because a courier with good eyes said it was the better bet.

The *Marn* limped into Coll four days late, out of fuel, half-frozen, every soul aboard alive — its crew already grumbling, she'd hear later, about the berth charges and the four days' lost trade, alive enough to be petty about it.

The *Wren* hit a gate that went dark while it was in the hook — the rim crossing she'd judged live had died in the four days it took to reach it, the way they all died now, with no notice and no author — and a sound boat with an honest captain and twenty-six people who'd trusted a stranger's good eyes went into a dark that did not give anything back.

---

She learned it at Borren, where she'd come down off Tellan herself on a third hull, expecting to find the *Wren*'s people ahead of her and trade news. She found the news instead, on a board, the way she'd learned everything since Vesper: a line of cargo logged *lost in transit* — which on the rim was not a soft word, not *delayed,* not *missing,* but the hard one, the one that meant no return tone, no beacon, no hull-signature, no path by which anyone would ever go out and bring them back — and under it, because Borren still kept the old courtesy, a hand-written list of the souls. She read the names. She'd put faces to four of them on the Tellan floor. A man with a bad hip who'd thanked her and shifted his weight off it as he turned, careful of the long trip ahead. A girl traveling alone to a grandmother she'd never met, who had asked Lena — shy, practical — which end of the *Wren* kept warmer on a long hook, and Lena had said the stern, by the galley, and meant it as a kindness. A small boy with a red travel-bag almost as big as he was, asleep on his mother's shoulder while the mother counted coins for the berth. The other twenty-two she'd never met and had killed exactly as thoroughly, with a word at an elbow, *this one.*

There was a particular silence that came after, and Lena went into it and could not find the bottom.

She had built her whole self on the gift. Not the word — she'd never have called it that — but the thing under it: that she *noticed,* that she could feel a route going wrong the way she could feel a manifest lying, that her attention was true and worth trusting, that in a world of frightened people guessing she was the one who actually saw. It had survived Anneth, where she'd doubted whether her truth was useful. It had survived Drevin, where it had only made her angry. It did not survive the board at Borren. Because the gift had not failed her at Tellan — that was the horror she kept walking into and out of like a cold room — the gift had *worked.* Her reading of the *Marn* was correct; that pilot was lying, that route was dying. Her reading of the *Wren* was correct too, on the day she made it. The route had been live. She'd been right with everything she had, right the way the Anneth Watch had been right, right the way Varik was right, and twenty-six people were dead of her rightness because the thing killing the network did not care how good your eyes were. It killed the live route four days after you cleared it. It made a liar of attention itself.

If she couldn't trust that — if being right was no longer a thing that kept people alive — then she did not know what she was for, and she had been moving for a month on nothing but the belief that she was for something.

She sat on the floor of the Borren concourse with her back against a cold stanchion and her dead scanner in her lap, and for the first time since the gate at Vesper had gone quiet she did not get up and find the next road. There was no next road. Every road was the *Wren.* Every word at an elbow was twenty-six names on a board. She had crossed a quarter of the network telling frightened people the truth was more complicated than they wanted, and the complicated truth had just killed more people in one crossing than every clean lie she'd argued with, and sitting on the cold floor she could not locate a single reason that her way of being in the world was better than Varik's, except that hers felt worse, and feeling worse was not a virtue, it was just feeling worse.

Across the concourse, a knot of slow-way hands stood at the lost-cargo board — rim pilots in off the long dark, frost still whitening their coat-seams, reading the same list she had. One of them kept shaking his head at it and saying a thing to the others, low, the same thing twice, his mouth moving around words she couldn't hear and didn't try to. It was just pilots talking. Everything now was just people talking.

---

Varik found her there. Of course he did. He was working Borren — the fuel crews had locked themselves in the pump house, someone had said at Khoury, a hundred years and a lifetime ago — and Borren was small, and a courier on the floor was the kind of thing he noticed, because noticing what people needed was the whole of his gift the way reading routes had been the whole of hers.

He didn't make a thing of it. He came and sat down on the cold floor beside her, not above her, the way he'd sat with the grandfather at Khoury — smelling of fuel-oil and pump-house cold, his hands black to the wrist from whatever he'd just put right — and for a while he didn't say anything, which she had not expected and which got past her guard more than words would have. He'd have read the board. He read everything. He knew what the names were and he knew she'd cleared the route, because he kept track, because his word still arrived.

"The *Wren,*" he said finally.

"I told them to take it."

"I know."

"It was the right call." Her voice came out flat and strange. "That's the—it was the *right call,* Varik. I read both hulls true. The boat I sent them away from is alive in Coll. The boat I sent them to is gone. I did everything right and I—" She stopped, because there was nowhere for the sentence to go.

"Yes," he said. Not arguing. For a moment something in him moved toward the easy thing — she caught it, the small breath taken to say *it wasn't your fault,* the reflex even he had — and then he set it down and chose the harder kindness instead. That was the terrible thing about him. He didn't tell her it wasn't her fault, the lie a lesser comforter would have reached for, the lie that would have let her push back. He let it be true. "You did everything right, and they're dead anyway. That's the thing nobody will say to you, so I will. Doing everything right doesn't work anymore. It hasn't for months. That's not your failure. It's the condition we're living in." He looked at the board across the concourse. "I've watched a hundred careful people learn that this season. The careful ones take it hardest. You built a life on getting it right, and the world has stopped paying out on right, and you're sitting on the floor because you can't find the new rule."

It was so exactly the inside of her head that she turned and looked at him, and he looked back with that worn, smooth grief she'd seen at Khoury, the sister-shaped wound he carried, and she understood that he was not performing this. He had sat on a floor like this once. He knew the room because he'd lived in it.

"There's a way out of the room," he said. "You're not going to like where it is, because you've spent your whole life on the other side of it. But you're ready to hear it now, which you weren't at Khoury." He turned to face her. "Stop trying to be right."

"That's—"

"Hear it. You've been trying to *understand* it — the whole thing, the real cause, the complicated truth, the thing too big to see. And you're good, Lena, you're maybe the best I've met, and it just put twenty-six people in the dark, because understanding takes time and the dark doesn't give you time. Every hour you spend getting it right is an hour the people in front of you spend hungry and scared and dying on routes nobody cleared at all." His voice was so gentle. That was the danger of it; it had no edge to brace against. "I'm not asking you to believe a lie. I'm asking you to stop demanding certainty you can't have before you'll let yourself act. You don't need to know the true cause to feed a child. You don't need the whole picture to get a convoy moving. You need one answer, good enough, today — and the courage to act on it while the people who need to be *sure* are still drawing their charts."

*One answer, today.*

And here was the thing she would be ashamed of for the rest of her life and never quite stop understanding: she almost said yes.

Not to the sabotage story. She still knew it was wrong, somewhere under the grief, the way you know a fact in a language you've stopped speaking. But to the *rest* of it — to the offer underneath the offer, which was *put it down.* Put down the manifest of contradictions. Put down the duty-fear declared at every wicket. Put down the gift that had turned to poison, the attention that had killed twenty-six people, the unbearable solitary labor of being the one who saw the whole and could fix none of it. Become one answer. Become useful. Let a man who had never once frozen on a cold floor tell her which corner to lift, and lift it, and feed someone tonight, and stop, finally, *stop* trying to hold a thing no one person could hold. He was offering her rest. He was offering her the thing she had wanted since Vesper without ever letting herself name it: permission to not carry it alone, by the simple expedient of not carrying it at all.

She opened her mouth, and in the half-second before she spoke she did not know which way it was going to go. That was the low place. Not that he tempted her with a lie. That he offered her relief, and she was so tired, and for one half-second the difference between *I'll help you* and *I'll decide for you* dissolved completely, and she wanted to be decided for, the way you want to lie down in snow.

---

What stopped her was so small she almost didn't notice it stop her.

Down the concourse, at the lost-cargo board, the frost-coated hand she'd half-seen when she sat down was still there, still reading the *Wren*'s name with the others — and now, turning to go, he said it loud enough to carry, to no one, to the cold air, the thing those pilots said now: "Should've sent word ahead. The watch-folk out past the dark had the rim hook flagged a week back. Bad water on the approach, light gone wrong over the marsh — they had it on a list. Anyone reading the lists wouldn't have cleared that crossing." He shook his head and moved off. "Funny old folk. Swapping notes about a marsh while the world ends."

*They had it on a list.*

Lena heard it the way you hear your own name through a wall. The settled people, the ones who never travel, passing scraps hand to hand — a light gone wrong over a marsh, a tide a quarter-watch late, *so the next one knows what to watch.* And a week before she stood on the Tellan floor and cleared the rim hook with her good eyes and all her gift, someone out past the dark who had never crossed a gate in their life had already known the approach was going wrong — not because they were smarter, not because they had better eyes, but because they had *written it down and handed it on,* and she had not been holding the one piece of paper that would have kept twenty-six people out of the dark.

And she had seen that kind of paper before. Folded behind the dead panel of her scanner, carried seven worlds, were the pages Jun had sent after her at Vesper before the *Osprey-Nine* cast off — the same cramped, hurried hand, the same un-stamped honesty, references copied out by a man who could whisper an uncertain true thing to one leaving courier and not say it to a room. *None of these go where the records say they go.* No seal on them. No signature office. Just a person who'd seen something wrong and meant it for whoever came next. She had never quite understood why she kept them. She understood now, at least this much: Jun's pages and the marsh-watchers' list were the same kind of thing — the true word gone quiet and un-stamped, slipped hand to hand because the offices that used to carry it had gone dark.

She had been right with everything she had — and someone who had never crossed a gate had already known what she needed a week earlier, because they had written it down and handed it on. Varik wanted one answer today. The pilot wanted anyone reading the lists. She could not say yes to putting it all down while that scrap was still in her, unaccounted for, refusing to lie flat.

"No," she said. To Varik. To the snow.

He didn't flinch, or push. He just nodded, slowly, the way he'd nodded when Bram let go of the lock-bar — as though her refusal, too, had been expected, and therefore deserved no spectacle. "All right," he said, and stood, and put out a hand to help her up, and she took it because refusing the hand was a different fight than refusing the answer. "You'll come to it," he said, not unkindly. "Maybe not to me. But you'll come to *one answer* eventually, Lena, because the alternative is the floor, and you've got a brother to reach." He said it as a gift, and it was, and it was also the truest threat anyone had made her since Vesper. "When you do, I'll still be here. I keep track."

He went back to his fuel crews — not hurrying, never hurrying, a man walking toward the next solvable thing with no need to arrive breathless. By nightfall, someone said, he'd talked the Borren pump-house open the way he'd talked Bram off the lock-bar: fuel moving again, a count posted, his name in the liability box, the station left running on rails he'd laid and walked away from. He had not lied to her once on that cold floor, and he had fixed, in an afternoon, a real thing that was killing people; the man who had offered to decide for her was also, demonstrably, the most useful person on the rim. Lena stood up off the cold floor on legs that didn't entirely want her, with twenty-six names on the board behind her and something lodged in her chest she couldn't name, and understood that she had come within one half-second and one frost-coated pilot of stopping — of becoming a name on someone else's list, checked, accounted for, told which corner to lift.

She didn't get warm again for a long time after Borren. But she got up, and she found the next road, which went, like all of them, sideways — and she walked it carrying the dead and the snag together, holding onto the one thing the cold floor had left her: that somewhere out past the dark gate, people who had never moved in their lives had already known what she'd needed to know, and the only thing wrong with what they knew was that it had no way to reach her in time.

She did not yet know whose hand was behind the lists, and she was a long way still from knowing. But for the first time since Vesper she was walking toward a question and not only away from a shut gate — not only *how do I reach Arin,* but the second one the *Wren* had cut into her: *how does a true word reach the person who needs it before they're a name on a board?* She had no idea yet what an answer would even look like. She only knew she could no longer leave the snag where she'd found it — that the question, not the gate, was what carried her north now.
