# Chapter 10 — Kind and Not Enough

Lena knew the woman before she remembered her, which was its own kind of unsettling — recognition arriving ahead of the name, the way a smell finds you before you can place the kitchen.

It was the third night on the Calder pilgrim road, in a wayhouse that had been built for forty and was holding two hundred, and a fight was starting over blankets. Not a real fight yet — the kind that was still deciding whether to be one, two exhausted men and a dwindling stack of wool and a circle of people too tired to stop it and too cold to walk away. Lena had seen how this went. She'd been *watching* how this went for a month, the small ugly arithmetic of not-enough, and she'd already marked the exits and decided it wasn't hers to fix, because nothing lately was hers to fix; she just carried the watching from world to world like a parcel addressed to no one.

Then a woman crossed the room into the middle of it, and the fight forgot what it was about.

She didn't shout. She didn't get between them, exactly. She just arrived, with the unhurried completeness of someone who had never once in her life believed that arriving faster would help, and put a hand on the blanket stack — not taking it, just resting there, a claim so mild it wasn't a claim — and said something Lena couldn't hear, and one of the men laughed, the surprised ugly laugh of anger losing its grip, and then she was folding the wool and handing it out by some logic that wasn't first-come and wasn't loudest and somehow nobody argued with, and the cold room rearranged itself around her calm the way iron filings line up around a magnet. Two minutes. No speech. No story. Just a person being entirely unafraid in a frightened room, and the room borrowing it.

And Lena, watching from the wall with her pack between her feet, thought: *Sennen.*

The quay at Sennen, a month and a lifetime ago, the gate stuttering, the dock half-panicked — and one woman in the middle of it who hadn't panicked, who'd had a pot of something hot going against a wall out of the wind, who'd handed Lena a cup she didn't ask for and pointed her at the one berth still sailing and said nothing wise at all, only the next true small thing. Lena had filed her the way she filed everyone — *not panicking, doesn't want anything, file it* — and moved on, and never learned her name.

Here she was again, four worlds north, folding blankets on the pilgrim road. The same calm. Lena had crossed a quarter of the network in the time it had taken this woman to walk one road on foot, and somehow they'd arrived at the same cold wayhouse on the same night, which meant either the network was smaller than it felt or grief ran on rails of its own.

"You were at Sennen," Lena said, when the blankets were done. "The quay. You gave me tea."

The woman looked at her, and something kind and amused moved in her face — not the flicker of being recognized, which most people enjoyed, but the deeper ease of someone for whom being remembered and being forgotten were about equally fine. "I give a lot of people tea," she said. "It's most of what I'm good for." She put out a hand. "Sera."

---

Lena fell in with her because the road went one way and so did they, and because — she could admit this to herself, in the cold, four worlds from anyone who'd known her before — she was tired of being the only person in every room working to keep her fear from choosing the answer, and wanted, for a few days, to stand near someone who did it better.

The Calder world was half-empty. That was the thing the pilgrim road was for now. It had been a populous place, terraced farm-worlds strung under a busy gate, and when the gate went dark the people had started walking — not in panic, that was over, just the long grey exodus of a world draining toward the two crossings that still worked, an old religious road that ran between them suddenly carrying the whole population's worth of the displaced. Wayhouse to wayhouse, shelter to shelter. Sera walked it the way Lena had once run freight: as the work, the whole work, no destination of her own. She'd been walking it for weeks. She'd walk it, she said, as long as people were on it.

"And then what?" Lena asked, the first day. "When they get to the crossing. Then where do you go?"

"Wherever the next road full of people is." Sera said it without weight, stepping over a washout in the path. "There's always a next road now. I used to worry I'd run out of road." A small smile. "I don't worry about that anymore."

She was, Lena came to see, exactly what she appeared to be, which was the rarest thing of all. Not a saint, not a fool — Sera was funny, dry in a warm way, knew the price of everything on the road and haggled when she had to, told a frightened child a joke instead of a comfort because the joke worked better. She noticed people the way Lena noticed cargo: instantly, completely, without trying. She knew who in the column was about to fall behind, who was giving their ration away and lying about it, which of the two factions that had formed among the walkers — there were always factions — was about to need talking down. She moved among them and they steadied. It was real. Lena had spent a month learning to tell the real thing from the performance of it, and this was real.

It was also, Lena could not stop seeing, not nearly enough.

---

She tested it the way you test ice, a little weight at a time.

"There's a man up the rim," she said, the second night, both of them watching a cook-fire that two hundred people were sharing in shifts. "Varik. You'll have heard the name by now."

"I've heard it." Sera fed a stick to the fire. "They say he's good. Feeds people. Faces down crowds."

"He does. I watched him do it. He took a station nine days from a riot and had it running in an hour. Named the children. Put his own name where the blame would land." Lena watched Sera's face and found nothing to push against. "And then he told four hundred frightened people exactly who was doing this to them — saboteurs, cutters, separatists out past the dark — and they believed him, because he'd just fed them, and it was a lie. Or near enough. He doesn't know any more than I do. He just decided, and gave them the deciding to hold, and they loved him for it."

Sera was quiet a while, feeding a stick to the fire with her free hand. "And you can't do that," she said. Not unkind.

"I won't do that."

"Mm." A log settled. "There's less daylight between *can't* and *won't* than we like." She said it gently, and it went in deep. "What would you tell them? If they asked you, the way they ask him. Four hundred frightened people, and you've got the floor."

This was the question. It was the Anneth man's question, the one she'd lost the gate over, the one she carried now like the anger and the fear, a thing declared at every wicket and never set down. Lena had thought about it across four more worlds and still didn't have it.

"That it's bigger than a villain," she said slowly. "That the gates aren't being cut, they're — the whole thing's been running on people quietly minding their own piece and assuming someone minds the rest, and the someone stopped, everywhere, so slow nobody saw. That the answer isn't to find who did it. It's to — " she stopped, hearing it, the place where the true thing ran out of road. "It's to start telling each other what we see again. I think. I don't know what that looks like. I just know his answer is wrong."

"It's a good true thing," Sera said, warming her palms over the flames. "It won't feed anyone tonight."

"No."

"That's the part I made my peace with a long time ago." She looked at the two hundred people sharing one fire — none of them eating each other, all of them still here. "I used to think if I could say the right thing, people would hold. Then I watched a lot of people not hold, and a lot of others hold with no true thing at all, just each other." She handed Lena a cup she'd kept warm against her coat. "So I stopped trying to give them the answer. I give them tea. Blankets. Each other. Most days that's enough to walk a road on."

"And the days it isn't?"

Sera smiled — the saddest thing on the road, with no self-pity in it. "Most true things don't prove enough. Mine doesn't. His doesn't." She tucked a blanket edge under a sleeping child's chin without looking. "I picked the one that doesn't get anyone hurt when it falls short."

---

On the fourth day a fear came up the road faster than the people walking it: the upper crossing was closing — had closed — would close before the column reached it. Nobody knew which. Nobody knew anything; the word had come back down the line the way all words came now, mouth to mouth, swelling as it went. The road heard it and shuddered.

A knot of families near the front broke for a shortcut — a high cut over the ridge that somebody's cousin swore was passable and would save a day, if it went, and would kill you if it didn't, and no one on the road could say which, because no one on the road had walked it this season.

Sera waded in the way she'd waded into the blanket fight, and most of it she held. She walked the line with her hand on the panic and it eased under her touch; she kept the bulk of the column to the known road, slow and sure, a known road open being worth two shortcuts maybe-closed. It was the same marvel as the first night — terror talked down to walking-pace by one unafraid woman.

But one family went. A man and his wife and two small children and the cousin with the story, up into the rocks in the failing light, and Sera could not hold them, because the only thing that would have held them was the one thing she didn't have: someone who *knew* — who'd walked the cut this season and could say *passable,* or *don't.* She had calm to give and no fact to give, and calm does not beat a shortcut when there are hungry children and a day to save. She watched them climb out of sight, and her face, for once, was not enough — not even for her. "It might be open," she said — to Lena, to the road, to no one. Nobody came back down to tell them otherwise, which on this road could mean the family had made it or could mean the other thing, and there was no way on earth to know which. The not-knowing ran both directions, and there was no one and nothing on that road to carry the answer back down it.

---

Lena wanted, with a force that frightened her, to believe her.

That was the thing she'd carry off the Calder road, the way she'd carried the wanting off Khoury — except this was the cleaner ache, the one with no shame in it. With Varik she'd wanted him to be right and hated the wanting, because his rightness had a price tag she could read. With Sera there was no trick to resist, no door being closed, no name being handed out to organize a mob around. Sera asked nothing, sold nothing, would have folded blankets for the saboteurs themselves if they'd turned up cold. To stand near her was to be quietly invited to set the whole thing down — the manifest of contradictions, the duty-fear, the anger, the brother going further away — and simply *help,* here, tonight, the person in front of you, and trust the rest to hold.

And Lena couldn't. That was the trouble with Sera, and with the road: she'd crossed too many worlds where goodwill had shown up with nowhere to put itself, and she'd just watched it happen again — a family climbing a ridge no one could vouch for while the kindest woman on the rim stood helpless at the bottom with nothing to give them but calm. *Trust them to carry each other* had no answer for the morning the carrying ran out, and none at all for the plain question of which road was open.

---

The lists came to her on the last night, and she almost missed them.

A slow-way pilot had put in at the upper crossing — one of the rim hands who ran the long dark routes around the Vesper gate, the way Osko did, the way Nadi wouldn't for her. He was drinking the wayhouse's bad tea and telling the table the strange thing he'd been telling tables all the way down, because pilots, like couriers, carried the odd as freight.

"Funniest thing out past the dark," he said. "The settled folk. The ones that never travel — lake-people, hill-people, the ones who couldn't tell you what world's next door because they've never once wanted to know. They've started trading *lists.*"

"Lists of what?" someone asked.

"That's the funny part. Nothing. Nothing you'd carry." He scratched his jaw. "Unseasonals. Marsh-light gone green. Cooling-river milky. Sixth-day tide late. Birds missing from the week they always come. Nothing worth carrying, unless you live where it happened." He laughed. "They write it down — little scraps, hand to hand, settlement to settlement, slow as the slow way — and pass it on. Not for trade. There's no money in a late tide. Asked one of them what it was for. She looked at me like I'd asked what air was for. Said, *so the next one knows what to watch.* Whole network falling down around their ears and they're swapping notes about the weather."

"Here." He dug in a vest pocket and came out with it — a scrap of coarse grey pulp gone soft from handling, which he flattened on the wet table under a thick thumb. Someone had written on it in charcoal, blocky and unpracticed, the hand of a person who didn't write often: *Marsh-light gone green, out of season. Cooling-river milky three days. Sixth-day tide a quarter late — tell the next.* No date, no seal, no signature office — none of the purple machine-stamps Lena had been reading off dead cargo for a month. Just a few lines a person had seen with their own eyes and meant for the next pair of eyes coming. "Fellow two crossings back pressed it on me like it was coin," the pilot said. "Keep meaning to toss it." He didn't. He tucked it back in his vest.

*Sixth-day tide a quarter late.* Lena's hand went cold around her cup before the rest of her caught up to why. The table laughed, and moved on, and she did not.

A tide a quarter-watch late. She'd stood on a bank at Anneth while a town gave thanks for the dry-bell flood, and she'd told them there was no who under it, and lost the gate. A light gone the wrong color. The canyon guide, reading silence off a wall where a town should be loud. Unseasonals. The exact little departures she'd been crossing the network collecting in her full drawer, the deviations nobody could explain and everybody filed — and somewhere out past the dark gate, the people who never moved were not filing them. They were *passing them on.* So the next one knows what to watch.

Across the wayhouse, Sera looked up from folding a child's coat and caught Lena staring at the scrap in the pilot's vest pocket. She didn't ask. That was one of her gifts — she saw the snag take, and let it be Lena's.

She didn't know what it was. She told herself it was nothing, a curiosity, weather-talk among the stranded — the pilot clearly thought so. She filed it with everything else.

But it didn't lie flat in the drawer the way the other things did. It snagged. Lena lay awake in the cold wayhouse with two hundred breathing strangers and Sera somewhere among them keeping them human, one hand resting on her pack where the dead scanner and the duty-receipt rode — the tool that read nothing now and the chit that said she'd carried fear across a border honestly. Her fingers wouldn't settle against the canvas while the room breathed around her in its ragged unison. Out past the dark, people who never moved were writing scraps and handing them on. *So the next one knows what to watch.*

It stayed with her the way a half-heard name does — the certainty that you know it, riding just under the certainty that you don't. She left the Calder road the next morning before Sera woke, because goodbyes were a luxury and she'd never been good at them, and carried the snag north with the rest of her cargo. She would not understand it for a long time. But she'd stopped, somewhere in the night, being able to pretend it was nothing.
