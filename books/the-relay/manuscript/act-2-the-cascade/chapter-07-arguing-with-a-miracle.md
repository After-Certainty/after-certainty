# Chapter 7 — Arguing With a Miracle

The causeway to the gate at Anneth had been closed for six days, and the whole town was alive because of it, and Lena was the only person on the flats who thought the reason was wrong.

She had come in on the morning tide-boat to a place in the middle of giving thanks — figures kneeling out on the drying mud at low water, casting handfuls of dried marsh-flower onto the last of the retreating tide, the way people thank a thing they are also afraid of. The flats reeked of cold salt and zinc, and under it, still, the green-water stink of the dry-bell flood, the flood that had climbed after the crossing bell rang safe. Anneth was a tidal world — a low country of salt-flats and stilt-houses under a gate-arch that stood a mile out across the mud, reachable at the ebb by the ebb-stones, a stone causeway laid down longer ago than anyone bothered to claim, bare only when the water withdrew. You crossed when the flats were dry and you were off them before the water came back, and that had been the rhythm of the place for so long that the schedule of it was holier than any calendar. Lena needed that gate, and not abstractly. It was the one door on this whole quiet stretch that still answered, and it pointed, more or less, north — onto the floodmark arch at Coll, a door that woke only when the tide climbed high enough to touch its feet, and the next flood that lined up northward came round in three days and then not for nine after. Her coin-roll was down to a few crossings. Miss the Coll tide and she'd sit out an Anneth week she couldn't pay for, while Arin got further away in the dark.

It was also barred, with a painted trestle and two volunteers in oiled coats, and a hand-lettered board that read, in the careful capitals of people who had decided something together: **CAUSEWAY CLOSED BY ORDER OF THE WATCH. WE DO NOT WAIT TO BE CUT.**

"You can't close the ebb-stones," Lena said to the nearer volunteer, reasonably, the way she'd have said it to any dockhand. "That's the only live crossing for four worlds. There are people behind me who need it."

"There are people behind you who are *breathing,*" the woman said, not unkindly, and pointed her chin out across the flats. "On account of it being closed."

So Lena got the story, because everyone on Anneth was telling it, and it was a good one. A fortnight back, the Watch had started reading the tide wrong. Not wrong: the tide had started reading wrong, and they were the ones who noticed. They were a handful of locals who'd taken up the cutter's almanac off the feeds: tide-stakes, cooling-river, marsh-light, every late mark copied under the same accusation. The marks came in a finger high and a quarter-watch late, three days running. The cooling-river that fed the gate ran milky and then ran clear. Little things. The kind of thing the old causeway-keepers would have shrugged at and the Watch had been trained, by the almanac, to treat as tampering — *someone's working the footings, someone's loosening the line, they cut the threads and wait for us to argue in the dark.* So the Watch closed the causeway. Pulled the market that crossed at every ebb, turned back the stilt-villages who came in to trade, took the insult of it for six days while the town muttered that they'd gone mad with rumor.

On the sixth night the water came after the dry bell. Not a finger high — a man's height, and early, and fast, the flats vanishing in the dark in the time it took to understand they were vanishing. The crossing bell had already rung safe; the sea climbed anyway. It stood deep over the ebb-stones for two full watches. Anyone crossing would have been a mile out on bare stone with black water coming faster than a person runs.

Nobody had been crossing. Because the Watch did not wait to be cut.

The flats still wore the proof of it. As the volunteer talked, Lena's eye kept drifting to the causeway trestle: a tide-line stained across it at chest height, a full hand above the highest watermark the weathered stone had ever carried, and snarled in the rail above even that, a thick rope of eelgrass left hanging where no eelgrass had any business being. Dead channel-fish silvered in the ebb-stones, stranded a mile up from their water. A boy in an oiled coat too big for him knelt on the trestle, scraping a skin of green river-mud off a course of stone that should never in seven centuries have gone under — Watch, by the band on his sleeve, grey-faced with however many nights he'd stood out reading stakes by lamplight. He didn't look vindicated. He looked like someone who had watched the water nearly take everyone and couldn't stop touching the place it had spared. The sea had left its argument all over the town, at a height that made Lena's reasonable voice feel like trespass before she'd even used it.

"Forty would've been out there," the volunteer said. "Market morning's the seventh. We'd have had the whole low market on the stones and the children besides." She said it plainly, a woman reciting a fact she had decided never to get used to. "They told us it was sabotage and we called them cranks. Then the water came." She looked at Lena with the flat, finished patience of someone who has stopped needing to be believed because the sea agreed with her. "So. The causeway's closed till the Watch says the threads are safe. You'll want the long way."

---

Lena should have said *thank you* and gone looking for the long way. She knew it even as she opened her mouth. She had crossed seven worlds learning exactly how to keep her doubts in her cuff where they couldn't get her thrown off a dock, and she had a coin-roll down to its last crossings and a brother going further away by the day and a Coll tide she could still make if she only stopped talking, and not one good reason to argue with a town that had just outrun a drowning.

But she had spent those same seven worlds watching this story eat the truth in front of her, and here it was being crowned, and something in her that was tired and frightened and right would not sit down.

"The flood," she said. "The dry-bell flood. That wasn't sabotage."

The volunteer's face didn't close, exactly. It just went somewhere Lena couldn't follow it. "The water came a man's height early. You think that's natural?"

"No. I think it's wrong. I just don't think there's a *who* under it." Lena heard her own voice doing the thing she hated and could not stop it. "At Vesper the lamp went first. At Sennen the arch thinned before it failed. Here the water came after the dry bell. That isn't one person at your footings. It's something moving through local things — tides, rivers, gates — and showing up where people live. You closed the ebb-stones for the right reason and the wrong cause."

"And how," said a new voice, "is that different from being lucky?"

This was an older man, come up the bank in the unhurried way of someone people made room for — not the Watch, Lena would learn, but the man whose grandfather had kept the causeway, which on Anneth was a kind of throne. He wasn't hostile. That was the trouble. He was curious, and kind, and entirely certain, and he had buried no one this fortnight and meant to keep it that way.

"You're telling these people," he said, "that the thing that saved their children was a guess about the wrong reason. And maybe you're even right. But walk it forward for me, courier. Suppose tomorrow the marks come in high again. By your account there's no one to catch, no footing to mend, nothing to *do* — it's the whole network, everywhere, too big to see. So what would you have the Watch do with that?"

"Watch the tide," Lena said. "Same as they did. Just — without inventing a villain to make the watching feel like winning."

"The villain is why they watched." He said it gently, as a correction a teacher makes. "You take away the who, you take away the watching. I've listened to careful people my whole life, and careful people told us the marks were nothing right up until the water. The crank with the pry-bar story got everyone off the stones." He spread his hands. "Maybe you've the right of it. Maybe there's no one out at the footings with a bar, and the cutters are a tale somebody told. I'll grant a stranger that much. But the tale put forty pairs of eyes on the tide-stakes while the careful folk were still smoothing their ledgers and waiting to be sure. You're asking us to trade a story that saved forty lives for a truer one that saves none. Out here that's not wisdom. That's just a colder way to be useless."

And there it was — she could feel the flats tilt under her, the awful sideways slide of being *right* and *losing* in the same breath, because everything he'd said was true and it added up to a wall. She had crossed worlds carrying her certainty like contraband, sure the sabotage story was a hole somebody had given a face, and now the hole had saved children and the face was getting statues, and she had nothing to set against it but *it's more complicated than that,* which had never once, in the history of frightened people, beaten *here is who to blame and here is what to do.*

"Who do they say it is?" she asked. Quietly. She didn't know why she asked, except that she wanted to hear them say it out loud, the name under the miracle.

"The cutters," the man said. "Out past the dark gates. The ones who want the old order back, working the routes one footing at a time."

"That's not a who." Lena said it before she'd decided to, flat and certain, the truest thing she'd said all morning and the worst-timed. "That's a hole with grammar. You lost something you can't see, so you gave it a subject and a verb so you'd have somebody to watch for."

She knew the moment it landed: it was exactly the wrong sentence. Not because it was untrue. Because it was clever, on a bank where forty people had nearly drowned, said to a man who had spent six days being right while the town called him mad. She watched it reach him and curdle. The kindness went out of his face — not into anger, into something worse, the polite, final shutting of a door that has decided you are not a danger, only a draft.

"Mind the long way," he said. "The flats take strangers who talk too long." And he went back up the bank, and the volunteer turned the painted trestle a few degrees so that it faced Lena more squarely, and that was the end of her passage through Anneth.

---

The long way off Anneth was no way at all. The Watch had closed the live gate; the other crossings were the dead ones, the dark arches she'd been crabbing around for a fortnight. She was on a world with one working door and she had just argued the door's keepers out of opening it for her.

She spent the afternoon learning how thoroughly. The stilt-village pilots who ran the salt-channels in flat-bottomed boats — her one chance at reaching the floodmark arch two worlds over by water — heard *the courier who argued with the Watch* before she finished asking, and found reasons. Tide's wrong, can't risk it. Boat's spoke for. The word had crossed the flats faster than she had, the same way the sabotage story crossed worlds, and it had her labeled now: not a saboteur, nothing so grand — a *jinx,* a woman who'd stood on a thanksgiving bank and told them their save was an accident. On Anneth, where the sea had just proved that the careful were fools and the certain were prophets, a person who insisted on *complicated* was not someone you put in your boat after the tide crossed the bells.

It was a quiet thing to be shut out of a place. She walked the boardwalks asking, and the hollow knock of her own boots on the wet planks was too loud, and the pilots did not raise a voice against her; they only got busy as she came up — a line to coil, a net to see to, a skiff to step down into, a few feet of black water let to widen between hull and wharf before she could get her tally out of her cuff. No one was cruel. The water just kept appearing between her and the boats, opened by men who would not meet her eye, and she understood she was being closed out the way a careful crew closes its margins against bad weather. She was the draft under the door now. They were only shutting it.

She found her way out the way couriers always found their way out, which was to stop being right and start being useful.

There was a salt-trader named Hesk loading a flat-boat at the low wharf for a run down the channels to the floodmark arch at Coll — a man with a bad shoulder, a green crew, and a manifest that did not match what was going into the hold — Lena saw it in one pass. Not contraband. Just the ordinary grease, an extra two tonnes of salt logged as one so the gate-duty at Coll came in lower, everybody's secret, the loose bolt nobody kicked. She didn't threaten him with it. She just stood at his elbow while his green crew stacked the load wrong, and said, mildly, that he'd lose the top third to the channel-chop stacked like that, and showed the nearest boy how to cross-tie it the way she'd have tied a hold for heavy weather, and read Hesk's salt-marsh maps over his shoulder and found the two channels he'd marked passable that the dry-bell flood would have silted, and said so, and was right, and let him see her be right about something that fed him instead of something that frightened him.

He gave her the passage for a day's work down the channels and a promise to tie his loads herself the whole way. He did not ask what she'd done to make the flats hate her. Salt-traders, like couriers, had a professional disinterest in other people's reasons. As they poled out through the dropping light, the gate-arch stood black and barred a mile across the mud behind them, and the painted board faced the empty causeway, keeping forty people alive with a story Lena was nearly sure was false.

That was the part she couldn't put down, poling down the salt-channels in the cold with a stranger's badly-loaded boat. Not that they'd thrown her off. That she wasn't sure anymore that they should have listened.

She had been so clean about it, world after world. *The story is wrong. The failures are uneven, the seams are local, there's no hand at the footings.* She still believed every piece of it. But she'd stood on a bank where the wrong story caught forty people before the water did, and offered them, in exchange, a truth that caught no one — and heard how it sounded coming out of her. Like a draft under a door.

The man on the bank had asked her the one question she couldn't answer — *what would you have them do?* — and she'd had nothing a frightened town could organize around. She'd said it anyway, and lost the gate.

She tied Hesk's loads at the first channel-bend — hauling the hemp taut over two tonnes of salt logged as one, knuckles scraping the cold canvas, snugging a lie about weight down tight so a man could shave his duty at Coll. It was the work that had bought her a seat after the truth left her on the mud: not the honest thing, the *useful* one. She tucked the knot home anyway, clean and hard. The knots, at least, were true; they'd hold at Coll no matter what story anyone told about the tides, and right now they were the only true thing she had a hand on. It helped, a little. It was the only thing that did. She poled north down the dark water doubting, for the first time since Vesper, not the story — but herself.
