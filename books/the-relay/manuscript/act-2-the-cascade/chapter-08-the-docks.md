# Chapter 8 — The Docks

The crowd on the Drevin docks was being patient, and that was how Lena knew it was going to be bad.

Angry crowds were loud and survivable; she'd walked through a dozen. They shouted themselves hoarse and tired and went to find supper. It was the quiet ones that hurt people — the ones that had stopped arguing and started agreeing, where the noise dropped because the deciding was done and all that remained was the doing, and nobody wanted to be heard saying the thing out loud before it happened. This crowd had gone quiet. It stood in a loose ring three deep around the impound pen at the foot of Pier Four, and it watched, and it waited, with the slow terrible patience of people who had talked themselves all the way to the edge of a thing and were only working up to the step.

Drevin was a raft-city; it floated, and it answered. When two hundred people shifted their weight the same way at the same time — leaning, without meaning to, toward the pen — the whole lashed deck took it up: a long low groan running out through the mooring cables into the black water, the pier tilting maybe an inch under Lena's boots before it settled back. The market moved when the crowd moved. Even the floor was working up to the step.

Lena had come to Drevin to find a pilot. She'd walked into this instead.

Drevin was a drift market — the biggest on the rim, a raft-city of lashed barges and gantry-cranes and chandlers' hulks strung in the slack between three gates, where the slow-way traders met to swap what the dark routes still carried. It ran on its own time and its own law, which was mostly *don't,* and it had been, the last time Lena passed through with a full hold, the freest place she knew: a town with no owner, where a Halen salt-man and a Vesper seed-broker and a canyon ear-reader would haggle over the same crate and call each other friend by the second cup. She had liked it the way you like a thing you assume will always be there.

It had a committee now. That was the first wrong thing she'd seen coming in — a *Drevin Harbor Committee,* with armbands, which was like finding a uniform on a stray cat. And the committee had a doctrine, the same doctrine eating every world she crossed, and the doctrine had told it that the cutters working the gates came from the separatist worlds out past the dark, and that Halen, which had said the loudest the longest that the old order was finished, was the separatist world. So the committee had seized the Halen barge at Pier Four, impounded its cargo against "investigation," and penned its crew — eight of them — behind a chain-link gate to wait for an authority that did not exist anymore to come and decide their case.

And the crowd had gathered to help the authority make up its mind.

---

She got the shape of it fast, because reading a dock was the one thing she could still do when everything else had stopped working.

The committee men didn't want this. That was the worst part, the part that made it dangerous instead of merely cruel: the four armbands at the pen were grey-faced and out of their depth, a chandler and a crane-boss and two others who'd taken the armband to feel less helpless and now stood guarding eight frightened traders from a town they'd drunk with last season, not sure how they'd become the wall and less sure they'd hold. One kept saying *there's a process,* to no one, the way people say it when process is the only wall they have left. There was no process. There was a chain-link gate, eight people behind it, two hundred in front of it, and a story everyone had agreed on that said the eight were the reason their children were hungry.

The Halen crew knew exactly what they were. Lena could see it in how they'd arranged themselves — the older ones to the front of the pen, the two youngest pushed to the back behind a stack of their own seized crates, a man with a grey plait holding the chain-link not to shake it but to keep his hands where the crowd could see them empty. People who had done this calculation before, or whose parents had. People being careful not to be a spark.

A woman at the front of the crowd said, conversationally, "They were sounding the gate. Everyone saw. Sounding it for where to cut." A man beside her said the Halen barge had come in heavy and left light three times this season, which was true of every barge on the rim and meant nothing and now meant everything. Someone at the back said Varik would have held the Halen barges a month ago — held the lot till the cutters were named — and a few heads nodded at the name the way you nod at scripture, at a man none of them had met whose sentences had reached the dock before he had. Nobody was shouting. They were assembling the permission, brick by brick, passing each other the bricks.

Lena stood at the back with her pack on and did the arithmetic she hated. She was one woman. She could not move two hundred people, could not out-shout a doctrine, could not produce the authority everyone was pretending to wait for. She had walked away from a crowd before — at Anneth, off the bank, down the salt-channels — and told herself walking away was what a courier did, carried herself out and lived to carry the next thing. She could do it here. The north berths were two piers over. She could find her pilot and be gone before the quiet crowd took its step, and never know how it ended, which was its own kind of mercy, the courier's mercy, the mercy of the full hold and the down-turned head.

She got about as far as turning around.

Then a child behind the seized crates — one of the two young ones, not a child really, fifteen maybe, but small with it — caught her eye through the chain-link by accident, and did the thing the Halen crew were all so carefully not doing: looked at her, openly, the way you look at the one stranger in a closing room who doesn't have an armband and isn't holding a brick. Just looked. Not even pleading. Checking, the way you check a door you already know is locked.

Lena turned back around.

---

She didn't have a plan. She had a habit, which was better, because plans showed on your face and habits didn't.

The habit was: when you can't move the crowd, move the freight. She'd spent her life knowing that the way out of a stuck dock was never through the people; it was through the paperwork and the side-gate and the thing everyone had stopped looking at. So she stopped seeing two hundred people working up to a horror and started seeing a badly run impound — and badly run impounds, like badly loaded boats, she could fix.

The pen had a manifest. Of course it did; the committee men needed to feel like a process, and a process meant a clipboard, and the clipboard hung on a nail by the gate where the chandler kept touching it. Lena walked up to it the way she'd walked up to ten thousand wickets — bored, entitled, already reaching — and started reading it aloud before anyone decided whether she was allowed to.

"Who's carrying the consignees on the seized stock?" she said, to the chandler, in the flat irritated voice of an inspector behind schedule. "You've got perishable here logged to a live destination. That's not yours to hold." She tapped the clipboard. "Two of these are bonded transit — Halen registry, Coll consignee. You seize bonded transit on a separatist flag, you've just made Coll a party, and Coll's gate is the only one feeding this market. You want the committee's name on the notice that closed Drevin's last live route?"

None of it was quite true. Some of it was nearly true. All of it was said in the right voice, which was the only part that mattered — the voice of someone for whom this was paperwork, not a pogrom, who had clearly done this a hundred times and was annoyed to be doing it again. The chandler, who wanted more than anything in the world to be doing paperwork instead of being a wall, grabbed the offered rope with both hands.

"Which two," he said.

"These two." She pointed at the manifest, then through the chain-link — not at the front ranks, the grey-plait elders the crowd had its eye on, but at the back, at the two young ones behind the crates, because the young ones were the spark and the young ones were the ones nobody would miss off a manifest in the next four minutes. "Bonded transit travels with its cargo. They're not crew, they're *consignment escort* —" a clause as real as the eighth schedule and as dead, "— and you're not authorized to detain consignment escort, you're authorized to *forward* it. Forward it and the liability's mine. Sign here, here, and I take them off your count."

"That's—" The crane-boss had come over, scenting wrongness. "That's not how—"

"It's exactly how," Lena said, and put the courier's whole weight into it, the bored certainty that had gotten her through a thousand gates — a clean story a frightened man could pick up — and was now using to steal two children off a kill-list. "You're not releasing them. You're *forwarding bonded transit* to keep the Coll route clean. You'll have the elders for your investigation. You'll have the cargo. You'll have everything your process wants. You just won't have two kids in the pen for the committee to answer for when someone upstream finally turns the lights on." She looked the crane-boss in the eye. "And someone will. Doesn't it feel like someone will?"

It didn't. Nobody upstream was coming, and some part of the crane-boss knew it. But he wanted it to be true the way the chandler wanted the clipboard to be true, and Lena had handed him a way to do a small correct thing inside a large terrible one, and frightened decent people will reach for that every time. He looked at the two young ones. He looked at the quiet crowd, which had not yet noticed anything because nothing loud had happened. He took the stylus and signed where she pointed.

And there it was, the thing she'd carry out along with the two lives: she'd moved him the way the doctrine moved everyone — a clean fiction a frightened man could pick up and put to use. The same shape as every story she'd crossed seven worlds hating. She had just saved two children with it. She set the contradiction down somewhere she couldn't look at it yet.

It took ninety seconds to walk them out the side-gate. Lena talked the whole way, briskly, about routing and bond and nothing, her hand light on the nearer one's shoulder, both of them with their heads down doing the thing she'd asked in a few murmured words — *don't run, don't look.* The crowd parted for an inspector forwarding consignment, because it had not been told that this was the thing to stop, and a crowd assembling its permission could only see the bricks it had already agreed on. Then they were in the gap between two chandlers' hulks, out of the ring, and the older of the two — the girl who'd looked at her — was shaking so hard she had to lean on a bollard, and the boy was saying something fast in a tongue Lena didn't have, the same word over and over, and it didn't matter what the word was.

"My uncle's in there," the girl said, in trade-speech, finding it. "My uncle. The plait. You only — there's six. You only—"

"I know," Lena said.

"You could've—"

"I couldn't." It came out harder than she meant, because it was the truth and the truth had teeth today. "Two was what I had. Two is what I got. Go down to Pier Two, find the Coll boats, say *consignment escort* and show them no papers and most of them won't ask. Don't come back for the barge. Don't come back for the plait." The girl's face broke and Lena held her shoulder hard enough to hurt, because gentleness was a luxury and there wasn't time to buy any. "He pushed you to the back so you'd be the ones that got out. Don't make that a waste. *Go.*"

They went. She didn't watch them all the way; watching was a luxury too. Once, looking back despite herself, she found the grey-plait uncle at the chain-link — he'd seen the two of them clear the ring, and he pressed two fingers flat to the wire for a moment, toward the gap they'd gone through, the only thanks a careful man could give with two hundred people reading his hands; then he set them back at his sides, empty, where the crowd could see them. The other five stood close around him, faces turned the way faces turn from weather. She heard the crowd's note change behind her, the patience finally tipping, as she walked away up the pier — the low collective intake, the first loud thing — and she made herself keep walking, into the market, away, carrying two out of eight and the full weight of the six like a debt logged to her tally that she would never be able to pay.

---

She found Nadi an hour later, two piers over, loading a stub-winged rim-hauler in the lamplight with the particular violence of someone who'd heard the docks go off and decided it was not her barge and not her business.

Lena had been trying to reach Nadi since Sennen. Since before Sennen — since the slow way began, when she'd asked every pilot on the route who they'd want at the stick for a run around a dark gate, and they'd all said the same name and the same thing after it: *Nadi. If you can get her. You can't get her; she's out on the drift.* The best rim pilot any of them knew, off working the markets where the money was, while the amateurs ferried the desperate north. Lena had carried the name across seven worlds like a coin she was saving. Here, finally, was the woman it bought.

She was short and wide and furious-handed, maybe fifty, with a burn-scar up one forearm and a way of throwing crates that suggested they'd insulted her. She didn't stop loading when Lena came up. She'd clocked her in one glance — pack, dead scanner, the look — and gone back to work, which was its own answer, but Lena had not crossed seven worlds to take a shoulder for an answer.

"You're Nadi. I need a pilot north. The rim, around the Vesper dark, to the Tarn approach or as close as anything still runs. Everyone says you're the only one who'd make it."

"Everyone's right." Nadi swung a crate, tied it, didn't look up. "No."

"I can pay—"

"You can't, or you'd have led with the number." She straightened, finally, and looked at Lena with eyes that had done a great deal of arithmetic and trusted none of it to other people. "Let me save us both the dance. You're the courier from Vesper, chasing a brother up north, asking the wrong questions at every dock between here and the dark. Word travels. You argued with the Anneth Watch — bad for business, by the way, you're a little famous for it. And you want me to burn a full tank and a month of my life hauling one woman and her questions up a rim that's *dying,* toward a gate that's *dark,* for a fare you keep almost mentioning." She picked up the next crate. "No."

"And courier." She paused with the crate against her hip. "I heard you pulled two out of that pen just now."

Lena said nothing.

"Not six." Not a question. "Two's more than anyone else on this dock managed today. Doesn't move me. Might be the whole reason it doesn't." She stacked the crate. "I don't fly people who can't walk past a fire. They cost too much fuel."

"People are dying up there too. The settlements past Vesper—"

"People are dying *here.*" She set the next crate down — not the throw from before; she put it down, which was how Lena knew she'd landed something. It was stenciled along the side in cracked paint, *RELIEF GRAIN — SUMN DEPOT,* the lettering older than the doctrine, the depot it named gone dark a season ago. Nadi stood it upright and didn't reach for the one behind it. "That noise an hour ago. That was this town deciding the Halen barge is why its kids are thin."

A boy on the winch called down — were they still loading the Coll run, the tide was on the turn — and Nadi said *all of it, fuel chit's in my coat,* without looking up, and the plain fact of it, a circuit still running in the middle of all this, sat there louder than anything she could have argued.

"You want to know why the kids are thin." She toed the relief-grain crate. "Gate at Sumn went dark. This stopped coming. Used to arrive free, on a schedule, to a depot that isn't there anymore. Now it comes when *I* come, crate by crate, sold to whoever can pay, so I can buy the fuel to fly the next load. That's the thread holding the rim. Me. Not the committee, not the Watch, not your questions." She hauled the next crate up and tied it off, the work going on under the words the way it always did with her. "I run a circuit. Drevin, Coll, the wet markets, back. Feeds maybe four thousand who'd be eating each other by spring if I stopped. And you want me to drop it and fly you north, so you can stand on one more dock and tell one more frightened town that it's *complicated.*"

"It is complicated," Lena said. "The thing eating the gates isn't sabotage. I've watched it across—"

"I don't care." Nadi said it without heat, which was worse than heat. "I genuinely do not care if it's saboteurs or bad luck or the gods gone to sleep. You know what's the same in every story? People still have to eat tomorrow. Sabotage, eat tomorrow. Bad luck, eat tomorrow. The whole network's quietly dying of nobody-minding-it — eat tomorrow. Your truth doesn't put one bowl down. His does." She didn't say *Varik.* She didn't have to; he was on the rim now too, a name in every mouth. "At least the cutters get a convoy moving while they're wrong. What do *you* get moving?"

It landed exactly where she'd aimed it. Lena thought of Anneth, offering a town a true thing that caught no one. Thought of two children walking down to Pier Two and six people she'd left behind a chain-link gate because two was what she had.

"You've flown the rim around the dark gates," Lena said, changing tack, because Nadi was right and being right was a wall and she'd learned today to go around walls. "All of it. For years. You've seen which routes died and when and in what order. You know the shape of it better than anyone alive — better than the committee, better than Varik, better than the feeds. You're not flying it for a theory. You're flying it because it's true and it feeds people. That's exactly why what you've seen is worth more than what any of them are guessing."

For just a moment — half a heartbeat, gone before Lena could be sure of it — something flickered behind Nadi's arithmetic. Not agreement. Recognition, maybe, of her own logbook described back to her as a thing that mattered. Her thumb paused on the crate-latch, grease-scarred, not quite pressing it home; she didn't turn around, but her shoulders set against the weight of a thing she'd watched happen and never written down. She *had* seen the shape. She knew it in her hands and her fuel-math — which gates had gone in which order, and that it didn't run like a cord being cut; it ran like an engine starving, the rim dropping link by link the way a feed-line dies when the oil stops coming, slow and then all at once. She had no word for it and wanted none, because a word was a theory and a theory didn't fly.

"What I've seen," Nadi said, "is a circuit getting shorter every season, and a lot of people who'll starve while clever folks decide whose fault it is." She picked the crate back up. The moment was gone; she'd put it down with the cargo. "I'm not your pilot. Try the amateurs at Pier Six — they'll take your coin and maybe get you killed, but it's a free market. And courier—" she nodded, almost not unkind, the way you'd nod at someone you respected and would not help, "—stop arguing with frightened people on docks. You're going to get the next two killed instead of clear."

She went back to her loading and did not look up again.

Lena stood in the lamplight between the piers, two children saved and six lost behind her, the one pilot worth crossing seven worlds for turned solid against her, and felt the thing arrive that hadn't been there at Anneth. At Anneth she'd doubted herself. Here, with the crowd's noise still in her ears and Nadi's arithmetic stacked neat and unanswerable in front of her, she stopped doubting and got angry — a hard clean fury with no door to go out of, at the doctrine that turned hungry people into a wall, at the kind practical pilots who'd let a barge crew hang rather than lose a fare, at a network that had quietly arranged itself so that the only people getting anything *done* were the ones doing the wrong thing for the wrong reason, and the only thing a true word bought you was a closed gate and a long way around.

She went to Pier Six and bought passage off Drevin from an amateur with a cracked heater, north, toward the next live route, carrying her anger with her like a thing she'd declared at the wicket and would owe duty on at the far end. It was, she'd find, a heavier cargo than fear. It kept better, too.
