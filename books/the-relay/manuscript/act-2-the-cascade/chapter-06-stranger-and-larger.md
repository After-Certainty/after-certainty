# Chapter 6 — Stranger and Larger

On Maween you had to declare your rumors.

Lena stood at the inbound wicket with her pack on the counter and a clerk on the far side reading her a list of dutiable goods in the flat voice of a man who had read it ten thousand times and stopped hearing the strange parts. Spirits. Living stock. Refined metal. *Hearsay material affecting the movement of persons or freight* — a clause three hundred years old, written when a false tide-table could sink a fleet, never once repealed, because nobody repeals the law that has stopped mattering; they just stop kicking it.

"Anything to declare under the eighth schedule," the clerk said.

A season ago she'd have laughed and waved it off and he'd have stamped her through, because the eighth schedule was the joke couriers told at the back of the line — *mind you don't smuggle a secret.* But the line behind her was forty people deep and not laughing, and the clerk's stylus was waiting, and she found she could not quite say *no.* Because she was carrying something. She had been carrying it across four worlds, and it weighed more every time someone handed her a heavier version of it.

"There's a story going around," she said. "About why the gates are closing."

The clerk looked up for the first time. "Sabotage," he said. Not a question.

"That's the one."

He made a mark in a box, the way he'd have marked a crate of spirits. "Two-claim duty or a statement of source. You can pay or you can tell me who you had it from."

"I had it from everyone," Lena said. "That's the part I'd pay to understand."

The clerk did not find this funny either. He charged her the two-claim, stamped a chit, and told her the duty wasn't on the rumor; it was on *carrying* it across a border without saying so. She paid it — a real coin, off her thinning roll — and walked through onto Maween proper with a receipt in her cuff that said, in effect, that she had imported one unit of fear and declared it like an honest woman. It was the most accurate document anyone had given her since Vesper.

---

She had not meant to be on Maween at all.

The plan, such as a plan survived contact with the dark, had been simple after Osko set her down: one more junction, one more careful hop, and then the Tarn approach — the last cold leg around the dead gate, the one Harl had drawn for her in grease pencil a lifetime ago. She had been close enough to count it in days. Then the junction past Osko's run went the way they all went now: a notice, no author, a queue of stranded hulls, a board that listed sailings the way a gravestone lists virtues. The approach to the Tarn was on the far side of it, and the far side of it was dark.

So she went around the dark. And the way around the dark went around another dark, and that one around a third, until *around* had stopped being a detour and become the whole shape of the journey — a woman crabbing sideways across the network because every door that pointed straight at her brother had quietly stopped being a door. She was not traveling toward the Tarn anymore. She was traveling toward the next place that was still letting people in, and hoping its far wall had a gap that pointed north.

Maween was one of those. It let people in. It charged them for the privilege of admitting they were afraid, and it let them in.

What kept her moving now was a name. At the last three crossings she'd heard it the way the shipwrecked hear a rumor of dry land: *Coreth.* A gate-town out past the canyon world that still had a live arch, the slow folk said — one of the last on this whole quiet rim still pointing north, toward the Tarn approach, toward Arin. Nobody could swear it was open; nobody could swear anything was open anymore. But it was a door with a direction, and the way to it ran the only road left: through the Tessom float, onto a slow-hauler, up the canyon, and over the top to Coreth before whatever was eating the gate-towns reached it too. She had a route again — which meant, for the first time since Vesper, she had something left to lose.

What she could not get used to — what kept catching her sideways, the way a missing step catches you in the dark — was that the story always got there first.

She would cross into a world that had lost a gate three days ago, a world where she'd expected to be the one with news, and find the news already worn smooth from handling. *Sabotage. Separatists. They're cutting the threads.* The exact words, world to world, in mouths that had never met. At Vesper the rumor had been a thing taking shape on a dock, soft and unfinished, *probably, provisional, not syncing.* By Maween it arrived fully built, with the doubt sanded off and somebody's certainty varnished over the top, and the people repeating it did not say *they think* or *I heard.* They said *everyone knows.* She had watched freight move her whole life and never seen anything move like this — no hull, no manifest, no fuel, faster than the failures it claimed to explain, arriving on worlds the gates could no longer reach.

She took it in and kept moving, the way she took in everything she couldn't yet explain — though lately the things she carried had begun to rattle when she walked.

---

Tessom was where the size of it started to get in.

It was a float-market, strung in the slack water between two gates where the pull of neither quite reached — a reef of tethered stalls and net-bags and slow-turning crates hung in air that had forgotten which way was down. Lena came through the lock still walking — boots reaching for a floor that had quietly stopped being there — and her own momentum threw her out into three lurching strides of nothing, heels kicking at a void that gave them no purchase, her stomach turning over at the sudden, total absence of down. A dockhand caught her ankle without looking and reeled her in to a guide-line the way you'd land a fish you'd landed a thousand times. "First float?" he said, already past her. It wasn't. She'd run cargo through Tessom twice. She'd just never *looked* at it, because both times she'd had a full hold and a clock and a buyer, and a courier with a clock sees doors, not rooms.

Now she had nothing but time she couldn't spend, and she looked.

A vendor came to her across the open air. Not floating — *walking,* a slow rolling stride from tether to tether, hands and feet finding lines Lena couldn't see until they took weight, a whole economy of small holds learned so deep into the body that the woman could carry a tray of glass bulbs through emptiness without spilling the light in them. She walked the air the way Pell had walked his fuel quarter, the way Osko had read a gauge by its sound — decades laid into the hands, a competence so complete it had stopped looking like effort and started looking like a law of the place. She sold Lena a bulb of something warm to drink and a twist of dried fruit and asked, walking backward now along a line Lena would have sworn was empty, where she was bound.

"North," Lena said. "Eventually. The Tarn, if there's still a way to it."

The vendor's face did the thing faces did now at the word *Tarn* — the small recalculation, the pity filed before it could become rude. "Long way to be going sideways," she said, and walked off across the nothing to the next tether, light steady in her tray, and left Lena hanging on a guide-line with a warm bulb in her hands and the first true edge of it opening under her: that this — the woman, the reef of stalls, the borrowed gravity, the language of holds nobody had taught her and everybody here knew — this was *one market,* in *one slack stretch,* between *two* of a thousand gates, and she had run cargo through it twice and seen a door.

That was the thought that frightened her, hanging there with the light turning slow around her: not that the thing was vast. That it was vast and nobody was standing where she was standing. There was no deck high enough. A thousand worlds, seven centuries deep, grown wonderful and unrecognizable to each other — and the only people who crossed between them did it the way she always had, hold full, head down, seeing the next door. If it was coming apart, it was coming apart in a hundred places at once in front of a hundred thousand people who could each see exactly one of them.

She drank the warm thing. It tasted of chicory and something resinous she had no name for. She bought a second one she couldn't afford, to keep the vendor walking a little longer, and watched her work, and was — for the length of one bulb of warm drink in borrowed air — purely glad of the size of it, before the gladness turned back into the fear, which it did, because lately the two arrived in the same crate.

---

She crossed out of Tessom on a slow-hauler with a pilot who slept in four-hour shifts and a hold full of people going sideways like her, and somewhere in that leg she gave back another margin she would not get to keep.

The hauler ran lean, and it told the nose first: cabbage water, wet travel-wool, and under both the sweetish oily reek of a coolant seep somewhere in the hull — the kind every soul aboard had learned to stop smelling, because the mend wanted a part three dark gates away and a requisition no live office would ever sign, and there was nothing to do with a leak you couldn't fix but breathe it and trust it was someone else's watch. Heat was metered; you bought your warmth by the watch and slept in your coat through the watches you couldn't. There was a tank of grey wash-water with a hand-pump and a sign that said, in the patient lettering of a man who had given up being obeyed, *one cup, not the line.* Lena watched a mother of two read the sign and use one cup for all three of them, the children first, herself last and barely, and she thought of Harl's chalk line on the gauge, the mark that only went down, and understood she was watching the same line now drawn across four hundred strangers. Everyone on the hauler was running down a glass. Nobody was topping anyone up. The rim gave back nothing you hadn't brought, and the worlds had stopped sending anything around, and so every soul aboard was spending a store that had no resupply behind it and pretending, the way you had to, that the resupply would come.

Down the bench, two men had been trading the whole leg — a dented pressure-valve with good thread for a half-spent tube of sealing-paste — the haggling slow and courteous and deadly serious, because neither would see a chandler again this season and the valve might be the difference between a warm berth and a cold one. Nobody was making anything anymore. They were only moving the last of it around, valve for paste, paste for passage, the same finite pile changing hands until the day it wouldn't.

She gave the mother her own cup ration for the day and told her it was spare, which was a small lie and the easy kind, the kind that kept a line moving. The woman took it because she had two children and no room for pride. They didn't talk after. There was nothing to say that the wash-water hadn't already said.

---

It was on the next world that Vesper came back to find her.

She'd put in at a depot — a hard, ugly transfer station clamped to a world she never even learned the name of, all freight and no welcome — to wait out a berth, and she'd gone walking the holds the way couriers do, reading the stranded cargo because reading cargo was the nearest thing she had to reading the news. Pallets to nowhere. Whole bays of it. Relief stock and trade stock and someone's furniture, all of it logged to gates that no longer answered, all of it sitting in the dark with a destination that had become a fiction.

And on a low shelf, under a tarp gone stiff with cold, she found seed.

Not Corin's. She knew that before she finished the thought — Corin's crate was a thousand cold miles behind her, on the floor of a Vesper holding bay, logged to her tally and going nowhere, the one consignment in her whole career she had walked away from and left on the ground. This was other seed, somebody else's, bagged and bound, the manifest tucked into a cracked sleeve on the crate's end. *For the lower settlements,* it said, *Vesper transit, onward by surface* — and over that, the paper bruised purple where a routing desk had stamped it again and again with no hand anywhere near it. *Route suspended.* A day later, across the first mark: *awaiting clearance.* A week after that: *inventory validated* — the network reaching out through the dark to confirm, dutifully, on a schedule, that the seed was precisely where it had been left to die. No person had touched the crate in months. Something still came by every few days to find it present, and recorded that it was present, and went away satisfied. The same settlements. The hungry ones out past the dead gate, the ones the fast routes fed because the slow ones couldn't, the ones Corin's blue-ticket buyer had been gambling on when he second-lined a seed crate to make a margin in the dark.

She stood in front of it a long time. It had not made it across either. None of it had. The fast seed and the smuggled seed and the honest seed were all in the same condition now, which was *stopped,* and the settlements out past Vesper were going into a cold season on whatever they'd had in store, while three depots' worth of the exact thing they needed sat under stiff tarps on worlds the seed could no longer reach.

She had told herself, walking off Corin's crate onto the boat stairs at Vesper, that the contraband under it was a symptom and not a clue — that the lie in the lining wasn't villainy, just a man doing the small wrong thing the scarcity made tempting, the way everybody kicked the loose bolt and agreed not to mention it. She still believed that. What she hadn't let herself follow, then, was the rest of the sentence: that when the line finally stopped, all the little workarounds stopped with it, the honest and the grubby alike, and the people at the far end of every one of them — the ones who'd never see the lining or the blue ticket, who'd only ever waited for the seed — paid for the stop without ever knowing what they were paying. She was the courier of record on a crate of it. So was somebody, on every pallet in this bay.

She put the tarp back the way she'd found it, square at the corners, the way you left a thing you couldn't help. It was a stupid courtesy. She did it anyway.

---

The last world before she ran out of north was the one that read by ear.

It was a canyon world — a long cut of a place, settlements terraced down sheer rock, the gate-town pooled in the bottom where the light only reached at midday. Its people had been born to the echo and built everything else around it. They spoke, and then they *listened,* heads tipped, reading the way the words came back off the far wall the way Lena read a face — and what came back told them the things she got from eyes: how far, how many, what had changed since morning. A child found her in the dark of an underpass by clicking her tongue at the stone and reading Lena's shape out of the return, and was unbothered, where a seeing child would have startled, because to her Lena had been visible the whole time, lit by the sound she made just by being a body in the world.

Lena hired a guide there — an old woman who knew which of the upper terraces still had a live gate behind them and which were only saying so — and paid her to climb toward the high pass north, toward Coreth, the live arch Lena had been crabbing a fortnight to reach. They climbed in the cold for half a day, and the woman talked the whole way, less to Lena than to the canyon, throwing her voice ahead at the rock and listening to how the world answered, the way Lena would have glanced down a corridor for exits. Near the top she stopped, and tipped her head, and went still in a way Lena had learned to respect on the slow way, because stillness in a competent person was information.

"Quiet up there," the guide said.

"Is that bad?"

"It's wrong." She clicked again, listened. "Coreth's a day north of here." Lena's chest did something at the name said plainly aloud, by someone who could measure the distance to it. "You stand where I'm standing, you hear it — the haul-yards, the pumps, the river they run for cooling. It comes back up the canyon. I've heard it my whole life." She lowered her hand. "I can't hear it. The wall's giving me back nothing where the town should be." She snapped her tongue once more, hard, a sound Lena felt in her own teeth, and tipped her head north. Lena listened the way she'd listened all day, uselessly — to her it was only the dark. But she caught this much: the snap came back off the near rock, came back fainter off the far rock, and then went out past them toward the town and simply did not come home. Not quiet. Swallowed. Where seven centuries of haul-yards and pumps and cooling-river had given the stone something to throw back, the canyon now returned nothing at all, as though the space the town had filled had been hollowed deeper, or packed with felt, or unmade.

She would not take Lena further. Not for fear, she said — she'd been north a hundred times — but because a world that had gone quiet where it should be loud was a world she did not yet have the shape of, and she was too old to walk into a place she couldn't hear. She named Lena a price for the half-day, fair, and took it, and stood a moment longer reading the silence to the north like a woman reading a letter she didn't like.

"Strange times," she said, "when the dark's not the worst of it." She clicked, listened, shook her head at whatever came back. "I can hear a thing coming for three days. Whatever's taking the gate-towns, I can't hear it at all. It doesn't make a sound." She turned to go back down into the light. "Mind how you go, sideways woman. Some of us can tell when a place stops answering. Most of you can't, and you live in the places."

So that was Coreth. The last live arch on the rim, the door she'd spent a fortnight crabbing sideways to reach, gone quiet in the canyon's mouth like all the rest — and she had been climbing toward it for days, spending coin and water and warmth and what was left of her margin on a town that had stopped answering before she ever set out. Lena stood at the top of the cold canyon and let herself feel it, fully and without arguing it down: the duty-receipt in her cuff that said she'd imported one unit of fear honestly, the stranger's seed three worlds back that would never reach the mouths it was bagged for, the distance to her brother grown a fortnight larger while she crossed toward a door that was no longer there.

When she asked the guide where a person went from here, the old woman clicked at the canyon a long moment before she answered, as if checking the question against the rock. Back down the rim, she said. There was a wet gate two worlds south — the busy crossings, the trade worlds, the ones still thick with people. The ones, Lena understood, that were thick now with the story too: where sabotage had stopped being a word at a wicket and started closing causeways and hauling traders off docks. To keep going north she would have to turn around and go back *into* it.

She went down into the light to find a hull. She had wanted, since Vesper, only to go forward — and the dark had answered by making forward run backward, through the worst of what frightened people did when no one would tell them the truth. Sideways had stopped being the delay between her and the Tarn. It had become the shape of the collapse itself, and every road left to her now ran straight through the middle of it.
