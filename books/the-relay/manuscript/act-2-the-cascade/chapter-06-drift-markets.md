# Chapter 06 — Drift Markets

The boarding corridor spat her into Drift proper—not a corridor, a market, walls of numbers pretending to be geography.

Lena's shoulders still remembered the transfer sling's grip—the way Drift docking liked to pretend you were cargo until your feet found deck. Her bag strap had burned a line into her palm. The manifest in her inner pocket smelled like Northhook tea and cheap soap. Couriers learned to carry their whole life in weight you could sprint with; Drift learned you to carry it through rooms that charged by the hour.

Lena's wrist ached where the fee desk had stamped her band. Story tax, jurisdiction adjacency, the clerk's nail catching skin on the second press because the machine was faster than careful. She had Dael's scrap folded against the receipt in her outer pouch and the ozone taste of a crossing still in her throat—sharp, metallic, the smell of a gate she'd passed through without passing through anything she could name.

Nadi had told her not to romanticize Northhook and walked away before Lena could ask what *Quick Margin* meant on the pilot roster. Commerce waited for nobody. She was learning that already.

The Drift Markets didn't orbit a star. They orbited the next line.

Arrivals. Departures. Exchange rates. Freight auctions. Entire fortunes rose and fell before breakfast, and nobody asked where you came from because the answer changed too often to matter. The air was warmer than Northhook, drier, threaded with fried dough and solvent and the ghost of crossings. Signs pointed toward fees, pilots, arguments that hadn't been invented yesterday.

Above the flow, freight crawlers crossed on magnetic rails—containers the size of apartment blocks sliding between stations without touching, held by fields Lena had never cared about until she needed one to hold *her* cargo. A pilot shouted at a loader that had drifted half a meter off its clamp. The loader answered with a whine. Magnetic freight wasn't spectacle here. It was Tuesday.

It was also her problem now in the way Arin was her problem—personal, immediate, too large to see whole. Her around-route manifest didn't ride those crawlers. Her body did, station to station, fee desk to housing arcade to auction bay, learning Drift through the wallet and the wrist. But the Drift leg existed because magnetic freight moved enough cargo between systems that pilots like Nadi could file fringe and long corridor and wait in the same afternoon. Without the rails and the auctions and the pilots who read walls instead of bulletins, Orinreach stayed eleven days away and Arin stayed behind a gate that wouldn't speak.

Lena watched a crawler pause mid-rail while a clerk below argued about clamp fees. The pause rippled—pilots checking slates, a woman with a child pulling her manifest case closer, the wall updating reroute density before anyone on the crawler had finished breathing. Scale through a body: her heart rate up, her bag strap biting, ozone still in her throat, a station the size of a city district hanging in nothing and calling itself commerce.

Lena stood in the flow with her bag and her folder and let the wall hit her.

**VESPER GATE — SUSPENDED — REROUTE DENSITY +14% — LONG-CORRIDOR FEES ADJUSTING**

Not a bulletin. A ledger row. Same font as freight futures and berth auctions and someone's joke about mirror-tech insurance. Vesper sat between a grain contract and a delay labeled *ADMINISTRATIVE* with no further explanation. Fear here wasn't red percentages on a thread wall. Fear was a surcharge column that moved while you blinked.

She pulled up Arin's corridor on her display—Orinreach Basin, the corridor he'd used for six years, the one that now read **WAIT** in administrative white. Around-route quotes flickered higher than they'd been at Northhook. Not impossible. Ugly. The kind of ugly that ate standing-up margin before you slept.

A Markets runner shouldered past with a crate of pamphlets. "Separatist briefing," she called to nobody. "Updated borders. Two for one before lunch."

Lena's hand moved before she decided. Wrong-border pamphlet from the Ribbon—she'd filed it in the folder because the map had lied politely, official ink on a line that didn't match any corridor she'd ever flown. She bought two from the runner without negotiating. Habit. Paper when systems got nervous.

The runner barely slowed. "You want the fee schedule too?"

"I've got a schedule."

"Everybody's got a schedule." The runner grinned without warmth. "Schedule's what they're charging, not what they owe you."

Lena unfolded one pamphlet while the crowd pushed past. The border line cut through Vesper approach space like a knife through a map that had never been a map—clean ink, confident serif, *JURISDICTION ADJACENT TRANSIT* on the back in the same yellow as the housing board she'd not seen yet. She refolded it into the folder next to Dael's scrap and kept walking.

A pilot board at corridor fork listed insults where other stations listed weather.

**FRINGE LIARS — LONG CORRIDOR COWARDS — VESPER WAIT CLUB**

Someone had added **STORY TAX ENTHUSIASTS** in chalk. Navigation by insult was a Drift specialty—pilots reading tone instead of bulletins, knowing who'd fly what before the wall admitted it. Lena read the board the way she read berth assignments: who was moving, who was stuck, who was paying to look brave.

She wasn't hiring a pilot yet. She was learning the room.

---

The housing arcade off corridor seven was where Drift put people who couldn't afford to stand still.

Temporary berths stacked in modules that had been temporary for a decade. Couriers, pilots between contracts, a woman with two children and a manifest case arguing with a vending machine about coolant credits. Families waiting on a gate that would reopen Tuesday until Tuesday became next month. Lena needed a place to unfold her ledger without a boarding line breathing on her neck.

The fee board hung at the arcade entrance like weather.

**DRIFT HOUSING — 6-HOUR BLOCK — BASE + STORY TAX + JURISDICTION ADJACENCY**

The numbers were worse than Dael's scrap. Not by much. Enough. Story tax highlighted in the same yellow the Northhook clerk had used. Jurisdiction adjacency—a phrase that meant someone somewhere had been scammed once and wrote policy like revenge. Lena had seen the line on manifests that included words like *mirror* or *reflective surface*. Arin's basin work used both. She didn't like how the board knew that without asking her name.

She paid six hours because standing still cost more than moving and she needed one hour to think.

The housing clerk stamped her band without looking up. "Arcade C. Third module. Don't block the coolant vent."

"I won't."

"Everybody says that." He tapped his screen. "Vesper's loud today. Fees'll jump again before dinner. Not my problem."

"Whose is it?"

He almost smiled. "Pilot's. Analyst's. Yours if you stand here asking."

She took the module key—a strip, not a key—and walked arcade C with the pamphlets under her arm and the folder heavy on her hip. Her wristband pulsed once, a reminder that six hours was a countdown, not a room.

Third module smelled like recycled air and someone else's spice tea. Small. Honest. A fold-down desk bolted to the wall. Through the narrow viewport, Drift stations crawled between each other like dice someone had thrown and forgotten to collect. A magnetic crawler slid past close enough that the module wall hummed—a low vibration in her teeth, freight moving at a scale that made her courier bag look like a purse.

Lena spread the pamphlet beside Dael's scrap.

The map was the same wrong border she'd filed at Northhook—the separatist jurisdiction line drawn clean through a corridor that belonged to nobody and everybody, the kind of lie that looked official because fear wanted a shape. On the pamphlet back, a fee table for "border-adjacent transit." On Dael's scrap, the same column under a different name. On the housing board, jurisdiction adjacency again—three faces of the same charge, dressed as geography, dressed as story, dressed as policy.

She was still staring when she noticed the mark.

Housing module seam, access panel, the place where temporary became permanent if you didn't check it. Dot and stroke. Same grammar as Northhook's berth wall, as Arin's basin photo in her folder, as the dock housing she'd walked past without asking because asking cost time.

Not graffiti. Not sabotage evidence. Someone saying *I was here* on a wall that wasn't screaming about separatists at all.

The collision sat in her chest—not three clues, one scene. Wrong border on cheap paper raising fees on the board. Fees moving before any bulletin explained why. A mark on housing that said checking continued while the Markets sold fear by the hour.

She photographed the seam. Added it to the folder she was trying not to treat like a religion.

Her display chimed. Route query results—Orinreach around, eleven days, two fee tiers, Drift leg first. **COMMITTED** sat in her ledger like a dare. She couldn't go back to **TRACKING** at Northhook without eating the fees she'd already paid forward. She couldn't un-see the pamphlet border lining up with the housing surcharge. She couldn't make the mark mean sabotage no matter how loud the wall got.

She opened messages.

Arin's last line still said *Travel safe.* Her outbound note still showed **DELIVERED TO ORINREACH BASIN**. No reply. She didn't refresh. Refreshing was a fee you paid in hope.

Through the viewport a family in the next module argued in whispers about whether to spend their last credits on a long-corridor quote or wait for Vesper. The mother wanted to wait. The father wanted to move. The child wanted breakfast. Lena had seen that argument in three ports this year with different faces and the same math—people trying to be responsible while the wall moved without asking permission.

She wasn't family. She was a courier with **COMMITTED** in her ledger and a brother who sent photos of seams instead of voice notes. She made tea on the sad burner and burned her tongue because hot was faster than careful.

The module wall hummed when a crawler passed—magnetic freight moving mass she couldn't see, shaking temporary housing the way storms shook Orinreach mirrors. Arin would be on a ladder somewhere in that weather, knees bent, hands busy, choosing marks over arguments. She didn't know that for sure. She knew it the way you knew your own sibling: not from proof, from pattern.

She pulled the blanket from the bunk and sat on the floor instead. Sometimes couriers slept horizontal. Sometimes they sat vertical until the numbers stopped swimming. Six hours on the wristband. Eleven days on the manifest. One brother on **WAIT**.

---

By afternoon she'd walked the auction bay twice without buying anything except more context.

Pilots argued with a wall that updated faster than apologies. A mirror-tech firm posted premium contracts for "certainty" in a font that deserved contempt. Vesper suspension scrolled on the news ribbon between grain futures and a divorce proceeding—someone's personal catastrophe sharing the feed with everyone's gate problem.

Lena searched Arin routes the way couriers searched—practical, petty, precise. Which corridors still cleared basin manifests. Which fees jumped when manifests included reflective language. Which pilots had **WAIT** on their boards and which had **FRINGE** in small letters that meant creative accounting.

A clerk at the freight window told a mirror-tech lawyer that reflective language on a manifest wasn't discrimination, it was *experience*. The lawyer said experience wasn't supposed to be a surcharge column. The clerk pointed at the wall where **JURISDICTION ADJACENCY** sat between grain futures and a divorce. "Experience is what the board charges. Take it up with Vesper."

Lena filed the exchange without writing it down. She'd heard the same fight at Northhook with different accents. Fees before explanations. Always.

**Quick Margin** appeared on a departure slate—Nadi's ship, slot pending, corridor not yet honest.

She didn't chase it. Chasing pilots in the Markets meant paying to stand near their impatience.

At the public lip, a woman in consensus bureau gray watched belief flows on a portable display. Jun. She'd seen his brief on the thread wall at Northhook—*What Silence Buys*, charts that made fear look like data. He stood at the edge of the crowd taking notes with the calm of someone who thought stories were cargo you could weigh.

Maybe they were. The wall was certainly weighing them.

Lena turned away before he turned toward her. She wasn't ready to be a dot on somebody's chart.

A vendor sold corridor noodles from a cart that hummed. Lena ate standing up—courier habit—and listened.

"Sabotage?" a pilot asked.

"Sure," another said. "I believe my fuel gauge."

The first pilot laughed like he'd heard something wise. Lena recognized Nadi's line from the boarding corridor, passed hand to hand until it became weather.

She wiped her mouth, filed the noodle receipt with the others, and walked back to housing module C before her six hours expired.

The mark on the seam hadn't moved. The pamphlet border still didn't match any map the Markets admitted to. The fee board at the arcade entrance had already ticked story tax up a point.

Outside the viewport, magnetic freight kept moving—containers she couldn't afford to ship alone, fields she depended on without understanding, the Drift's answer to distance that wasn't solved, only priced.

Lena unfolded the fold-down desk, opened the ledger, and wrote one line under **COMMITTED**:

*Can't go back. Can't stand still cheap.*

Somewhere behind her, Northhook kept humming. Somewhere ahead, Orinreach waited behind a gate that wouldn't speak and a brother who sent marks instead of answers.

She had a folder that wasn't on any wall. She had eleven days around if the fees didn't eat them first. She had a housing module that smelled like someone else's tea and a seam that someone had checked without asking the Markets' permission.

That would have to be orientation enough until the wall told her something she could afford to believe.

---

The auction bay at second bell was where Drift taught you to stop pretending distance was solved.

Freight crawlers crossed overhead on magnetic rails, close enough to throw shadows across the deck. Pilots shouted in dialects that shared vocabulary—fuel, wait, fringe, liar—but not grammar. A consensus runner posted Jun's updated brief on the public ribbon without reading it. **SEPARATIST CLUSTER** climbed in the same font as grain futures.

Lena leaned against a pillar and watched money become weather.

A mirror-tech lawyer argued with a clerk about reflective-surface insurance. The clerk said, "Vesper's loud," as if that explained premiums the way storms explained wet shoes. The lawyer said, "My client paid for certainty." The clerk laughed—the honest laugh of someone who worked where certainty was always spelled with an asterisk.

Near the departure slates, a woman in pamphlet-runner orange had planted herself beside a pilot checking fuel manifests. Lena recognized the border-believer type before she heard the words—kiosk posture, official paper fanned like a hand of cards, the particular confidence of someone who'd mistaken a line on a map for a wall that could keep bad things out.

"You want to fly blind?" the woman said. "Vesper didn't close for maintenance. It closed because somebody drew a line and somebody else respected it too late. My briefing gives you the line that matters."

The pilot didn't look up from his manifest. "My briefing gives me fuel burn and berth fees."

"Berth fees are cheaper than losing a corridor to people who don't recognize jurisdiction."

"Jurisdiction." The pilot finally looked at her. "Lady, I've been flying fringe since before your pamphlet had a second edition. The jurisdiction that matters is the one the clamp accepts."

"The clamp accepts fear." She tapped the pamphlet border—the same wrong line Lena had in her folder, drawn clean through a corridor that belonged to nobody. "This is prudence. Separatist activity in the mesh. You don't reroute around prudence. You pay adjacency and you fly honest."

"Honest." The pilot laughed—not cruel, tired. "Honest is what I pay when the board moves before the bulletin. Honest is story tax on my manifest because my return cargo mentions mirrors. Your prudence is a surcharge column dressed as geography."

"Geography protects people."

"Geography protects fee desks." He shouldered his manifest case. "You want to protect people, fix the gate. Until then, I'll believe my fuel gauge."

The border believer called after him—something about duty, about borders that held when administrators wouldn't. The pilot was already gone, swallowed by the bay's noise.

"You believe that?" Lena asked before she could stop herself.

The woman turned—pamphlet-runner orange, tired eyes, the sincerity of someone who'd repeated a sentence until it became a spine. "I believe gates close when jurisdictions fail. I believe people need lines they can see."

"I believe my wristband." Lena held up the stamped skin. "Your line raised my housing fee without asking my name."

"Then the fee's doing its job." No malice. Worse—conviction. "Prudence costs. You want cheap, fly blind and hope separatists are a podcast."

"I'm not flying blind. I'm flying around."

"Then you're already paying prudence." She offered a pamphlet. "At least know what you're paying for."

Lena didn't take it. "I know what I'm paying for. I don't know if it's true."

"Truth's not my department." The woman was already turning to the next pilot. "Safety is."

Lena stayed at the pillar. She didn't buy another pamphlet. She already had the lie in her folder and the surcharge on her wrist.

A magnetic crawler groaned overhead, shifting a container stack toward the Meridian auction block. Lena watched it the way she'd watched Lake Orin tourists—trying to understand scale through a body that only carried bags. Her manifest wasn't on that crawler. Her brother's basin freight wasn't either. But the rails that moved those containers were the same rails that made Drift leg possible, and Drift leg was the only reason **COMMITTED** didn't mean **STRANDED**.

Magnetic freight wasn't her problem the way Arin was her problem. It was her problem anyway.

Nadi crossed the bay with a coil of cable and grease on her jaw. She saw Lena and didn't slow.

"You still romanticizing housing?" Nadi asked.

"I'm romanticizing nothing."

"Good. Romanticizing costs extra." She shifted the cable. "*Quick Margin* lifts tomorrow if the board stops lying. Fringe viable. Long corridor for people who hate money. You hiring margin or collecting stamps?"

"Collecting paper."

"Paper's honest." Nadi's eyes flicked to the folder edge visible at Lena's hip. "Jun's at the field office if you want decimals with your fear. I file under fly."

"I met Jun."

"Then you know his cluster's pretty." Nadi almost grinned. "Pretty doesn't open gates."

She walked on before Lena could answer. Pilots didn't wait for answers. Couriers learned that or they paid berth fees for conversation.

---

Evening brought fried dough smell and a different kind of queue—housing arcade, six-hour blocks expiring, people arguing about coolant vents like vents were politics.

Lena's wristband pulsed twice on the walk back. Six hours gone. Six more if she wanted a desk instead of a boarding line floor.

The fee board had ticked again. Story tax up another point. Jurisdiction adjacency unchanged—the insult of a fee that didn't need to move because it already owned the sentence. The pamphlet runner at the arcade mouth sold updated separatist borders—same wrong line, new ink, two for one before dinner as if dinner mattered to jurisdiction.

Lena didn't buy more paper. She had enough lies in the folder.

She paid six hours again because the alternative was boarding line sleep, and boarding line sleep cost dignity on top of money.

The housing clerk recognized her band. "Back already."

"Still standing still."

"Standing still's a luxury." He stamped without sympathy. "Arcade C hasn't moved. Vent's still there. Don't block it."

"I heard you the first time."

"Everybody hears me." He tapped his screen. "Fees'll jump before breakfast. Not my problem."

"Whose is it?"

He almost smiled—the same almost-smile as this morning. "Yours if you're still here asking."

The renewal hurt differently than the first payment. First payment was orientation—buying time to think. Renewal was admission: she was stuck in Drift long enough for the Markets to count her twice. The numbers weren't catastrophic. They were cumulative—the kind of cumulative that turned thin margin into no margin while **DELIVERED TO ORINREACH BASIN** sat in her queue like a message that had arrived and not landed.

She did the math at the fold-down desk with the pamphlet open and Dael's scrap beside it.

First six hours: base, story tax, jurisdiction adjacency—already ugly.

Second six hours: story tax up one point, base unchanged, adjacency still grinning on the board like a bruise that liked its job.

Around-route quote from this morning: worse than yesterday.

If she renewed again tomorrow she'd be buying Drift time at Northhook berth prices without Northhook's lake. If she didn't renew she'd sleep in a boarding line and show up at the courier desk looking like someone who'd lost an argument with gravity.

She opened the ledger. **COMMITTED** stared back. Not a word you could negotiate with.

Under it she wrote the renewal numbers in her own hand—small, mean, precise:

*Second six hours: paid. Margin: thinner. Arin: still quiet.*

In module C she compared Dael's scrap to the board one more time—not because the numbers would change, because comparison was how couriers learned to distrust their own hope. Adjacency matched. Story tax matched. Around-leg projection worse than this morning.

She opened Arin's photo again. Basin dusk. Mark on seam. His hands somewhere out of frame doing work that didn't publish on a thread wall.

*Following the mark,* she'd written at Northhook.

The mark on her housing module rhymed with his—not proof of conspiracy, proof of attention. Someone checking while administrators practiced silence. Someone not waiting for Vesper to explain the week.

She couldn't tell that story at the auction bay. The bay wanted separatists because separatists fit on pamphlets.

She closed the folder, killed the display, and listened to Drift hum through the module wall—freight fields, distant arguments, the ozone ghost of crossings she hadn't finished paying for.

Committed meant you couldn't go back to tracking without eating what you'd paid forward.

Tomorrow she'd need a corridor decision. Nadi's fringe or long corridor emergency math or another day of housing surcharges while **DELIVERED** sat in her queue like a fact with fine print.

Tonight she had orientation—ugly, expensive, enough.

She set the alarm for boarding bell and slept with her bag against the bunk rail, compass patch warm against her shoulder, wristband pulsing once in the dark like a fee that hadn't finished with her yet—the way couriers slept when they'd finally stopped calling it tracking.

---

Morning came with boarding bell and fried dough from the arcade vending wall.

Lena walked the housing arcade one more time before the bay filled—not because she had credits to burn, because bodies remembered routes better than displays. The fee board had ticked overnight. Story tax again. Of course. The pamphlet runner at the mouth had fresh ink and the same wrong border. A magnetic crawler groaned past on the upper rail, shaking dust from the module seams.

In corridor seven dock housing—not her module, the public seam she'd photographed on the walk in—someone had added a second mark beside the first. Steadier hand. Same grammar.

She didn't photograph it yet. She had enough marks in the folder to prove checking continued. She needed a pilot decision more than she needed a third photo.

At the auction bay lip the border believer from last night argued with a different pilot—same pamphlet, same conviction, same tired pilot laugh. Lena bought corridor noodles and listened without joining.

"Prudence," the believer said.

"Fuel," the pilot said.

"Adjacency," the clerk at the next window said to nobody, and stamped a wristband.

Collision before breakfast: wrong border on the runner's crate, adjacency on the clerk's screen, mark on housing down the arcade, Vesper **WAIT** on the wall between grain and divorce.

Lena wiped her mouth, checked **COMMITTED** in her ledger, and walked toward the field office rumor Jun had left at Nadi's lips.

Tomorrow she'd need a corridor. Today she needed to know what seventy-four percent cost in a language that wasn't decimals.

She had a folder. She had a wristband. She had a brother behind a gate.

That would have to be enough until the wall moved again.
