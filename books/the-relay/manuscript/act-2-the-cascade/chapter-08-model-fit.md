# Chapter 08 — Model Fit

Lena found Jun at the Markets field office because the wall told her where belief went to get dressed as fact.

She'd walked from housing module C with the morning board tick still on her wrist and the pamphlet border folded in her folder like a bruise you kept poking. Nadi's fringe offer waited in her queue unread. Arin's **DELIVERED** sat above silence. Anger from last night had cooled into something she could carry without spilling—fuel, not fire.

The field office wasn't an office—just a side table in the public lip where consensus bureau gray could borrow electricity and call it outreach. Pilots leaned against the wall behind the queue. A clerk cried quietly about a refund that would never arrive. Someone smelled like fried dough and panic.

At the side table Jun had set up a portable display with the calm of a person serving coffee, not conclusions.

**BELIEF CASCADE — VESPER SUSPENSION — SEPARATIST CLUSTER — CONFIDENCE 0.74**

The number had a chin. Lena had seen it on the Ribbon thread wall when it crossed seventy. Seeing it here, beside freight futures and housing surcharges, felt worse. At Northhook fear had been red percentages. In Drift it was a ledger row with a decimal.

Jun looked up when she stopped at the edge of his table. Not surprised. Analysts were never surprised in public; surprise was something they saved for private.

"Courier," he said. "Compass patch. Northhook berth, six days, now committed around."

"You've got a chart for that too?"

"I've got a chart for movement under stress." He started to turn the display.

Lena didn't let him finish the gesture.

"My brother is behind that gate," she said.

Jun's hand paused on the screen. The separatist cluster glowed between them—elegant, tight, the kind of shape that made people nod because elegance felt like explanation.

"Orinreach Basin," he said after a beat. "I have basin traffic down, not zero. Mirror crews still working."

"Working isn't answering."

"No," he agreed, and didn't apologize for the agreement. "Traffic isn't silence. Silence is harder to chart."

She hated that he was right about the sentence and wrong about what it cost.

Only then did he turn the display so she could see. Routes and traffic. Couriers as dots. Gates as valves. Vesper closed, reroute pressure redistributing through Drift and long-corridor corridors like water finding cracks.

The separatist cluster sat in the center—her brother reduced to a traffic line before she'd agreed to be reduced at all.

Lena watched the cluster pulse on the screen and thought of Arin's hands on a ladder, knees saved before policy, tourists misreading mirrors for games. None of that lived in Jun's elegant center. None of it needed to. It lived in her chest, where decimals couldn't reach.

---

A woman in bureau gray stood behind Jun's chair—not Jun's assistant, someone with a badge and the posture of a person who'd come to defend decimals. Early thirties. Hair clipped for field work. Clipboard that wasn't a clipboard, just a screen with better shoes.

"This is Maret," Jun said. "Consensus liaison. She reviews what gets published."

Maret nodded at Lena the way administrators nodded at couriers: polite, already sorted into a bucket.

"Seventy-four percent isn't panic," Maret said before Lena asked. "It's publishable confidence. We don't publish at fifty because fifty is weather. Seventy-four means preparatory routing, fee advisories, public lip language—measures that save lives when the story is close enough to act on."

"Close enough to scare people," Lena said.

"Separatist activity in the mesh tracks with gate trouble. Vesper suspension timing, reroute density, pamphlet circulation—"

"My brother photographs marks on housing seams," Lena said. "He doesn't blow gates."

Maret's expression didn't change. "Maintenance lag is in the model. It's green. It's lagging."

"Because maintenance doesn't buy pamphlets."

Maret glanced at Jun—permission, procedure—then back. "Seventy-four lets us act before ninety. Acting at ninety is apology. You want apology routing? Fly long corridor after the fees triple."

The words were smooth. The blade was real. Lena felt the old impatience rise—the kind that made clerks sigh and pilots grin.

"I want my brother to answer his queue."

"Then you want basin relay sorting, not chart revision." Maret tapped her screen. "We can flag delays. We can't make silence polite."

Lena looked at the queue behind them—a pilot with grease on his neck, a woman holding a child's hand, someone who'd come for refunds and stayed for decimals. Seventy-four percent wasn't abstract here. It was the language clerks used when they said *preparatory* instead of *sorry*.

"You publish at seventy-four," Lena said. "Housing boards tick at seventy. Pamphlet runners sell at seventy. My wristband knew mirror language before I said Arin's name. That's not lead time. That's permission."

Maret's jaw worked once. "Permission to route around harm."

"Permission to charge adjacency while maintenance crews photograph seams." Lena tapped her folder. "I've got the seams. You've got the cluster. The wall's got the fees. Don't tell me seventy-four saves lives. Tell me who pays for it."

Maret glanced at Jun again. Jun watched Lena with the expression of someone seeing a person who wouldn't fit his chart.

"We're not the fee desk," Maret said.

"No," Lena said. "You're the reason the fee desk sleeps at night."

---

Jun walked Lena through the cascade the way he'd walk a client through a fee schedule—patient, precise, allergic to drama.

Separatist sabotage narrative picking up speed after Vesper suspension. Belief flows reinforcing through courier networks, public lips, pamphlets with clean borders that didn't match corridors. Maintenance explanations lagging—not absent, lagging. The model didn't need maintenance to be false. It needed maintenance to be slow.

"People build with certainty on hand," Jun said. "Certainty on hand is loud right now."

"You sound pleased."

"I sound accurate." He tapped the cluster. "This shape fits. Not perfectly. Fits."

Lena pulled the wrong-border pamphlet from her folder and laid it on his table beside the display.

The separatist line on the map didn't match his chart's jurisdiction layer. Same lie, two costumes—geography on paper, confidence on screen.

Jun's mouth tightened. "That's amateur cartography."

"Amateur cartography moved my housing surcharge." She slid Dael's scrap next. "And my story tax. And jurisdiction adjacency on basin manifests with reflective language."

"Those are fee mechanisms."

"Those are the same mechanism." She kept her voice level because level was cheaper than rage and she was running out of cheap. "Your cluster fits because fear fits. Not because someone's sabotaging gates."

Jun pulled up a maintenance lag layer—thin green lines trailing the red separatist cluster like understudies arriving late to a play. "Maintenance narrative exists. Look. It's not absent."

"It's not winning."

"Winning is a political word."

"It's a courier word." Lena tapped the green lines. "My brother photographs marks instead of answering because marks are maintenance language. Your chart treats them like noise."

Jun studied the layer. For a second she saw hunger—not for sabotage, for elegance. The strain of a mind that wanted the world to fit a shape it could publish.

"Noise is what your chart won't count."

"Noise is people checking walls while you count believers."

A pilot in the queue behind them said, too loud, "Separatists blew the corridor, everybody knows it."

Everybody didn't know it. Everybody was paying story tax on the knowing anyway.

Lena felt the queue press closer—bodies wanting decimals to mean safety. A woman with a child's hand asked Maret if seventy-four meant Vesper would reopen Tuesday. Maret said seventy-four meant preparatory measures. The woman said preparatory sounded like *soon*. Maret said preparatory sounded like *not yet*. The child asked for water. Nobody had free water. The vending wall charged story tax on bottles now, or so the pilot behind Lena claimed.

"Adjacency on hydration," the pilot said. "No shit."

Lena almost smiled. Almost.

Maret stepped in before Jun could answer. "Seventy-four is enough to act on. Usable isn't endorsement. It's lead time."

"Lead time for what?"

"For fees," Maret said, and almost sounded human. "For routing. For not pretending the wall is lying when the wall is pricing fear. You think markets move because analysts are cruel. Markets move because people pay before they understand. Our job is to understand first enough to reduce harm."

Lena wanted to throw the pamphlet at her. She didn't. Paper was evidence and evidence was expensive.

Jun lowered his voice. "If I count maintenance higher, the cluster loosens. If I loosen too early, people stop preparing for real failure. Measurement isn't cruelty. It's—"

"Filing," Lena said. "You filed me long-route before you asked my brother's name."

He closed the maintenance layer. "Arin. Mirror crew Orinreach. Last basin traffic on record thirty-one hours ago. Not zero." He said it like offering a bandage. "I can flag basin relay delays if that helps your queue."

"It helps my queue. It doesn't help my chest."

"I know." The admission surprised them both. He recovered with analyst speed. "Fear waves don't care about chests. They care about certainty on hand. Certainty on hand is separatist-shaped right now because separatist-shaped charges adjacency fees on reflective manifests."

"Pamphlet border."

"Pamphlet border." He nodded once. "Amateur cartography with professional consequences. I can add wrong-border pamphlets as a fee excuse if you have samples."

She almost laughed. "You want my folder."

"I want better facts." No apology. No smile. "You want your brother. We are not solving each other's problems."

"Finally something true."

Jun studied the pamphlet border without touching it. "The model doesn't require sabotage. It requires fear picking up speed."

"Seventy-four percent acceleration. My brother's not a number."

For a moment something moved behind his eyes—not sympathy, strain. The kind of strain that came from seeing a person where the chart wanted a dot.

Corin shifted the pamphlet stack an inch, nervous. Maret checked her screen like the queue might revolt if decimals wavered.

Jun compressed her anyway.

"You committed around." His voice stayed level—sorting habit, the part of him that worked even when the rest flinched. "That puts you in the long-route list—people who pay distance premiums rather than wait on an official story. Useful on paper. Painful individually." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Your choice is a number on his chart."

"I'm not choosing for your chart."

"You're choosing in view of it." He pulled up another layer—fee spikes mapped against fear tickers. The lines tracked too closely to be coincidence. "Fees move before stories do. Markets move before bulletins. Your housing board proved that yesterday."

"I didn't need a model to prove it."

"You needed a model to see how many people are paying the same proof." He hesitated. "There's a pilot—Nadi, *Quick Margin*—filing fringe corridors three days before public reroute density catches up. I don't like that she sees it first. I don't like that fees see it before either of us."

"Nadi insults Jun for free," Lena said. "I met her at boarding."

"She's not wrong about the fees." Jun closed the fee layer. "She's wrong about caring why. You care why. That's also a number to him."

Corin—graduate assistant, young, ink on his fingers—hovered at the table edge with a stack of pamphlets someone had submitted as samples. Wrong borders. Clean ink. He looked at Lena's folder like he wanted to ask permission and knew he wouldn't get it.

"Fees moved first," Corin said quietly to Jun. "Nadi filed fringe again. Wall moved before we updated."

Jun didn't look up. "I know."

"She's going to make the model look late."

"The model is late." Jun said it without heat. "Markets are rude."

---

Nadi chose that moment to ping Lena's queue—not friend, business.

**Fringe viable Meridian return — premium still open — certainty not included — reply if you want margin not lecture**

Then, three seconds later, a second line—fees moving first, not poetry:

**Fees jumped on long corridor while you were in Jun's office. Fringe still cheaper. Wall already priced 0.74. Reply.**

Lena stared at the messages.

Jun read them upside down with the ease of someone who'd learned to read fear in other people's pockets.

"She's offering you corridor knowledge at market price," he said.

"She's telling me your seventy-four already hit the wall." Lena looked at Maret. "Fees move first. Stories catch up late. Your publishable confidence, priced while we talked."

Maret's mouth tightened. "That's—"

"Tuesday," Lena said. "Nadi filed Tuesday. You filed decimals. The board filed fees. My brother filed a mark on a housing seam. Same storm. Different ledgers."

"She's offering you a route that feels wrong," Jun said.

"Wrong corridors are underbid." He almost smiled. "That's her religion."

"Your religion is seventy-four percent."

His expression cooled. "My religion is measurement. The cluster will shift when data shifts. Until then, acting like the elegant story isn't winning is—"

"Foolish?"

"Expensive." He gathered the pamphlet and scrap into a neat stack and returned them to her folder without being asked. "You came here angry. Good." He paused. "It won't keep your brother answering."

Maret added, too late to be helpful, "Seventy-four is how we get ahead of expensive."

Lena took the folder. Her hands were steady. Her chest wasn't.

---

Outside the field office the wall had updated without ceremony.

**REROUTE DENSITY +21% — LONG-CORRIDOR FEES ADJUSTING — VESPER WAIT**

A Markets runner shouted updated separatist pamphlets. Two for one. Wrong border unchanged. Story tax ticked up on the housing board visible down the arcade. Lena didn't need to walk there to know. She could feel fees the way she felt weather now.

At the dock housing past corridor seven, someone had added a third mark beside the seam she'd photographed—dot, stroke, steadier hand again. Checking continued. The wall didn't care.

Collision in one glance: Jun's cluster on the display behind her eyelids, pamphlet border in the folder, fee column on the board, mark on housing.

She opened messages to Arin.

**DELIVERED TO ORINREACH BASIN** still sat under her last honest note. No new words from him. No weather joke. No *don't come on my account* to argue with.

She typed nothing. Deleting drafts was becoming habit.

A pilot behind her in the noodle line said, "Separatists," like a weather report.

His friend said, "Fees."

"Same thing," the vendor said, and handed Lena extra broth without being asked—courier face discount, maybe pity, maybe both.

---

That evening she stood at the auction bay rail with noodles she didn't taste and watched *Quick Margin*'s slot flicker between pending and honest.

Jun had packed his display by then. Maret too. Belief would cascade whether they watched or not. Lena had seen the shape. Elegant. Fit. Wrong in the way a glove fit a hand that was still reaching for something else.

My brother is behind that gate.

The sentence didn't fit his chart. It didn't need to. Charts weren't gates. Charts weren't brothers on ladders sending marks instead of answers.

Anger sat clean and usable in her jaw. Fuel. The kind of fuel that made you walk to the courier desk instead of back to the field office. The kind that made you reply to Nadi with **Not hiring. Still committed around. Send viable if fees jump** and mean it.

She finished the noodles, filed the receipt, and walked back to housing module C.

The seam mark gleamed in corridor light. Someone checking. Someone not waiting for Vesper to explain the week.

Lena unfolded the desk, opened the ledger, and left **COMMITTED** where it was.

Tomorrow she'd need a corridor. Nadi's fringe or the long corridor or standing still until the fees ate her standing-up margin.

Tonight she had anger that worked like a tool—not fire for its own sake, grip. She could feel it in her legs on the walk back to module C, in the way she didn't stop at Jun's table though second bell had started and believers were already clustering for decimals.

That would have to be enough until something broke—the model, the gate, or her own habit of tracking instead of moving.

She washed her face in the module's thin sink. The water tasted like metal. The seam mark watched her like a colleague she hadn't earned yet.

---

She dreamed in percentages and woke before the alarm.

The housing seam mark was still there in corridor light—unchanged, unimpressed by Jun's decimals. She made tea on the module's sad burner and read the field office hours on the public lip.

Jun would be back at his table by second bell, serving measurement to anyone who stood still long enough.

Lena didn't go.

Going twice would make her a dot on his chart with a timestamp. She had Sera's fee strip and Dael's scrap and Nadi's message still glowing **certainty not included**.

She replied to Nadi with courier brevity: **Not hiring. Still committed around. Send viable if fees jump.**

Reply came while the tea cooled: **Fees already jumped. Fringe still cheaper than your pride.**

No emoji. Markets dialect respect in plain text.

Lena snorted into the tea. Pride wasn't the expense. Distance was. Person was. Eleven days around if corridors held and messages didn't die at relay jurisdiction.

She packed the bag, checked the folder—wrong border, marks, fee strips, Jun's brief she hadn't asked for but kept—and walked to the auction bay to watch departures like weather.

*Quick Margin*'s slot had moved from pending to **BOARDING**.

Nadi visible on the bay floor, cursing at a loader with affection. Jun not visible. Good.

Lena stood where pilots couldn't charge her rent for air and let anger settle into use—fuel, not fire.

She wasn't hired on fringe. She was still committed around.

She turned toward the courier desk to ask about basin relay sorting without paying for theater, and didn't look back at the field office table where belief went to get dressed as fact.

The courier clerk had seen her kind before—committed around, folder weight, eyes that had argued with decimals and lost politely.

"Basin relay," Lena said. "Orinreach. I need to know if messages are sorting or dying."

The clerk typed without looking at her face. "Delivery confirmed two days ago. Return traffic wait. Relay jurisdiction flagged reflective manifest language—"

"Bullshit."

"—standard adjacency review." He slid a strip across. "Not my bullshit. Want it faster, pay priority or fly."

"I committed around."

"Then you're already paying." He tapped the strip. "Basin crew traffic on record thirty-one hours ago. Mirror work. Not zero. Your brother's not a ghost. He's a queue."

Lena took the strip. Paper when systems got nervous. Paper when your chest needed something your display wouldn't give.

"Thank you," she said—courier courtesy, not forgiveness.

The clerk shrugged. "Don't thank me. Thank the mark on your housing seam. Somebody's still checking walls."

She walked back toward module C with the strip in her folder and anger still working—directed, not spent.

At the housing arcade mouth the board had ticked again. She didn't need to read it. Her wristband pulsed like it enjoyed the joke.

In module C the seam mark gleamed—hers, Arin's rhyme, somebody else's steadier hand down the arcade. **COMMITTED** in the ledger like a promise she'd already spent money on.

Enough to move on tomorrow. Enough for tonight.
