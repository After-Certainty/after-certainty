# Chapter 07 — Quick Margin

Nadi knew something was wrong before the analysts did because she had fuel in her tanks and bills on her desk.

The Drift Markets didn't orbit a star. They orbited opportunity—the slow gravity of price differentials, rumor gradients, the way freight moved when administrators spoke in clean notices and pilots spoke in fuel burn.

Nadi spoke in fuel burn.

Her ship, *Quick Margin*, was not pretty. Pretty ships cost money. She had a hull patched where a dock collision had kissed the port side three years ago, a crew of one plus a semi-autonomous loader that worked when it felt like it, and a reputation for taking routes other pilots called creative and fee clerks called why.

Creative paid when the Markets shifted.

This week the Markets were shifting.

She stood in the auction bay with a cup of bitter station coffee and watched the wall.

Every wall in the Drift Markets carried arrivals, departures, exchange rates, freight auctions. Entire fortunes rose and fell before breakfast. Nobody asked where you came from. The answer changed too often to matter.

What mattered was the next line.

The next line was Vesper Gate — suspended — reroute density up eighteen percent — long-corridor fees spiking.

"That's not eighteen," the pilot beside her said. "That's someone lying about eighteen."

Nadi didn't look at him. "Someone's always lying about eighteen."

"This is different."

Everything was different until it wasn't. Nadi had flown through three gate scares, two real slowdowns, one actual closure that lasted a week and turned out to be a certification backlog everyone pretended was sabotage so they could sell consulting.

She'd made money on two of those.

She sipped coffee and did math in her head.

Fuel load for Meridian leg: acceptable.

Berth fee if she missed the premium window: unacceptable.

Loader calibration bribe: regrettable but standard.

Return via Vesper: dead.

Return via long corridor: eleven days she didn't have if she wanted to keep the ship.

Return via fringe: ugly, underbid, margin if she didn't die of boredom or whatever pilots meant when they said *wrong*.

Her contracted run to Meridian Arc was still good—Ribbon freight paid reliably, and reliable was a word you didn't sneeze at when your loader had moods. But the return leg had been Vesper corridor, Vesper fees, Vesper fuel economics that only worked if you didn't think too hard about why they were cheap.

Cheap meant enough traffic to hide margin.

No Vesper meant long way home or sit in the Markets burning berth fees she couldn't afford.

"Sabotage?" the pilot tried.

Nadi rolled her eyes at the wall. "Sure."

"You don't believe it?"

"I believe my fuel gauge." She finished the coffee, crushed the cup. "I believe reroutes. I believe fees. I believe people will pay to move cargo before they pay to be right."

The pilot laughed like she'd said something cynical instead of something she'd learned by scraping hull plates with her own hands.

---

At the edge of the auction bay, Jun somebody stood with a portable display and the expression of a person who thought fear could be graphed.

Consensus bureau gray. Belief flows. The kind of profession Nadi filed under later.

He wasn't looking at ships. He was looking at the wall the way Nadi looked at fuel—measuring what moved first.

Nadi had seen his public brief on the thread ribbon. Separatist cluster. Elegant curves. The wall had priced his elegance three days ago and moved on without waiting for the footnotes.

She didn't have time for footnotes.

She had a loader to bribe and a fringe corridor to file before the premium contract window closed.

She walked past him on her way to the dock clamp. Close enough to count as contact. Far enough to keep it cheap.

"Pilot," Jun said without looking up. "Quick Margin. Fringe filing."

Nadi stopped. Not because she wanted conversation. Because he had her ship's name right.

"You filing or asking?"

"Asking." He tilted the display a degree—not enough to show her, enough to prove he had layers. "Fees on fringe corridors are running ahead of public reroute density. I'm trying to understand why."

"Because fringe pilots fly before analysts finish sentences." She shifted her grip on the cable coil. "Because fees move before bulletins. Because wrong corridors are underbid and underbid is margin."

"That's adaptation."

"That's Tuesday." She met his eyes. "You want to graph Tuesday, graph Tuesday. I want to lift before the premium window closes."

He nodded once—the nod of someone filing her as a case. "If your fringe corridor sees traffic before the cluster updates, I'd like—"

"You'd like a pilot who works for footnotes." Nadi smiled without warmth. "File me under later."

She walked on. Ninety seconds, maybe less. Enough to know he was measuring what moved first and not liking that pilots moved first. Good. Analysts should be uncomfortable in auction bays. Auction bays were for people who paid for fuel.

Jun's voice followed her one step. "If the fringe corridor prices fear before the cluster updates—"

"Then your cluster's late." Nadi didn't turn. "File that."

Silence. Then the scratch of his stylus. Filing her after all.

---

In the ship she spread her trade maps across the nav table—not the public ones, her maps, layered with three years of memory.

Red lines where routes had thinned.

Green where prices had jumped.

Yellow where rumors lived.

Rumor was not data. Rumor was weather. You flew through it anyway if the cargo was time-sensitive and the client was desperate.

Vesper suspension had turned yellow into red on two corridors she'd depended on.

She tapped the long corridor option. Eleven days. Two fees. A jurisdiction that charged extra if your manifest included words like mirror or reflective surface because some clerk had been scammed once and wrote policy like revenge.

She tapped the Markets hold option. Berth fees. Opportunity cost. Loader sulk.

She tapped a third line she'd been ignoring—a fringe corridor through archive adjacency space, longer than Vesper, shorter than the full long way, ugly and unpopular because pilots said it felt wrong in a way they couldn't price.

Wrong was often underbid.

Nadi marked it.

Her comm chimed. Client ping—mirror-tech firm from the Ribbon, the kind that paid well and asked few questions.

**Need priority slot Meridian Arc — revised delivery window — paying premium for certainty.**

Certainty.

Nadi almost laughed.

She typed back: **Premium accepted. Certainty not included. Delivery yes.**

She didn't wait for the frown that wouldn't come because clients like this didn't use emojis. They used lawyers.

---

By afternoon the bay smelled like hot metal and hurry.

Reroutes stacked up. Dock crews shouted in four dialects. Jun was still at the wall edge, still taking notes, still calm in a way that made Nadi want to charge him rent for the air he breathed.

She found her assigned dock clamp and saw the mark—dot and stroke, same as last month, same as a corridor housing she'd photographed once and filed under third time.

Not a theory. A habit.

Today someone had added a second mark beside it. Steadier hand. Cleaner stroke. The dot sat closer to true center, the line longer by a millimeter that mattered if you were the kind of person who read walls with your fingers.

Nadi ran her glove along both marks. Old hand first—quick, functional, the mark of someone working between lifts. New hand second—patient, almost formal, the mark of someone who'd decided the clamp was worth returning to.

Keeper work. Not her religion. Her religion was clamps that held.

She photographed both and sent them to her own archive with a note: **not alone**.

Habits were how pilots survived when theories were cheap.

Her loader sulked until she promised it extra calibration time. She promised. Loaders remembered promises the way fee clerks remembered lies.

Pre-flight check by hand because trust was a luxury. Clamp pressure. Seal integrity. Fringe corridor nav package loaded twice because once was for optimists. Fuel numbers entered, then entered again because a pilot who mistyped fuel deserved whatever the corridor did to her.

Cost to lift: high.

Cost to sit: higher.

Margin if she lifted on time: enough to insult an analyst in public and buy better coffee next week.

---

She lifted on schedule with Meridian Arc freight humming in the belly and the fringe corridor loaded like a promise she might regret.

Lift cost: fuel, clamp fees, loader dignity.

Lift savings: not being in the Markets when the next reroute density tick hit.

Lift risk: fringe felt wrong in the way pilots meant when they wanted more money and didn't get it.

Worth it: yes.

The Markets fell away behind her—stations bright as thrown dice, ships crawling between them, the wall's numbers still updating in her peripheral display because pilots who looked away from numbers too long ended up working for numbers instead of the other way around.

Vesper Gate suspension scrolled past on the news ribbon.

Separatist theories scrolled past after it.

Nadi set her jaw and flew.

---

Meridian Arc corridor opened like a clean knife.

Ribbon freight delivered. Client satisfied. Premium paid.

Delivery margin: good.

Return margin: depended on fringe being merely wrong, not catastrophic.

Nadi stood in Northhook Transit for one night on the client's dime and watched Lake Orin throw Meridian Arc overhead in doubled light. Tourists failed the stair exercise. Locals didn't.

She took her berth assignment for the return fringe run and left the lake to the people who needed it more than she did.

Northhook at night had its own wall—the thread percentages quieter but still red at the edges, Vesper still suspended, maintenance line still not winning. Nadi drank bad tea in a public lip and listened to a dock clerk tell a tourist that Lake Orin was safe and gates argued.

"Tourists pay double for metaphors," the clerk said.

"Tourists pay double for everything," Nadi said.

The clerk grinned. "You flying fringe?"

"You filing gossip?"

"Filing's free. Fringe isn't."

Nadi crushed the cup. Fringe wasn't free either. It was only cheaper than honesty long enough to matter.

She ran return numbers on the nav table while the lake tourists embarrassed themselves below.

Fringe fuel burn: high but bounded.

Long corridor fuel burn: higher.

Berth hold in Markets until a sane corridor opened: bankruptcy dressed as patience.

Premium contract return window: closing.

She filed fringe again and ignored the part of her that hated corridors that felt wrong. Feelings didn't pay dock fees. Margins did.

---

The return fringe leg was where *Quick Margin* stopped being a job and became a body problem.

Nadi had flown ugly corridors before—lonely routes, thin traffic, echoes that came back wrong. Wrong was usually underbid because wrong was usually boredom with a fee discount.

This corridor was wrong in a different register.

Pressure first—not hull breach pressure, the subtle squeeze of adjacency space that made your ears treat silence as a substance. *Quick Margin*'s patched hull groaned in a frequency Nadi felt in her molars. The cabin lights dimmed a shade, brightened, dimmed—flicker tied to nothing she could fix without stopping, and stopping in fringe space was a fee you paid in hull integrity.

Then the nav display hiccuped—stars sliding half a degree left while the chronometer insisted nothing had moved. Wrong-feeling corridor. Pilots said it like a weather report. Nadi had always heard *underbid* inside the phrase.

She kept her gloves on the stick and her eyes on fuel burn. Numbers didn't flicker. Numbers were the contract.

"You're being dramatic," she told the loader.

The loader whined like it disagreed.

Fringe corridors didn't have traffic to hide in. No other ships to blame. No dock foreman to shout at. Just *Quick Margin* and the corridor and the feeling that the walls were listening wrong—listening the way a crowded room listened before someone said the thing nobody wanted said.

She'd filed this route because the numbers worked. Numbers were supposed to be the religion.

The viewport showed nothing useful—dark with occasional starchart bleed, the Ribbon a rumor ahead. Pressure leaned on her shoulders. Flicker eased for thirty seconds and came back meaner, nav lights strobing half a beat off the chronometer, the kind of out of step that made you distrust your own hands before you distrusted the map.

Nadi flew anyway. Adaptation wasn't philosophy. Adaptation was burn rate and a steady grip.

Then the curve changed and Lake Orin opened below like a held breath.

Northhook's lower path. Doubled Meridian lights on water. A figure at the railing, bag on one hip, faded compass rose on the shoulder visible even from altitude—the particular patch couriers wore when they wanted to be found by other couriers and not by tourists.

Lena.

Nadi knew the look without needing the name. Folder weight. Ledger face. The private set of someone holding a conversation in her head with someone far away. Compass patch faded but readable—the courier rose on the shoulder, the mark of someone who still believed routes connected people and not just manifests.

The courier wasn't looking up. Good. Pilots didn't need gratitude. They needed clear berths.

Nadi didn't ping. Didn't wave. Didn't slow.

A viewport pass wasn't a meeting. It was a sight line—proof the long way and the fringe way and the committed way all still touched the same lake while Vesper stayed silent. Proof that the woman on the railing and the pilot in the ugly corridor were paying different prices for the same silence.

She noted the pass in her head the way she noted fuel: **courier, Northhook lip, committed face, no hire**. Business for later maybe. Not now.

She adjusted course one degree—fuel cost, negligible—and let the lake fall away behind.

Wrong corridors felt wrong for a reason. Usually the reason was loneliness—too few ships, too much silence, too many places where your echo came back unfamiliar.

This corridor had all of that.

It also had fees she could afford.

Halfway through she got a ping from a pilot she knew only by callsign—**BRICK**—running the long way because BRICK didn't take fringe bets.

*You seeing prices on the back leg?*

Nadi checked. Prices were ugly-beautiful, the kind that meant someone somewhere was scared enough to pay.

*Seeing them,* she sent.

*Gate's not coming back Tuesday,* BRICK wrote. *My cousin in admin says wait until they admit they don't know.*

Nadi didn't have cousins in admin. She had fuel and a premium contract and a map that remembered what fear did to freight.

*Tuesday's a theory,* she sent. *I'm flying today.*

BRICK sent back a string of characters that meant respect in Markets dialect.

The corridor narrowed—not physically, perceptually. Pressure increased. Flicker returned. Nadi's hands stayed steady because steady was cheaper than correcting a wobble after the fact. She thought about Jun at the bay edge measuring what moved first. She'd moved first. The wall would catch up or it wouldn't. Either way she'd landed with margin.

The flicker eased after the lake pass—not gone, eased. Pressure stayed. Nadi flew through it the way she flew through everything: burn now, adapt later, let analysts name it when it was over.

Fuel readout: acceptable.

Margin readout: better than acceptable if she made Markets before the next fee tick.

She didn't think about the courier again. Thinking about people who weren't paying you was a luxury. She thought about BRICK's cousin's roommate and Jun's decimals and the wall that would price her survival before anyone called it insight.

---

Back in the Markets a week later—because that's how fringe corridors measured time, in weeks you hadn't planned for—Nadi updated her maps.

Vesper still suspended.

Reroute density up twenty-two percent now, not eighteen; someone had stopped lying.

Separatist theories thicker on the public ribbon.

Her business alive.

Jun somebody would publish a cascade chart next week and call it insight. The wall had already priced fear into the fringe corridor before his chart finished rendering.

Nadi didn't mind fear.

She minded missing the margin.

She filed the fringe corridor as **viable** and the long corridor as **emergency only** and Vesper as **wait**, which was the closest thing to prayer she allowed herself.

Margin report for the week: alive.

Fuel spend: ugly.

Premium client satisfaction: sufficient.

Jun's impending chart: irrelevant to her ledger.

On the dock clamp, the steadier mark remained beside hers from the week before.

Same dot. Same stroke.

Different hand.

Nadi touched it with her glove and didn't photograph it this time.

Some habits became trust when you saw them repeat.

---

Before the second lift she walked the auction bay with BRICK on comms and Jun's published chart on the public ribbon.

**CONFIDENCE 0.76** now. Someone else's decimal. The wall had priced seventy-six before Jun's animation finished.

"Saw your cousin's memo," Nadi told BRICK.

*Not my cousin. My cousin's roommate.*

"Even better."

*You're still alive.*

**Still viable.**

BRICK sent a separatist podcast link. Nadi didn't open it. Podcasts didn't burn fuel. She watched housing fees climb on the board—story tax highlighted, jurisdiction adjacency footnoted, wrong-border pamphlets stacked near a runner's feet.

A border believer in orange argued with a pilot at the next clamp—prudence versus fuel, adjacency versus gauge. Nadi had heard the fight before. She'd file it under **Tuesday** and lift anyway.

Collision in one walk: fee spike, separatist noise, fresh mark on her clamp when she reached the dock.

She touched the mark. Steadier hand. Third voice in the grammar.

"Lifting?" the dock foreman asked.

"Lifting."

"Jun says Tuesday."

"Jun can file Tuesday under later."

The foreman snorted. Dock workers didn't believe analysts. They believed schedules and hands and marks on clamps that meant someone checked before the loader sulked.

*Quick Margin* rose on thrust that cost money and saved more.

The Markets fell away.

Inside the cockpit the fringe corridor pressed again—less wrong than the first time, which meant either adaptation or numbness. Nadi didn't care which. She noted fuel, noted margin, noted the viewport memory of a courier on a lake railing with a compass patch and a face that said **committed** in a dialect pilots could read without introductions.

BRICK pinged mid-climb: *Heard Jun published. Seventy-six. You alive?*

**Alive. Viable. Fringe still cheaper than his chart.**

*Copy. See you when the wall lies again.*

Nadi closed the comm and flew.

Somewhere a gate stayed silent.

Somewhere a courier walked toward a field office with a folder and a brother behind **WAIT**.

Somewhere a mirror crew kept tourists from breaking knees.

Nadi kept flying.

The wall would update. She would read it. BRICK would send another theory. Jun somebody would publish a cascade chart that made fear look elegant.

Nadi would laugh once, quietly, in a cockpit that smelled like her own coffee and patched hull, and adjust course before the elegant people finished their sentences.

That was also a kind of answer.
