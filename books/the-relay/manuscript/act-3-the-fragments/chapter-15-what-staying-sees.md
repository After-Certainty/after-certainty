# Chapter 15 — What Staying Sees

Arin knew the lake was wrong before the gauge did.

Lena would have hated that sentence. She trusted things that could be checked quickly: a manifest, a seal, the cant of a badly loaded hold, the nervous too-bright answer of a man smuggling contraband under seed. Lena liked evidence with its hands where she could see them.

The lake did not.

The lake gave itself away by returning late to ordinary things.

It left a mineral shine on the lower lock-stones three mornings in a row where there should have been green scum. It pulled the reed-beds flat to the east though the wind had held steady west since second night. It put the gulls on the wrong pilings and kept them there, heads tucked, bodies hunched against a cold that had no business arriving before the redleaf turn. At dawn, when the mist should have risen in two clean layers — low silver on the water, high blue along the ridge — it came up in one thick breath and stayed close to the surface, as if the lake had forgotten the old agreement between water and air.

The gauge at South Lock read within tolerance.

The gauge was lying.

Not intentionally. Instruments had the innocence of things wrong only in the way they were built to be wrong. The gauge measured level against the stone throat, and strictly speaking the level was fine. A dry-office clerk could stamp the morning stable. But the Tarn was not a number in stone. It was water moving through beds, channels, undercurrents, mountain melt, mirror-rain, and black springs cold enough to turn a man's hand clumsy in summer. The level could hold while the lake changed its mind entirely.

Arin stood on the wet boards with one boot braced against the lock rail and listened to the south chain speak through its rust.

Not with romance. The chain did not whisper secrets. It creaked. It scraped. It complained like an old animal asked to stand after a long night. But every complaint had shape. Too high a scrape meant grit in the lower wheel. Too dry a snap meant the second pulley needed grease. A dull lag between the crank and the gate meant the water on the far side was pressing wrong, either by level or by mood, and mood was not a category any office form had ever been built to respect.

This morning the lag was too long.

He stopped turning.

Behind him, Mala said, "You going to stare it open?"

"Maybe."

"Good. Saves the shoulder."

She was eighty if she was a day and had been eighty, by everyone's account, for at least twenty years. She sat on an overturned bait crate under the lockhouse awning with a blanket across her knees, mending net with fingers bent almost sideways by age and use. There were three people on the Tarn who could mend a net that fine. Two of them admitted it. Mala never had. She had fished the southern mirrors before Arin was born, before Lena was born, before the fast gate turned the old passenger wharf into a café and made everyone pretend the lake was a view instead of a discipline.

"You hear it?" Arin asked.

"I hear my knee and that stupid chain."

"The delay."

"I hear that too. I was being polite."

He turned the crank one notch, then another. The gate moved, but reluctantly, shoving a narrow black mouth open between lock and lake. Water should have pulled through in a clear tongue. Instead it came in thick and late, rolling once against the stone before it remembered the direction.

Mala stopped mending.

There. That was the real gauge.

"What did North Lock say?" she asked.

"Nothing yet."

"Then they're either asleep, dead, or ashamed of their handwriting."

"At least two of those are always possible."

Mala grunted. "Write it down."

He had already reached for the slate hanging from the lockhouse nail.

*South Lock. Third dawn before redleaf. Level within tolerance. Chain lag long. East reed-beds flattened against west wind. Mist single-layer, low. Gulls on wrong pilings. Water entered lock thick, late roll.*

He paused, charcoal above the slate.

Then added:

*Feels early-winter under summer skin.*

He almost rubbed that line out. It sounded like something his sister would laugh at, though not cruelly. Lena did not laugh cruelly. She laughed like someone kicking a stone off a road before it tripped her. The line was not technical. But old Jaro at North Lock would know what it meant. So would Mala. So would Innes at the reed beds, who had been saying for a week that the roots were hearing freeze.

He left it.

Mala watched him. "You put the skin line?"

"Yes."

"Good. Jaro's bones understand skin better than numbers."

"He'll complain."

"Jaro complains to prove he's alive. Let him."

Arin tore the slate-sheet free, folded it twice, and tucked it into the oiled pouch by the door. Beside it were four others going out by water before noon: reed-root reports from Innes, fish-depth marks from the southern mirrors, a note from the hill orchards about bees returning to lower blossoms out of order, and one from the children at Pike School, who had been assigned to count night insects and had done it with the severity only children and judges could sustain.

Their note was the neatest.

*Lantern-moths below bridge on cold nights. Wrong. Teacher says send.*

He smiled despite himself every time he saw it.

*Teacher says send* was better than half the official directives he had ever read.

The fast gate had been dark for twenty-six days.

At first people had counted by announcements. Suspended. Review. Delayed reopening. Regional assurance. Words from screens, clerks, travelers who had come through two gates before the last one went. Then the screens stopped updating, the clerks stopped pretending, and the travelers became residents because no road opened long enough to take them back out.

After that the Tarn counted by shortages.

Twelve days since citrus. Sixteen since lamp oil in sealed tins. Nineteen since a courier arrived who had not come the long way and looked half-starved by the time he put his boots on the dock. Twenty-two since the north school closed because the heater part was somewhere on the wrong side of a dark gate. Twenty-three since Arin had stopped walking to the message office at dusk, though some evenings his feet took him halfway there before dignity caught up.

Twenty-six since Lena's last message should have come.

Not since it came. Since it should have.

He did not tell people that number. People on the Tarn knew how to respect a silence when it had family inside it. Or most did. Mala had no such respect, but she was old enough to have earned the right to trespass.

"You dreamed about her again," she said.

Arin tied the oiled pouch shut. "Good morning to you too."

"You had the face."

"I have many faces."

"You have three. Lock face, sister face, and the one you use when outsiders tell you water is symbolic."

He lifted the pouch from the nail and slung it across his shoulder. "What does sister face look like?"

"Like lock face, but angry at yourself instead of the chain."

That was too accurate to reward.

He stepped out from under the awning. The mist had begun to lift in shreds now, late and ugly, pulling apart instead of rising. Beyond the lock mouth, the Tarn spread under a dim sky, wide and dark and crowded with islands that were not really islands when the water was honest — reed mats, sand bars, old stone foundations, half-drowned woods, all of them appearing and vanishing across the year according to rules outsiders mistook for chance. In spring, the mirror fields opened. In summer, the southern channels warmed and the bluefish came shallow. In redleaf, the lake gave back the dead branches it had borrowed. In deep cold, the locks sang under ice, and everyone who knew anything walked differently.

This year the lake was doing four seasons at once and none of them well: bluefish deep in warm weeks, reeds flattening under the wrong wind, fog thick when the moon was thin, insect hatches returning after they had already finished. Every sign could be explained alone. Together, they made the old people quiet.

He took the narrow skiff because the lock launch used fuel they could not replace, and rowing gave his hands something to do.

At the edge of the lower dock, he glanced toward the gate road.

The arch stood above town on its stone platform, too beautiful and too useless, seven centuries of pale engineering catching the weak morning light. Before the dark, it had made the Tarn small: everywhere else near, the Tarn picturesque, a place between more important distances. It let a person step through and return with sweets from a market under three suns, machine parts from a moon with no weather, rumors from cities whose names sounded like coins. It made leaving easy enough that staying began to look like a defect of imagination.

Lena had loved it for that.

Not selfishly. That would have been simpler to resent. She loved motion because motion had saved her. She learned to read routes and manifests and rooms that tried to lie. She learned to leave before anything asked her to belong badly. The fast roads took what had always been restless in her and gave it a profession.

He had hated them for the same reason.

No, not hated. That was too clean.

He had hated what they made respectable.

At the family table, years ago, Lena had spread her first courier posting across the wood beside a half-peeled orange, smiling as if the future were a series of doors finally admitting what she already knew about herself. Three gate families in one season. Vesper to Sennen to Maween and back. Fast pay. Fast roads. No long berths, no winter locks, no waiting out fog.

"Easy," she had said.

He had said, "No. It's just fast."

He had not meant to wound her. Or he had, but not as much as it had landed.

She had stared at him as though he had slapped the paper out of her hands.

"Some of us like arriving," she'd said.

"Some places are only places if you don't use them as doors."

"Gods, Arin."

"I'm serious."

"I know. That's the problem."

Their mother had made a noise then, warning or plea, but it had been too late. Lena had folded the posting with careful hands and put it back in its envelope. Later, when she left, she hugged him hard enough to be forgiven and not long enough to discuss it.

After that she sent sweets.

Peppermint drops when she passed through Vesper. Sour fruit twists from Tessom. Once a tiny sealed jar of salt-honey from Anneth with a note saying, *The man said this was local; I suspect the man lies but the honey does not.*

He kept the notes.

Not in a sentimental way. In a drawer. Which was, he supposed, the sentimental way if one had no imagination.

The skiff knocked softly against the dock. He stepped in, pushed off, and rowed into the mist.

By midmorning he had crossed to Pike School, traded the outgoing pouch for two fresh reports, and been scolded by twelve children for failing to bring news from the gate town.

"News is expensive," he told them.

"Teacher says news is observation plus answer," said a girl with ink on her nose.

"Teacher has become dangerous."

Teacher, whose name was Bel, stood in the doorway behind them with both arms folded and did not deny it. She was younger than Arin by a few years and had the exhausted brightness of someone trying to make children feel the world had not become less trustworthy while personally suspecting the opposite.

"They counted moths," she said.

"I saw."

"They want to know if North Lock answered about the cold nights."

"I'm on my way there."

The ink-nosed girl pointed at his pouch. "Then tell them Teacher says answer."

"I will tell them the moths said it. That carries more force."

This pleased them.

Bel walked him back to the skiff.

"You heard from Lena?" she asked.

He shook his head.

"She's clever."

"That's not the same as safe."

"No."

They stood a moment. Across the schoolyard, two boys were arguing over whether lantern-moths counted if found dead in a windowsill.

Bel handed him another folded page.

"From the orchard terraces. Bees on lower blossoms again. Third time."

"Third?"

"Second on paper. First time old Pera said she didn't want to sound foolish."

"That kills more than storms."

"Yes," Bel said. "I told her so. Kindly."

"No you didn't."

"No. But I gave her tea after."

He tucked the note into the pouch.

"Bel," he said. "Why are we doing this?"

She looked toward the children. "Because they're watching."

"That's Sera's answer."

"Who?"

"Never mind."

Bel studied him. "You mean the notes."

"I mean the notes."

"The offices don't answer. The gate doesn't answer. The message board doesn't answer. Someone should."

"Yes," she said. "Manners."

He laughed then, because of course.

The skiff shifted under him as he stepped in.

"Arin," Bel said.

He looked back.

"If she can come, she will."

He did not ask whether she meant Lena. There was only one *she* who entered sentences that way now.

"I know."

Bel's expression gentled, which he hated because it was kind. "Knowing doesn't always help."

"No."

"Then I'll say the other thing. If she can't, it may not be because she chose not to."

He looked down at the oarlocks.

If Lena did not come, it might not mean she had stayed away. If no message arrived, it might not mean no message had been sent. A courier could be three worlds away carrying a packet in her cuff and still be no closer than silence.

Or she could be alive and not trying.

He did not believe that. Most days.

Some evenings the unworthy thought came and sat beside him anyway.

"I should go," he said.

Bel let him.

At North Lock, Jaro had already received the morning's pouch by ridge boy and had three objections prepared.

First, south people exaggerated mist because they liked poetry. Second, gulls were unreliable witnesses and should not be granted column space. Third, *feels early-winter under summer skin* was either nonsense or exactly what his left hip had been saying since moonset, and he resented having to agree with Arin in writing.

"Put that," Arin said.

"I will not put my hip in the record."

"Then put *delay at south chain confirmed by Jaro's hip.*"

Jaro spat into the water, missed, and looked personally betrayed by the wind. He was a broad old man with one white eye and a beard that had collected twenty years of weather without becoming wise about any of it. He had kept North Lock through three floods, two bad freezes, one algae year, and the brief fashionable period when central officials tried to rename the channels by number. He had ignored the numbers until the officials left and then burned the map for kindling.

He read Arin's notes with slow care.

"This is too much," he said.

"The lake?"

"The pattern."

Arin waited.

Jaro took a charcoal stub from behind his ear and made his own marks beneath Arin's. *North Lock: winter-skin confirmed. Chain pull heavy though level within tolerance. Bluefish reported deep under warm surface. North fog moving against upper air.*

Then he added:

*Not ordinary early. Not ordinary late.*

"That's helpful," Arin said.

"It's frightening."

"Also helpful."

Jaro folded the sheet and sealed it with a thumb of wax warmed over the lockhouse lamp. No official seal. Just North Lock: three lines for water, one slash for gate. His granddaughter had carved it; the slash leaned left.

"Where does this go?" Jaro asked.

"East reed-beds, hill orchards, Pike School, south mirrors. Then wherever the next runner can take it."

"Beyond Tarn?"

"If there's a boat."

"There's always a boat until there isn't."

"That is almost wisdom."

"It's age. People confuse them when they want something."

Jaro handed him the sealed sheet.

"Send it past the dark if anyone comes through," he said.

Arin looked at him.

Jaro's face remained turned toward the water, but the set of his shoulders had changed.

"You think it goes that far?"

"I think if the lake is wrong here and the gates are wrong everywhere, either the world has developed a sense of humor or the wrongness is speaking more languages than ours."

"Who out there would care what our lake says?"

"Someone whose own water stopped answering." Jaro finally looked at him. "Or no one. Send it anyway."

*Send it anyway.*

The lake gave a dull knock under the lock gate.

Both men turned.

The sound came again. Not metal. Water against wood from below, a pulse where there should have been steady pressure.

Jaro's hand went to the rail.

"Black spring," he said.

"Here?"

"No."

The answer came too fast.

Then the water in the lock darkened from underneath.

Not fully. Not dramatically. A bloom of cold spreading upward through the green-brown water, ink in a glass, bringing with it the particular smell of deep stone and old winter. Arin felt the temperature fall across his face before his hand reached down. The surface filmed around his fingers, not ice, not yet, but a memory of it.

Black-spring cold in North Lock before redleaf.

Jaro swore once, softly.

"Write," he said.

Arin already had the slate.

For the next half-hour the lockhouse filled with measurements. Jaro measured depth and flow. Arin took temperature with a cracked mercury tube and then with his own hand because he trusted neither alone. Jaro's granddaughter came running from the upper path and was sent for Mala, then sent back because Mala had already started over, having heard something in the gulls or her knee or the moral fabric of the morning. A boy from Pike School arrived with two older students because Teacher had seen the mist shift and decided school could learn outside.

By noon there were nine people at North Lock and five separate notes being written.

The black-spring cold came in pulses, not flow. That was the worst part. A living system could misbehave continuously and still be read. Pulses meant something opening and closing, somewhere under the lake or beyond it, a rhythm with no instrument attached. Every seventh pull of the chain lagged longer. Every third pulse brought a faint metallic taste to the air. The gulls lifted all at once and resettled farther inland.

"Don't write *strange,*" Jaro barked when one of the schoolboys wrote *water tasted like pennies.*

"Write *pennies,*" Bel said, arriving breathless behind him. "If it tastes like pennies, write pennies."

"It's imprecise."

"It's observable."

Arin nearly laughed at Jaro's offended face.

Mala arrived last, because she refused to hurry and because everyone who had ever hurried on wet lock-stones had eventually stopped being smug about it. She leaned on her stick and looked at the water.

"Bad," she said.

Jaro threw up his hands. "Excellent contribution."

"Worse than yours. That is my contribution."

"Can you be specific?"

"I can be old." She pointed with her stick to the lower eddy. "There. The turn's backward under the lip."

Arin crouched, followed the line of her stick, and saw it: a small curl in the water, rotating against the lock's pull. Tiny. Nearly nothing. The kind of thing a central gauge would never know existed.

He wrote it down.

By late afternoon the notes had become a packet.

Not a report. Reports had destinations and the self-importance of formats. This was a packet because a packet could be handed to a person with wet sleeves and told *go.*

Arin copied the summary twice. Bel's older students copied the observations in block hand. Jaro argued about every adjective. Mala added a line herself, the letters sharp and angry from disuse.

*The lake is taking winter into the locks from below.*

No one improved it.

When they were done, Arin laid the pages on the lockhouse table and read them through. South lag. North pulse. Reed flattening. Moths low. Bees wrong. Bluefish deep. Pennies. Backward eddy. Winter-skin. *Tell the next.*

*Tell the next* had become the closing line on most of their scraps without anyone voting on it.

He hesitated, then added beneath it:

*Answer if you see the same.*

Bel read over his shoulder.

"That's new."

"Yes."

"Good."

"Maybe."

"No," she said. "Good."

He let it stay.

Near dusk, a boat came in.

Not through the gate. Nothing came through the gate anymore. This was a slow skiff from the west channel, a narrow trader with patched sails and a hull low with jars of lamp fat, dried kelp, two sacks of flour, and letters that had been collected across three settlements and would disappoint nearly everyone waiting for them because most were old. The pilot was a woman named Senn, no relation to Sennen and tired of the joke, with a bad cough and a worse temper. She tied off at North Lock and asked whether anyone was buying fat.

Arin asked where she was going next.

"Depends who pays."

"Can you take a packet past the lower mirrors?"

"I can take a packet anywhere before I read it and nowhere after, if it looks troublesome."

"It's troublesome."

"Then pay before I gain ethics."

He did, with two strips of dried fish and the last of his good lamp oil. She inspected the oil, found it acceptable, and tucked the packet into the inner pocket of her coat.

"Who gets it?" she asked.

"Anyone keeping notes on wrong water, wrong light, wrong season. Ask at the reed villages first. Then west if anyone's running."

"That is not an address."

"No."

"People used to know how to address things."

"People used to know who would answer."

Senn looked at him then, more closely than the transaction required.

"You expecting one?"

"An answer?"

"A person."

He looked across the darkening water toward the gate road, the useless arch, the town lights coming on one by one below it.

"Yes."

"From beyond?"

"Yes."

Senn nodded, as if that explained some small stupidity in him she had been trying to place. "Then I'll carry it."

"Because you're moved?"

"Because waiting people pay badly but remember debts accurately."

"Fair."

"And because the water's wrong west too."

Arin went still.

She coughed into her sleeve. "Light over the marsh went green three nights running. Not storm green. Old-metal green. Frogs shut up when it came, all at once. My uncle says he saw that once before, the year the west sluice cracked under calm water."

"Did he write it down?"

"He told me."

"Write it down."

"I'm a pilot."

"So was Osko," Arin said, though he did not know why the name came to him then — old slow-way pilot out of Lena's occasional stories, a man who read gauges by sound and tolerated nothing decorative except profanity. "Pilots know things maps don't."

Senn looked suspicious of being respected. "Give me paper."

Bel had some. Of course she did.

The pilot wrote slowly, chewing the inside of her cheek. Arin did not correct her spelling. Nobody did.

*West marsh light green three nights. Frogs stopped. Old-metal color. Uncle saw once before west sluice cracked.*

She paused, then added:

*Tell the next.*

Arin watched the words appear in her hand.

Not his. Not Bel's. Not Jaro's. Hers.

He folded her note into the packet before she could change her mind.

The trader left under first dark, slipping west through a channel the fast-gate people had always called unnavigable because the charts did, while everyone who lived near it used it twice a week when the reeds were low. Arin stood on the dock until her stern lamp became another wandering star among the reed shadows.

Mala came to stand beside him.

"You look less stupid," she said.

"I feel exactly as stupid."

"Then your face lies."

"It comes from my mother's side."

"Mm. Your sister got the motion. You got the sulk."

He did not defend himself. She was not entirely wrong.

They watched the lamp disappear.

"You think she's alive?" Mala asked.

He answered too quickly. "Yes."

"That was not a question for your fear."

He breathed out.

The lake moved below them, dark and slow and wrong.

"I don't know," he said.

Mala nodded. "Better."

"I hate better."

"Most better things are hateful at first."

He leaned his forearms on the wet rail. Across the water, the arch glowed faintly in the town lights, empty at its center. He imagined Lena somewhere beyond it: moving, arguing, noticing, getting herself shut out of places because she could not leave a wrong sentence uncorrected. He imagined her dead and punished himself for it. He imagined her alive and not coming and hated himself worse.

His chest hurt.

Mala touched his sleeve, briefly. She was not a woman who wasted contact.

"If she's your sister," she said, "she's trying."

He laughed once, and it broke. "You don't know her."

"I know you. Stubborn breeds familiar."

The dark gathered fully.

Behind them, in the lockhouse, Bel was teaching the children how to make three copies from one note without changing the sense. Jaro was pretending not to listen and correcting them anyway. Someone had put a pot on. The north chain cooled in the air. The water below the lock gave another slow wrong pulse, and Arin felt it through the boards under his boots.

He went back inside and wrote it down.

*Second pulse after dark. Boards felt it.*

Then, after a moment:

*We are still watching.*

He looked at the sentence, embarrassed by it and unwilling to strike it out.

Bel saw it.

She did not smile. That was kind of her.

"Send?" she asked.

"Tomorrow."

"And if no one answers?"

He looked toward the dark window, where the lake lay black beyond the reflection of their small lit room: children at the table, Jaro's uneven seal, Mala's bent hands, wet notes drying near the stove.

"Then we send again," Arin said.

Outside, the gate remained dark. The lake did not answer in any language he trusted.

Inside, Bel copied the line, Jaro objected to the word *still* and lost, and Mala fell asleep in the chair nearest the stove with the expression of a woman who considered rest a tactical victory over fools.

Arin sat at the table and sharpened three more pieces of charcoal for the morning.

He did not know that Lena was coming.

He knew only the lake, and the children's moths, and Jaro's hip, and Mala's backward eddy, and the west pilot's green light, and the empty gate above town.

So he stayed awake a little longer, listening to the wrong water move under the boards, and when it moved again, he wrote that down too.
