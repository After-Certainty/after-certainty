# Chapter 14 — No One Erased It

Lower Vellum did not look like a place where records went to die.

She had expected dust, silence, sealed shelves, the same dry lunar hush as the retention annex above. Instead the lift opened into noise.

Not chaos. Work.

A long under-city had been cut into the anchor-rock beneath the archive moon's main dock, a service settlement grown around copy-houses, binding rooms, repair stalls, and the small stubborn economies that gather under institutions which forget they depend on hands. Near the lift an ink kitchen spilled heat into the passage; two apprentices argued over whether the black batch was too thin to survive damp storage, while a third stirred without looking up. Beyond them, sheets hung dripping in a paper bath until they would take ink without buckling. The ceiling hung low in places and very high in others, depending on which century had done the cutting. Heated air moved in damp breaths from the paper rooms, warmer than the archive above and smelling of starch, vinegar, boiled fiber, and glue.

Children carried drying boards wider than their shoulders. Two men argued over whether a cracked atlas should be repaired according to the second binding or the first, because the first was original but the second was the one everyone had used. At a bench near the lift, a girl no older than thirteen scraped old glue from a spine with such severity that Lena straightened as she passed.

Toma moved through it with the offended ease of a man returning to a place that knew him too well.

"Don't touch anything," he said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to look like you might."

"That's different."

"In Vellum it is not."

He kept the folio tucked beneath one arm, one hand pressed flat over it. Since leaving the deaccession room, he had become less theatrical. Cheer was armor. This was attention.

They passed a shop where three old women sat under a bright lamp copying names from a warped memorial slate onto narrow strips of white fiber. One read the stone. One repeated each name. The third wrote. After every seventh name they traded roles, not speaking except to correct a letter.

Lena slowed without meaning to.

Toma did not look back. "If you stop for every act of resistance down here, we'll die before the first stair."

They turned into a narrower passage where the walls were papered with notices: missing ledgers, apprentice hours, funeral meal, copied map disputed, do not boil hide glue in the east kitchen after second bell, *Veya says this means you.*

Toma stopped before that last notice.

"She still writes like a hammer," he said, and knocked.

The door opened before the knock finished.

Veya Sol was small and old and very straight, with silver hair braided close to her skull and hands so clean they looked ceremonial. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow. Her forearms were scarred with old burns, pale against brown skin. She wore no badge. She needed none. The room behind her had arranged itself around her the way a good crew arranges itself around a competent pilot: not by deference exactly, but by unconscious orientation.

She looked at Toma first.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard the question."

"I've heard your footstep."

"That seems unfair."

"It is efficient. Go away."

"I brought a courier."

Veya's eyes moved to Lena.

Lena felt the strange discomfort of being read by someone who was not guessing. Veya took in the pack, the dead scanner panel under her arm, the worn cuff where the duty-receipt still rode, the way she stood with her weight ready to move. Then she looked at Toma's arm over the folio.

Her face changed.

Not much. A tightening at the mouth. A grief so practiced it did not need room.

"You opened the unlabeled cabinet," she said.

"It was unlabeled."

"That was the label."

"Then it was badly labeled."

"You are a vandal with filing privileges."

"I brought it to you instead of copying it badly in secret."

"That is the only sentence saving you from my kettle."

Toma looked past her shoulder. "Is the kettle hot?"

"Always."

"Then we accept judgment indoors."

Veya held the doorway another moment. Then she stepped aside.

Her workshop was smaller than the noise outside had suggested. Nothing in it was stored as if storage were the point. Everything was either being used, drying after use, or waiting its turn.

A long table stood under three lamps. On it lay a map in pieces, each fragment weighted with smooth stones. Along one wall, shelves of repaired books — stitches visible, patches matched for strength rather than beauty. Near the back, beside a stove with a kettle steaming on it, a row of children's copy-slates leaned against the wall. Each bore the same sentence in careful practice hands.

*A copy is not a replacement. It is a promise to answer.*

Lena looked at it too long.

Veya noticed. "Children copy what adults stopped meaning."

"That sentence yours?"

"No. My grandmother's. Which means yes, eventually."

Toma set the folio on the long table, both hands now, careful despite himself.

"We found the transfer papers," he said. "Local Return Functions to Central Assurance. The objections. Veya Marren. Veya Sol's chair inventory. The knife."

Veya did not move toward the folio. She looked at it the way a person looks at a grave she already visits.

"You didn't find it," she said. "It was where I put it."

"In my deaccession overflow."

"In the only room upstairs nobody important understands well enough to clean properly."

"That is not an answer I enjoy."

"It was not designed for your enjoyment."

"You hid original closure documents in an unlabeled cabinet inside an annex scheduled for pulping."

"I hid them in a place whose neglect was stable."

Toma opened his mouth, closed it, and looked almost proud against his will.

Lena stepped closer to the table. "You know what they are."

Veya looked at her again. "Yes."

"They closed the offices."

"Yes."

"They took the local returns and the stayer reports and folded them into central review."

"Yes."

"They made the network blind."

Veya's eyes flickered then. Not away. Down, briefly, to the folio. "No."

Toma made an irritated sound. "Veya."

"No," she said again, and this time the word carried the whole room with it. "That is the story a person tells when she has finally found a document large enough for her anger."

Lena felt heat rise in her face.

"I'm not looking for a story."

"Everyone says that at the beginning of one."

Toma leaned both hands on the table. "The memorandum is plain. Local Return work was transferred. The forms were changed. Answer received disappears. Stayer Notices reclassified as folklore. You kept the papers because you knew."

"I kept the papers because my grandmother kept the papers."

"Because she knew."

"Because she was angry."

"At what?"

"At the closure. At the stupidity. At the arrogance. At the way men in clean rooms use the word *efficiency* when what they mean is *I do not want to be inconvenienced by what you know.*" Veya's voice remained level. "She was right to be angry. So was her mother. So am I. So are you, though you make uglier furniture of it."

Toma accepted this as tribute.

"But no one erased it," Veya said.

Lena heard them and did not understand them. Or understood them and refused.

"No," she said.

Veya turned to her fully.

"They erased the offices," Lena said. "The ledgers stopped answering. The forms changed. The chairs were denied storage. There's a document."

"Yes."

"How is that not erasure?"

"Because erasure is a hand with intention, and this was worse."

Toma went still.

Veya finally reached for the folio. She untied it without looking down. She did not open to the memorandum. She passed that, and the objections, and the transfer lists, and the chair inventory, until she found a page near the back Lena had not seen.

It was a schedule.

Not dramatic. Not secret. A maintenance schedule for acknowledgment traffic among local return rooms. Columns of places, dates, expected reply times, assigned clerks. The handwriting in the margins was dense, practical, annoyed.

Veya tapped a line.

"Read that."

Lena leaned over.

*Return acknowledgment delayed due to staff reassignment. Temporary manual forwarding authorized pending central integration.*

"Now here."

*Manual forwarding burden exceeds local capacity. Recommend reduction of non-critical seasonal observations until consolidation completes.*

"Here."

*Observation packets retained locally pending clarification of new priority schema.*

"Here."

*No reply received. Resent. No reply received. Resent. No reply received.*

The marks continued down the page, shrinking as the writer grew tired.

Veya turned another sheet. Same office family, later year. Fewer entries. Another. A printed notice.

*Local offices may discontinue duplicate acknowledgment where central receipt has been issued.*

"The killing sentence," Toma said softly.

"One of them," Veya said. "There were many."

Lena stared at the page. It did not look like murder. It looked like a rule invented by someone whose desk had too much on it.

"Central receipt," she said.

"Yes."

"But central receipt didn't answer the local observer."

"No."

"So the local office stopped knowing whether anyone downstream had heard."

"Yes."

"And downstream stopped knowing whether the first office was still watching."

"Yes."

"And everyone thought—"

"That someone else was still paying attention." Veya closed the folio with one hand. "Exactly."

*Someone else was still paying attention.*

The most familiar sentence in the world. Every passenger at Vesper had trusted some version of it. Oyo at Khoury. Jun behind his curve. Lena herself, stepping through doors she called routes.

"They stopped replying," Veya said. "Then they stopped expecting replies. Then they stopped noticing they had stopped expecting them. That is not erasure. That is abandonment made administrative."

Toma's face had gone hard, but not in disagreement.

"The central offices still closed the rooms," he said.

"Yes."

"They still made the forms worse."

"Yes."

"They still decided local reports were noise."

"Yes."

"Then there is guilt."

"Of course there is guilt." Veya looked almost offended. "Do not mistake me for a forgiving woman. There is guilt in every signature that called return work duplicate. There is guilt in my grandmother's supervisor, who told her to stop sending packets after second notice. There is guilt in her, too, because eventually she did stop." She laid her palm flat on the folio. "But guilt is not the same as a culprit. And if you go looking only for a culprit, you will misunderstand the body."

The kettle began to hiss. Veya crossed to it, poured three cups without asking whether anyone wanted tea. The tea was dark and bitter. Toma accepted his like a man accepting a legal penalty. Lena took hers because refusing would have required knowing what she wanted instead.

Veya sat at the table. She did not invite them to sit. Toma did. Lena followed.

The chair was too low. Or she was too tired. Sitting made the ache in her legs arrive all at once.

"You think I want a culprit," Lena said.

"I think you want the world to have failed in a way that gives you a door."

Lena's hand tightened around the cup.

Toma looked between them and wisely chose, for once, silence.

"I have a brother past the dark," Lena said.

"I know."

"How?"

"You walk like someone whose destination has become an accusation."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the truest one. But Toma also told me while you were staring at the memorial copyists."

Toma did not apologize. "Context seemed useful."

"My brother is in the Tarn," Lena said. "The roads keep closing. Every straight path goes dark before I reach it. At Borren I put twenty-six people on a boat because I thought the route was live, and somewhere past the dark gate there were people with a list who already knew it wasn't." The cup in her hands trembled once. She made it stop. "So yes. If someone closed the rooms that would have carried that list, if someone made the network unable to know what the people at the edges knew, I would like that someone to have a name."

Veya's face changed then, not softening exactly. Grief moved through it, quick and deep.

"So did my grandmother," Veya said.

She rose and went to the shelf behind the stove. From it she took a small box of pale wood darkened by years of touch. She brought it back and set it between them.

Inside were strips of paper, each one folded once.

"Her family was a return family," Veya said. "Three generations on outer acknowledgments. They lived by reply marks. Her mother could tell a corridor was sick by which stations stopped answering in pairs."

She unfolded one strip.

"My grandmother was a child when the office closed. Old enough to remember the sound of it."

"The sound?"

"Rooms have sounds. Your brother would know that, if he keeps water."

Lena looked up sharply.

Veya continued as though she had not noticed. "The return room sounded like paper moving with purpose. People reading aloud. Clerks arguing over whether a silence counted as a response. After consolidation, the room got quieter every season. Work remained, then less work, then work waiting for central receipts, then only copies of things nobody would answer." She looked at the strip in her hand. "My grandmother said the worst day was not the closure. The worst day was three years before, when she realized everyone was still coming to the room out of habit, but no one expected the room to change anything anymore."

She handed the strip to Lena.

It read, in a child's careful hand:

*Today the north desk did not shout once.*

Lena swallowed.

"She kept those?"

"Her childish records. The old women upstairs are not the only ones who write names before water takes them."

Toma had gone very still beside her. His tea sat untouched.

Veya unfolded another.

*Father says central will send the replies together. Mother says together is late by another name.*

Another.

*The chair by the map window is gone. They said no storage value. I took one screw.*

Veya reached into the box and drew out, from beneath the paper strips, a tiny dark screw no longer than Lena's thumbnail. She set it on the table.

There was the chair.

Not in the inventory. Not in the denied storage request. Here, in a little wooden box, the part small enough to hide.

Lena felt something in her chest shift, painfully.

"That's why you kept the folio," she said.

"No." Veya put the screw back. "That is why I did not burn it."

"What's the difference?"

"Keeping is active. Not burning can be done for a hundred years by people too sad to decide."

Veya closed the box and rested both hands on it.

"My grandmother grew up believing someone had stolen the work. Her mother believed it until she died. Whole institutions gone from the record. Of course they thought purge, standing close to the wound."

"Toma thought it."

"Toma enjoys sensible conclusions too much."

"I object to *enjoys.*"

"Noted."

Veya ignored him. "For years, my grandmother looked for the true order. The one that said *stop listening to the edge.* She found budgets. Transfer notes. Reclassification schedules. Complaints that local observers sent too much noise. Complaints that central offices never answered. Arrogance. Laziness. Exhaustion. Offices protecting themselves from work they could not measure. She found no knife."

"Maybe she didn't find it," Toma said.

Veya looked at him with real affection and real impatience. "I love archivists because they think a missing document proves there must be a document."

"It often does."

"And sometimes it proves only that a thing happened without ever becoming a sentence."

Silence moved through the room.

Outside, through the half-open door, someone laughed in the corridor. Then a scolding voice told them not to run with wet sheets.

Veya turned back to Lena.

"No one erased it. They simply stopped doing it."

Lena looked down at the table.

The sentence was less satisfying than erasure. It gave her nowhere to put her anger except everywhere.

Toma's erasure story had documents. Closed offices. Signatures. It was true enough to grip. Veya's grief made it larger and colder.

"What do you do with that?" Lena asked.

"That is the question people ask when they realize blame will not repair the roof."

"And?"

"And then most of them go back to blame, because the roof is high."

Toma snorted softly despite himself.

Veya opened the folio again, but not to the closure documents. She withdrew a loose page tucked near the back. A teaching copy.

At the top, in a strong hand, someone had written:

*Return work requires three motions.*

Below it:

*Observe.*
*Send.*
*Answer.*

Veya turned the page around so Lena could read it.

"People remember the first two," she said. "Even now. Your marsh-watchers observe. They send. Jun did too, barely. You observe until it breaks you, and you send when someone is close enough to hear." Her finger tapped the third line. "The old offices died here. Not because no one saw. Not because no one spoke. Because answer became optional."

Lena stared at the three words.

*Observe. Send. Answer.*

They were too plain. Almost childish. Like the copy-slates near the wall. *A copy is not a replacement. It is a promise to answer.*

She thought of the lists moving hand to hand among people who never traveled. *So the next one knows what to watch.* Observe and send. Not yet the third motion, at least not far enough to reach Tellan before she cleared the *Wren.*

She thought of Jun's pages folded behind the scanner. He had sent. She had never answered.

"What counts as answer?" she asked.

Veya's eyes sharpened, and Lena realized she had asked something that mattered.

"Not agreement," Veya said. "That was the center's mistake. It wanted confirmation — reliable, unreliable, priority, non-critical. Answer is simpler and harder. Received. Compared. Corrected. Carried onward. Sometimes *no.* Sometimes *we saw it too.* Sometimes *do not send this again until the second sign.* Without answer, the first observer never learns what their seeing became, and the next never learns what came before."

Lena let out a breath that was almost a laugh and not at all one.

"My whole life was send without answer," she said.

"Courier?"

"Cargo."

"Ah." Veya nodded. "The respectable version."

"I took a packet from one place to another. Got a signature. Sometimes not even that. I thought delivery was the point."

"Delivery is important."

"But not the same as answer."

"No."

"What do you want from me?" Lena asked.

Veya took the question seriously.

"Nothing."

"That's unlikely."

"I did not say there was nothing I should want. I said wanting from couriers has historically disappointed my family."

Toma raised a finger. "In fairness, wanting from institutions also—"

"Be quiet."

He did.

Veya folded the teaching page and returned it to the folio.

"You came here following records because you believed the missing records would tell you who closed the world. They do not. They tell you how the world learned to close without feeling its own fingers on the latch." She retied the folio. "That is less useful for anger and more useful for grief."

"I have enough grief."

"No. You have grief looking for anger to wear."

Lena stood too abruptly. The chair scraped back. In the weaker gravity the motion nearly carried her farther than she meant, and she caught herself on the table.

Toma half rose. Veya did not.

"You don't know me," Lena said.

"No."

"You don't know what I've been carrying."

"No."

"My brother is in the Tarn. I don't know if he's alive. I don't know if he thinks I didn't try. I don't know if every road I take is making me later. I don't know how many people I've killed by being useful in the wrong way." The words came faster now, and she hated that they did. "So forgive me if I'd like one part of this to have a name. One person. One office. One hand that did the thing, so I can stop finding another room full of frightened people and old forms and somebody saying *yes, but.*"

Veya looked up at her.

For a moment there was no instruction in her face at all. Only sadness with memory in it.

"My son died because a reply did not come," she said.

Lena went still.

Toma looked away. Not surprised. Respectful.

"He was twenty-eight," Veya said. "A paper-runner. Too curious, too proud of his feet. There was a mold outbreak in the lower east stacks. Small thing. A local thing. One copy-house saw it first — green thread in the binding glue, not dangerous unless heated. They sent a notice upward. The intake marked it non-critical because the report came without central verification. The copy-house sent again. No answer. They sealed their own room and waited. Two days later, the heating vents carried spores through three workshops, including his. He was binding old fever records. He breathed enough of it before anyone knew to put masks on."

She folded her hands.

"The paper trail is very clear. No villain. No cover-up. No one wanted my son dead. The first clerk followed priority schema. The second was covering two desks because staffing had been reduced. The copy-house did not know who else to tell after the second no answer. The maintenance office had stopped reading non-critical notices unless they came through central. Central had no live category for local mold until it became facility-wide. Everyone behaved understandably. My son is dead."

Lena could not speak.

"So when I tell you no one erased it, I am not being gentle. I am telling you the crueler thing. A culprit would have been a mercy. I would have liked to hate one person properly. Instead I had to learn how much death fits inside ordinary failure."

The kettle clicked as it cooled.

Outside the room, someone called for more thread. Someone answered. The answering voice traveled down the hall and back.

Lena sat down again.

This time carefully.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"Yes," Veya said. "You are."

It was not absolution. It was receipt.

Toma reached for his tea and drank half of it in one swallow.

After a while, Lena said, "Then the gates going dark now—"

"Same failure," Veya said. "Only louder."

"The network did not fail because one office closed two hundred years ago." Lena looked toward the copy-slates. "The office closed because people had already begun treating local variation as noise until confirmed, and reply as waste once receipt could be logged."

"Folklore," Lena said.

"Yes. Folklore is often knowledge whose office was closed."

Toma whispered, "That is excellent."

Veya ignored him again. "The gates kept working, so the loss looked harmless. Every year the absence did not kill anyone important became evidence that it did not matter."

"Until it did."

"Until people were dying inside unanswered notices and calling it ordinary weather."

"Can it be restarted?" Lena asked.

Veya's eyes sharpened again, then cooled deliberately.

"No."

"Not like that," Veya said. "The old return offices are gone. The people are gone. The trust is gone. The forms are dead, and even if we rebuilt them, no one would know why to answer them. Institutions do not come back because a courier has found a folio and a conscience."

Toma looked personally wounded by the attack on folios.

"But something is happening," Lena said. "The lists."

"Yes."

"They're local returns."

"They are what people invent when the need outlives the office."

"Then—"

"No." Veya's voice was not sharp, but it stopped her anyway. "Do not name it too quickly. Naming can kill a thing as surely as silence. The lists are not an office. They are not salvation. They are frightened people doing the next right motion because no one taught them to wait for permission. That makes them precious. It also makes them fragile."

*Observe. Send. Answer.*

"What's missing?" she asked.

Veya regarded her for a long moment. "You already know."

"Answer."

"Yes."

"How?"

"If I knew that, I would be out saving civilization instead of teaching children to copy dead wisdom on bad slates."

Toma coughed. "To be fair, your slates are very good."

"They are adequate."

"They are better than adequate."

"Your standards are lunar."

Lena almost smiled again. This time she let herself.

It vanished quickly.

Veya rose and went to the copy-slates. She picked one up and brought it to Lena. The practice hand was uneven but legible.

*A copy is not a replacement. It is a promise to answer.*

"My apprentices hate this sentence," Veya said. "They think copying makes another first thing. It does not. A copy answers the first thing: you reached me, and I will carry you differently but not alone." She set the slate in front of Lena. "You tell me what you see. I tell you what I see beside it. Without that, every warning becomes weather. Then someone like Varik offers one answer large enough to end the loneliness."

Lena looked up.

"You know him?"

"No. I know his shape. The world produces men like that when answer fails."

"He helps people."

"I imagine he does. That makes him possible."

Lena sat with that.

"What do I do with this?" she asked.

The folio. The slate. The sentence. All of it.

Veya took the question more gently than before.

"You carry it badly at first. Everyone does."

"I'm serious."

"So am I."

"I have to keep moving north."

"Yes."

"I have to reach Arin."

"Yes."

"And now I have this." She tapped the slate. "Observe, send, answer. Lists that aren't enough. Offices that won't come back. No culprit. No plan."

"Good."

"That is your answer to everything?"

"No. It is my answer to people who think a true diagnosis owes them immediate usefulness." Veya returned to her chair.

Toma groaned softly. "You see what she's like."

"I do."

"And yet you asked."

Lena looked at the folio. "Can I take a copy?"

"Yes."

Toma sat upright. "I said half a day."

"I heard you say it. I did not agree to make it true." Veya went to a drawer and removed a packet wrapped in plain cloth. She untied it and revealed pages already copied in a firm black hand: the memorandum, Veya Marren's objection, the reply schedule, the teaching sheet, several pages of Local Returns with answer marks intact, and the chair inventory, including the denial.

Toma stared at it.

"You already copied them."

"Of course."

"You let me carry the original down here under threat and insult when you already had copies."

"I wanted to know which parts made you angry."

"That is monstrous."

"It was informative."

"You manipulated me with archival theater."

"Yes."

Lena found herself laughing once, short and unwilling and real enough that it hurt her ribs.

Veya looked pleased, but only briefly.

She tied the copied packet and handed it to Lena.

"Not proof," she said.

"Memory."

"And obligation."

The packet weighed almost nothing. Lena took it with both hands.

"What obligation?"

"To answer it."

"How?"

"You tell me."

Lena almost protested, but the words would not form. She looked at the copy-slates, the screw in its box, the folio that did not prove what Toma wanted.

"I don't know yet," Lena said.

Veya nodded.

"That is the first honest answer anyone gives the dead."

She stood then. They were leaving. Or being dismissed.

At the door, Toma lingered.

"I was not entirely wrong," he said.

Veya touched his cheek, unexpectedly, with two fingers. "No. You were wrong in the direction of a truth. It is the family talent of archivists."

He blinked and became very interested in the corridor.

Lena stepped out after him. The under-city's noise met them: presses, voices, paper, the ordinary clatter of people keeping things from vanishing.

Behind her, Veya said, "Courier."

Lena turned.

"If you reach your brother," Veya said, "do not begin by telling him what you found."

"What should I begin with?"

Veya's face softened.

"Answer what he has been sending you all these years."

Then she closed the door.

Lena stood in the passage with the copied packet in her pack and the dead panel under her arm. Arin had been sending her things for years, though not in the way couriers counted: mud on boots, complaints about fast roads, warnings about skiffs and fog and gates that made people forget the weight of distance. *Next time, Lena. Stay long enough to learn the place.* She had treated those as brotherly weather, familiar and inconvenient and easy to move through.

Maybe she had never answered.

Toma began walking toward the lift, slower now.

"She is wrong about the lack of culprit," he said after a while.

"Is she?"

"Yes. Also right. Infuriatingly common in Sol women."

Lena adjusted the packet in her pack so it would not bend. "Does it matter?"

"Of course it matters."

"I mean right now."

Toma considered that. "Right now, it matters that you do not let her grief make you gentle with the people who signed those forms."

"I don't feel gentle."

"Good."

"I also don't feel certain."

"Less good, but fashionable."

They reached the lift. A boy came out carrying a tray of wet pages, walking carefully, eyes fierce with the importance of not dropping what he had been trusted to carry. Toma stepped aside without mockery. Lena did too.

When the lift doors closed, Vellum's under-city disappeared behind metal, but the sound of it seemed to remain: scrape, press, call, answer.

The lift rose.

Toma watched the floor numbers with exaggerated resentment. "Where now?"

"North."

"Every fool's favorite direction."

"Not straight north. I need Ivera first."

"Gate mechanic?"

"Gate keeper. Or something like one. Edrin mentioned them at Vesper. Jun's pages point through the old return line. If the local fixes kept working after the offices died, someone there would know."

"And if they refuse?"

"Everyone refuses."

"They may know what people changed locally and never logged."

Toma looked at her. "You heard Veya say no institutions will come back because you found a folio."

"I heard her."

"And yet?"

"And yet if no one erased it, then no one can simply restore it by finding the eraser." Lena watched the lift lights climb. "But people still did pieces of the work. Gates kept running. Local fixes. Lists. If the old answer died, maybe some of the motions survived without the name."

Toma's expression sharpened.

"Observe," he said.

"Send."

"Answer."

The lift slowed.

Lena held the dead panel tighter against her side. For once it did not feel only dead.

The doors opened onto the dry, low-lit corridor of the archive moon.

*No one erased it.*

*They simply stopped doing it.*

Lena stepped out of the lift carrying the worse truth, the copied packet, and the obligation Veya had placed in her hands.

For a little while, that was enough to move by.
