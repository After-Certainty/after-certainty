# Chapter 13 — The Slope

Toma had learned not to answer urgent queries on the day they arrived.

Urgency was a weather pattern on other worlds. On the Archive Moon it was a letter that sat in the queue until the person asking had either solved the problem themselves or learned to ask a better question.

Cai's query had come through three forwarding chains—keeper supervisor, regional coordination, guild liaison—before reaching his desk with **URGENT** in capitals, which meant someone downstream had panicked and someone upstream had not read the archive's response-time charter.

**REQUEST — HISTORICAL KEEPER ROSTERS — RELAY GUILDS**

**FROM: CAI** (First Gate approach, maintenance keeper, mark code verified)

**NOTE: Historical rosters may show names we've stopped paying.**

Toma read Cai's note twice.

Keepers rarely wrote like historians. Historians rarely climbed scaffolds. The combination interested him.

He set the query in the Tuesday slot—not dismissal, sequencing—and went back to the catalog he had been working on for eleven months: oral testimony from a relay guild on a world that no longer had a name anyone pronounced the same way twice.

Preservation was his independent goal.

Not answers. Not speed.

Records before they became rumors.

---

The Archive Moon kept its oldest stacks beneath three kilometers of stone.

Toma worked in the upper memory gardens when he wanted light and the deep vaults when he wanted honesty. Light made you think civilization was continuous. Stone made you think in gaps.

He took the tram down after lunch, carrying only the credentials that opened doors slower than people wished and faster than institutions deserved.

Vault 14 held labor rosters—guild memberships, stipend ledgers, the unglamorous accounting of who had been paid to remember which cables mattered when storms rolled through.

Relay guilds had never been famous.

That was the point.

Famous institutions left statues. Relay guilds left schedules.

Toma found the First Gate approach guild first because Cai's mark code had pointed there, and because keepers who still cut dots in housing seals deserved the courtesy of being looked up in the order they asked.

The roster for **Year 218** was intact.

**Year 219** had a hole six names wide.

Not a fire. Not a war. A budget line struck through in administrative ink with a note in the margin: **CONSOLIDATED UNDER VENDOR CONTRACT 7-A.**

Toma photographed the page the way he photographed everything—twice, with scale marker—and felt no surprise.

Institutions did not usually erase people loudly.

They stopped paying them and waited for the silence to become normal.

---

He might have stopped there and sent Cai a polite extract with three citations and a recommendation to file a contemporary staffing appeal through proper channels.

That would have been procedurally correct.

It would also have been a lie about what the hole meant.

Toma had seen holes like this before—in colony collapse records, in failed oversight networks, in the slow attrition papers Veya called *the quiet purges*, though she meant erosion, not conspiracy.

He pulled the adjacent ledgers.

**Year 217:** forty-two names on the approach guild roster.

**Year 220:** eleven names, four marked *interim*.

**Year 225:** vendor contract only; keeper column blank.

The gap was not one decision.

It was a slope.

He sat on the cold bench outside Vault 14 and dictated a note to himself in the measured voice he used for oral archives:

*"Relay guild attrition on First Gate approach appears gradual, administrative, and precedes current gate anomalies by decades. Urgent query likely reflects present fear projected onto past record. Present fear still valid. Past record still relevant. Do not conflate."*

That last sentence was for him.

He conflated when he was tired.

---

The tram back to the gardens took twenty minutes.

Halfway up, Veya sent a message from the language stacks—she lived in lost grammars the way other people lived in cities.

**VEYA:** Heard you got an URGENT from a dot man.

**TOMA:** Tuesday slot.

**VEYA:** You'll answer Tuesday.

**TOMA:** I'll answer Tuesday.

**VEYA:** You'll answer Tuesday and include something from the Meridian collapse because you can't help yourself.

Toma smiled despite himself.

Veya was wrong about hope and often right about him.

---

He did not answer on Tuesday.

He answered on Wednesday, because Tuesday he'd found the Meridian parallel she'd predicted—a relay oversight network that had thinned across forty years until a corridor failure everyone called sudden.

The Meridian records were not metaphor. They were procedure written in another century's hand: oversight circles defunded in increments small enough to survive audit, vendor contracts thick enough to hide absence, public bulletins that announced *modernization* when the honest word would have been *fewer eyes*.

Toma photographed Meridian page by page and felt the slope repeat under his fingers like a texture.

Different world.

Same grammar of forgetting.

He spent Tuesday afternoon in Vault 11 pulling oversight minutes—not because the minutes would change page nine, but because minutes sometimes told you what people feared saying in summaries.

**Year 203:** *Recommend guild roster review.*

**Year 208:** *Recommend roster review — deferred pending vendor trial.*

**Year 214:** *Trial successful; recommend contract extension.*

**Year 219:** six names struck through on a different shelf in a different vault, same hand, same ink patience.

Nobody had written *fire the keepers*.

They had written *modernize* until modernize meant nobody home.

Toma photographed each page until his scale marker felt like a ritual object. Archivists joked about his duplicates. Duplicates were how you proved you hadn't imagined a slope when someone demanded a villain later.

In Vault 14's climate hush, time moved in ledger years. The cold bench outside held the kind of silence that made present fear sound loud in your own head—which was why he sat there to dictate notes, to hear the conflation before it reached his mouth.

*"Do not conflate,"* he told the bench.

The bench did not argue.

---

Wednesday evening he dictated the Meridian parallel into the packet with the care of a man describing weather to someone who only believed in lightning.

Forty years of thinning.

A corridor failure everyone called sudden.

Post-event bulletins full of surprise verbs.

Pre-event tables full of small percentages nobody wanted to headline.

Toma knew which pages future readers would quote.

He numbered them anyway.

---

Thursday he met Courier Vance in the east wing and gave her page one.

He watched her jaw set—the courier weather he'd flagged on the manifest—and recognized the shape of someone who would read page nine without permission if he left it on a bench.

He did not leave page nine on a bench.

Page nine was a list, not a story. Lists made fear efficient.

She'd looked like Northhook—jaw set, compass rose on her shoulder, the particular fatigue of a person who had changed **TRACKING** to **COMMITTED** and discovered commitment did not open gates.

He'd seen couriers like her before. Not often. Often enough.

They arrived during absences because absence felt like insult to people who moved for a living.

They left either quieter or louder.

Rarely wiser on schedule.

---

Friday morning he returned to Vault 14 with better light and worse coffee.

The cold bench outside the vault had become his office for roster work. Archivists joked about it. Toma preferred stone to gardens when numbers lied politely.

He pulled Pilgrim Road junction guild samples for comparison—not because Cai had asked, but because patterns rarely respected gate theology.

Junction rosters from Years 216–224 showed the same slope at a gentler angle: fewer names, more *interim*, more vendor language where guild language used to be.

Not purge.

Not conspiracy.

Ink patience.

On the bench he opened a message from Cai—short, keeper-plain:

*Hum steady. Marks multiplying. Supervisor says visual confirmation sufficient. I say insufficient. Send what you have.*

Toma typed back: *Wednesday. Packet en route. Do not let supervisor conflate visual with checked.*

Cai's reply was one line: *Too late for that. Send anyway.*

---

The report he assembled for Cai was twelve pages long.

Page one was the extract Cai needed: names, dates, the budget strike-through, the vendor consolidation line.

Page four was the Meridian comparison Toma knew Cai would not have time to read.

Page nine was a list of institutions whose ledgers showed the same slope—relay guilds, junction keepers, oversight circles—stopped paying, stopped naming, stopped expecting anyone to notice.

Page twelve said:

*"If your supervisor ignores this, do not assume malice. Assume habit. Habits are harder to fight and easier to document. I am documenting."*

He did not write *purge*.

He did not write *conspiracy*.

Those words arrived later, in other people's mouths, when fear needed a face.

---

Before sending, he walked the packet to Veya's stacks—not for approval, for friction.

She read page nine standing up, as if sitting would commit her to comfort.

"You're going to make the dot man famous," she said.

"I'm going to make the dot man informed."

"Same tragedy." She tapped the Meridian section. "You couldn't resist."

"Parallel is not prophecy."

"No. It's grammar." Veya set the page down. "Someone will read this list and hear footsteps in a corridor at night."

"Then they should read page twelve."

"They'll read page nine first. You numbered it that way on purpose."

Toma did not deny it. Lists belonged in the middle where impatient hands found them.

Veya handed the packet back. "Courier Vance was in my corridor yesterday asking about lost languages."

"She's asking about her brother."

"Everyone asks about their brother eventually. The archive answers in slopes." A dry pause. "Send your packet. I'll be here when she misreads it."

Veya had misread things too, once—early career, a colony purge theory that made a good paper and a bad prediction. Toma had watched her learn erosion the hard way, verb by verb. He did not mention that. Veya did not need reminding. Her stacks were the reminder.

---

He sent the packet to Cai at midday.

The confirmation chimed with the soft bureaucratic politeness of a system that treated twelve pages of institutional decay like freight.

Toma returned to the memory garden where new deposits were indexed before descending to stone—recent records treated with the tenderness usually reserved for the old.

A courier manifest crossed his screen in the public feed: **LENA VANCE — ARCHIVE MOON — PROVISIONAL — EXTENDED**.

Not his business yet.

He flagged it anyway.

People who traveled toward archives during absences were either lost or beginning to be useful.

Sometimes both.

---

At dusk he ate in the garden cafeteria with wind in the hollow mountains and vault climate hum in his teeth—another thing keepers maintained without applause.

He thought about Cai on a scaffold, trusting a hum.

He thought about names struck through in ink that had outlived everyone who'd held the pen.

He thought about the slope, which was not dramatic enough for bulletins and too consistent to be accident.

Tomorrow he would start Vault 9 in earnest—junction guilds on the Pilgrim Road, because Lena's question about who checked cables when storms rolled through was, annoyingly, a good question asked at the wrong speed.

Tomorrow someone would read page one and panic, page four and argue, page nine and misread.

Tonight he stacked his notes in order, labeled the box **FIRST GATE — ROSTER ATTRITION**, and felt the particular calm of a person who had turned urgency into work he knew how to do.

Before sleep he opened the Vault 9 request queue—junction guilds, Pilgrim Road, the place patterns went when gate theology failed. A cooperative deposit had arrived from Two Valleys: oral story, child slate, public roster update. Not his shelf. Not yet. He flagged it for cross-reference anyway.

Patterns rarely respected gate theology.

They also rarely respected pilgrim schedules.

He wrote one line in his personal log before sleep—not for Cai, not for Lena, for the stone:

*Courier Vance will misread page nine within the week. Veya will scold. Jun will publish something. Cai will keep cutting dots. Document anyway.*

The names had waited decades.

They could wait until morning.

He wasn't sure that was enough either—but unlike Cai, Toma's doubt sounded like patience, and patience was how archives outlasted the people who needed them.
