# Chapter 13 — The Slope

Toma had learned not to answer urgent queries on the day they arrived.

Urgency was a weather pattern on other worlds. On the Archive Moon it was a letter that sat in the queue until the person asking had either solved the problem themselves or learned to ask a better question.

Cai's query had come through three forwarding chains—keeper supervisor, regional coordination, guild liaison—before reaching his desk with **URGENT** in capitals, which meant someone downstream had panicked and someone upstream had not read the archive's response-time charter.

**REQUEST — HISTORICAL KEEPER ROSTERS — RELAY GUILDS**

**FROM: CAI** (First Gate approach, maintenance keeper, mark code verified)

**NOTE: Historical rosters may show names we've stopped paying.**

Toma read Cai's note twice.

Keepers rarely wrote like historians. Historians rarely climbed scaffolds. The combination interested him.

He set the query in the Tuesday slot—not dismissal, sequencing—and went back to the catalog he had been working on for eleven months: oral testimony from a relay guild on a world that no longer had a name anyone pronounced the same way twice.

Preservation was his independent goal.

Not answers. Not speed.

Records before they became rumors.

---

The Archive Moon kept its oldest stacks beneath three kilometers of stone.

Toma had worked here twenty-two years and had never seen all of it.

That was not humility. It was inventory. Vault corridors branched under hollow mountains like roots under a tree too large for one season of growth—14, 11, 9, each with climate specs, access tiers, and ledgers that outlived the clerks who filed them. He knew Vault 14's labor rosters the way a keeper knew one housing seam: intimately, locally, without claiming the whole scaffold.

The rest was citation by reference. *See adjacent shelf. See Meridian parallel. See Veya stacks for grammar loss.*

He worked in the upper memory gardens when he wanted light and the deep vaults when he wanted honesty. Light made you think civilization was continuous. Stone made you think in gaps.

He took the tram down after lunch, carrying only the credentials that opened doors slower than people wished and faster than institutions deserved.

---

Vault 14 held labor rosters—guild memberships, stipend ledgers, the unglamorous accounting of who had been paid to remember which cables mattered when storms rolled through.

Relay guilds had never been famous.

That was the point.

Famous institutions left statues. Relay guilds left schedules.

Toma found the First Gate approach guild first because Cai's mark code had pointed there, and because keepers who still cut dots in housing seals deserved the courtesy of being looked up in the order they asked.

The cold bench outside Vault 14 was his office when numbers required stone. Archivists joked about it. Toma preferred climate hush to garden wind when ink was the witness.

He photographed the **Year 217** roster first—forty-two names, stipend columns intact, guild seal uncracked.

**Year 218:** forty-two names, three marked *training transfer*, one *injury leave*.

**Year 219:** the hole.

Six names struck through in administrative ink. Margin note: **CONSOLIDATED UNDER VENDOR CONTRACT 7-A.** Not fire. Not war. Budget.

He photographed the page twice with scale marker—duplicate habit, proof against later demands for villains.

**Year 220:** eleven names. Four marked *interim*.

**Year 225:** vendor contract only; keeper column blank.

Forty-two to eleven across eight years on one bench, stone at his back, vault climate drying the air until paper sounded like skin when you turned it.

Toma felt no surprise.

Institutions did not usually erase people loudly.

They stopped paying them and waited for the silence to become normal.

He sat on the cold bench and dictated a note in the measured voice he used for oral archives:

*"Relay guild attrition on First Gate approach appears gradual, administrative, and precedes current gate trouble by decades. Urgent query likely reflects present fear projected onto past record. Present fear still valid. Past record still relevant. Do not conflate."*

That last sentence was for him.

He conflated when he was tired.

---

He might have stopped there and sent Cai a polite extract with three citations and a recommendation to file a contemporary staffing appeal through proper channels.

That would have been procedurally correct.

It would also have been a lie about what the hole meant.

Toma had seen holes like this before—in colony collapse records, in failed oversight networks, in the slow attrition papers Veya called *the quiet purges*, though she meant erosion, not conspiracy.

He pulled the adjacent ledgers.

The gap was not one decision.

It was a slope.

He matched Pilgrim Road junction samples for comparison—Years 216–224, gentler angle, same ink patience—and flagged the box **FIRST GATE — ROSTER ATTRITION** before the tram back to the gardens.

Back on the cold bench he opened the stipend ledger beside the roster—because names without money were only half the record—and traced the slope in numbers:

**Year 217:** forty-two stipend lines, forty-two paid.

**Year 218:** forty-one paid, one *vendor trial — visual inspection only*.

**Year 219:** thirty-six paid, six struck through, five *interim*, vendor line **7-A** appears in margin for the first time.

**Year 220:** eleven paid, four *interim*, nineteen lines absent without strike-through—absence by omission, the quietest grammar.

**Year 225:** zero guild stipends. Vendor contract total only.

Toma photographed each page until his wrists ached. Duplicates were how you proved you hadn't imagined a slope when someone demanded a villain later. Archivists joked about his duplicates. Toma preferred jokes to lawsuits.

He dictated a second note—citation first, interpretation second:

*"Stipend attrition on First Gate approach relay guild precedes roster strike-through by one ledger year (see Year 218 vendor trial line). Suggests budget decision prior to public roster edit. Not covert ops. Not single event. See Meridian parallel Vault 11."*

The bench did not argue.

---

Halfway up, Veya sent a message from the language stacks—she lived in lost grammars the way other people lived in cities.

**VEYA:** Heard you got an URGENT from a keeper on a scaffold.

**TOMA:** Tuesday slot.

**VEYA:** You'll answer Tuesday.

**TOMA:** I'll answer Tuesday.

**VEYA:** You'll answer Tuesday and include something from the Meridian collapse because you can't help yourself.

Toma smiled despite himself.

Veya was wrong about hope and often right about him.

**VEYA:** If you send Meridian to a keeper on a scaffold, he'll think the sky is falling.

**TOMA:** Cai already thinks the sky is humming wrong. Meridian is context.

**VEYA:** Meridian is your favorite ghost.

**TOMA:** Meridian is Item 16.

A pause long enough to count as friction.

**VEYA:** You photographed Item 16 again, didn't you.

**TOMA:** Twice.

**VEYA:** Obsessive.

**TOMA:** Employed.

**VEYA:** Send page one Wednesday. Send page nine when you're ready to be hated. Send page twelve when you're ready to be ignored.

**TOMA:** That's the plan.

**VEYA:** Plans are how historians pretend they control fear.

**TOMA:** Fear is how couriers pretend they control routes.

**VEYA:** Touché. Don't make the keeper famous.

He pocketed the slate and felt the tram rise through stone—the Archive Moon's habit of making depth feel like ascent.

---

He did not answer on Tuesday.

He answered on Wednesday, because Tuesday he'd found the Meridian parallel she'd predicted—a relay oversight network that had thinned across forty years until a corridor failure everyone called sudden.

The Meridian records were not metaphor. They were procedure written in another century's hand: oversight circles defunded in increments small enough to survive audit, vendor contracts thick enough to hide absence, public bulletins that announced *modernization* when the honest word would have been *fewer eyes*.

Toma spent Tuesday afternoon in Vault 11 pulling oversight minutes—not because the minutes would change page nine, but because minutes sometimes told you what people feared saying in summaries.

In the minute book's binding he found a ribbon bookmark—archivist habit from a century ago, silk still intact in vault climate. Someone had marked the meeting where Item 16 would later appear. Toma photographed the bookmark too. Evidence of attention, long ago, insufficient to stop the slope.

**Year 203:** *Recommend guild roster review.*

**Year 208:** *Recommend roster review — deferred pending vendor trial.*

**Year 214:** *Trial successful; recommend contract extension.*

**Year 219:** six names struck through on a different shelf in a different vault, same hand, same ink patience.

Nobody had written *fire the keepers*.

They had written *modernize* until modernize meant nobody home.

---

Wednesday morning he opened the Meridian oversight packet Veya had flagged three years ago—**COLLAPSE — RELAY NETWORK — ADMINISTRATIVE ATTRITION**—and found the minute he always found when someone asked whether slopes could be accidents.

One page. One meeting. Year 207, Meridian Arc oversight circle, appendix C.

The minute was not dramatic. It never was. That was why Toma trusted it.

*Item 14 — Guild stipend review — deferred.*

*Item 15 — Vendor trial extension — approved.*

*Item 16 — Public bulletin language — recommend "modernization" over "reduction."*

*Attendees: 11 present, 4 excused, 2 vacant seats unfilled since Year 204.*

Toma laid his scale marker along the margin and photographed Item 16 twice.

Under his fingers the paper held the kind of minute civilization survived on—no villains, no covert ops grammar, just verbs small enough to audit and large enough to empty a guild over forty years. He read Item 16 a third time without moving his hand. *Modernization* over *reduction.* The pen had pressed lightly. The decision had not.

Different world.

Same grammar of forgetting.

He dictated into his slate: *"Meridian Item 16 (Year 207) recommends public language substitution prior to roster attrition documented Vault 11 shelf 4-C. Parallel to First Gate approach Year 219 strike-through. Not prophecy. Procedure."*

Veya would call that obsessive.

Toma called it citation.

---

In Vault 14's climate hush, time moved in ledger years. The cold bench outside held the kind of silence that made present fear sound loud in your own head—which was why he sat there to dictate notes, to hear the conflation before it reached his mouth.

*"Do not conflate,"* he told the bench.

The bench did not argue.

He pulled Vault 11's Meridian oversight stack next—not because Cai had asked, but because Veya had been right about his habits and right habits deserved evidence. Item 16 was one minute. The stack was forty years. He photographed representative pages the way a physician palpated a spine: looking for repetition, not drama.

**Year 204:** two vacant oversight seats unfilled.

**Year 207:** Item 16 language substitution.

**Year 211:** vendor trial extension without guild counter-review.

**Year 216:** public bulletin *modernization complete* adjacent to roster ledger rows struck through on a shelf three vaults away.

Citation chains, not stories. Toma built them the way keepers built seal logs—one entry per inspection, no skipped intervals, duplicates for the day someone called him a alarmist.

---

Wednesday evening he dictated the Meridian parallel into the packet with the care of a man describing weather to someone who only believed in lightning.

Forty years of thinning.

A corridor failure everyone called sudden.

Post-event bulletins full of surprise verbs.

Pre-event tables full of small percentages nobody wanted to headline.

Toma knew which pages future readers would quote.

He numbered them anyway.

---

Thursday he met Courier Vance in the east wing.

She arrived with Drift dust in her boot seams and a courier bag that had learned to sit upright without being asked—the posture of someone who had changed **TRACKING** to **COMMITTED** and discovered commitment did not open gates.

The east wing smelled like cold tea and stone. Wind worried the terrace louvers. Somewhere below, deposit cases clicked in their rows—the moon's intake rhythm, indifferent to courier jaw set.

"You flagged my manifest," she said before he could greet her.

"I flag everyone who travels toward archives during absences."

"I opened my own query six days before Cai's chain reached you."

Toma looked at her slate dates. "Then you're early for someone who doesn't know Tuesday."

"I'm not early for my brother."

The sentence landed without theater. Toma filed it under *present fear, valid*.

He gave her page one.

She read the first struck-through name and her hand stopped—a physical pause he recognized from twenty years of urgent couriers encountering administrative ink for the first time.

"These are keepers?"

He gave her the answer he'd given a hundred times and meant every time. Budget language. Not criminal language. Someone stopped paying them.

Her jaw set. Northhook weather. Compass rose on her shoulder. The particular fatigue of a person who had been asking routes for a month and had just learned routes were not the only ledger.

"My brother is behind a gate that won't answer."

"I know where Orinreach Basin is."

"Then you know why I didn't come here for a history lesson."

He slid year 218 and year 220 photographs across. Forty-two names. Eleven names. Four *interim*.

"The lesson is the shape," he said. "Not the event."

She asked about page nine.

He closed that door gently. "Page nine is for people who have read pages one through eight in order."

"Jun reads out of order."

"Jun reads for publication. You read for your brother. Different hazards."

She left with the sealed sleeve and the strike-through shock still visible in her shoulders—not defeat, recalibration. Toma had seen couriers like her before. Not often. Often enough.

They arrived during absences because absence felt like insult to people who moved for a living.

They left either quieter or louder.

Rarely wiser on schedule.

He did not leave page nine on a bench.

Page nine was a list, not a story. Lists made fear efficient.

After she left, he stood in the east wing doorway and watched her cross the terrace toward the reading corridor—bag on shoulder, stride courier-fast, body still carrying strike-through shock in the set of her neck.

He'd wanted to say: *Your self-opened query will merge with Cai's by Friday. You will think that means you were right to hurry. It means only that two attentions found the same hole.*

He hadn't said it. Urgent people heard lectures as delay.

Instead he recorded the encounter—**VANCE, LENA — PAGE ONE DELIVERED — CONFLATION RISK HIGH**—and returned to Vault 14 that afternoon to pull junction guild samples before page nine finalized, because lists without comparison shelves were invitations to misread.

The cold bench welcomed him back. Stone remembered slopes longer than people remembered grievances.

He pulled junction samples until the light failed—Years 216–224, Pilgrim Road, Two Valleys spur—and photographed the gentler angle because gentler angles were how institutions defended themselves later: *not us, not sudden, not here.*

Gentler was still slope.

---

Before sending the packet, he walked it to Veya's stacks—not for approval, for friction.

She read page nine standing up, as if sitting would commit her to comfort.

"You're going to make the keeper famous," she said.

"I'm going to make the keeper informed."

"Same tragedy." She tapped the Meridian section. "You couldn't resist."

"Parallel is not prophecy."

"No. It's grammar." Veya set the page down. "Someone will read this list and hear footsteps in a corridor at night."

"Then they should read page twelve."

"They'll read page nine first. You numbered it that way on purpose."

Toma did not deny it. Lists belonged in the middle where impatient hands found them.

"You met the courier," Veya said. Not a question.

"Thursday. Page one only."

"She'll find page nine without your permission."

"Probably."

"She opened her own query before Cai's chain." Veya almost smiled. "That means she's useful, not lost."

"She conflates timelines."

"She's human." Veya handed the packet back. "Everyone asks about their brother eventually. The archive answers in slopes." A dry pause. "Send your packet. I'll be here when she misreads it."

Veya had misread things too, once—early career, a colony purge theory that made a good paper and a bad prediction. Toma had watched her learn erosion the hard way, verb by verb. He did not mention that. Veya did not need reminding. Her stacks were the reminder.

---

Thursday night, after Lena's visit, Toma pulled page nine's institution list one more time in the garden's last light—forty-three entries, each with citation, date range, budget verb, vendor contract repeat.

He'd never seen all forty-three ledgers in person.

Nobody had.

That was the archive's scale—not a boast, a fact. Vault 14's forty-two-to-eleven roster was one shelf on one moon above one world's gate approach. Page nine was a map of maps. He trusted it because he'd sampled slopes the way geologists sampled rock: core drills, not omniscience.

Wind moved through louvers. Deposit cases clicked. A junior archivist asked if he wanted dinner. Toma said no and meant *not yet*.

---

Friday morning he returned to Vault 14 with better light and worse coffee.

The cold bench outside had become his office for roster work. He pulled Pilgrim Road junction guild samples for comparison—not because Cai had asked, but because patterns rarely respected gate theology.

Junction rosters from Years 216–224 showed the same slope at a gentler angle: fewer names, more *interim*, more vendor language where guild language used to be.

Not purge.

Not conspiracy.

Ink patience.

On the bench he opened a message from Cai—short, keeper-plain:

*Hum steady. Marks multiplying. Supervisor says visual confirmation sufficient. I say insufficient. Send what you have.*

Toma typed back: *Wednesday. Packet en route. Do not let supervisor conflate visual with checked.*

Cai's reply was one line: *Too late for that. Send anyway.*

---

Toma spent Friday afternoon assembling page nine entry by entry—not invention, citation.

Entry 1: First Gate approach relay guild (Vault 14, documented).

Entry 7: Pilgrim Road junction keepers, Two Valleys spur (Vault 9 partial, file link pending).

Entry 12: Meridian Arc oversight circle (Vault 11, Item 16 chain).

Entry 19: Fringe station maintenance chief memo—*guild marks decorative*—(oral deposit, Year 224).

Entry 22: Ribbon intake relay guild (Vault 14 file link, slope gentler, same vendor chord).

Entry 23: Orinreach Basin mirror guild stipend review—deferred, Year 221—(not Lena's brother's file, institutional cousin).

He stopped at entry 23 and walked the list to Veya's corridor for the second friction pass.

"Forty-three is a lot of footsteps," she said.

"Forty-three is a lot of forgetting."

"You're enjoying this."

"I'm indexing." He tapped entry 12. "Meridian isn't prophecy."

"It rhymes." She handed him a language note—*modernize* in three dead dialects, same euphemism drift. "Your courier will hear rhyme and call it plan."

"I know."

"Then why number page nine where impatient hands find it?"

"Because impatient hands exist." Toma took the note. "Page twelve exists too. I'm not responsible for read order. I am responsible for read material."

Veya snorted. "Archivist poetry."

"Citation," he corrected.

---

The report he assembled for Cai was twelve pages long.

Page one was the extract Cai needed: names, dates, the budget strike-through, the vendor consolidation line. Citation: Vault 14, shelf 2-A, Years 217–225.

Page four was the Meridian comparison Toma knew Cai would not have time to read. Citation: Vault 11, oversight minutes, Item 16 Year 207.

Page nine was a list of institutions whose ledgers showed the same slope—relay guilds, junction keepers, oversight circles—stopped paying, stopped naming, stopped expecting anyone to notice. Forty-three entries. Dates. Budget notes. Vendor contract numbers repeated like a chord.

Page twelve said:

*"If your supervisor ignores this, do not assume malice. Assume habit. Habits are harder to fight and easier to document. I am documenting."*

He did not write *purge*.

He did not write *conspiracy*.

Those words arrived later, in other people's mouths, when fear needed a face.

---

He sent the packet to Cai at midday.

The confirmation chimed with the soft bureaucratic politeness of a system that treated twelve pages of institutional decay like freight.

Toma stood in the send queue corridor and read the confirmation twice—citation habit, not distrust. Twelve pages. Page one for the scaffold. Page four for the patient. Page nine for the frightened. Page twelve for the honest.

A junior clerk asked if the packet was urgent.

"Tuesday," Toma said.

The clerk nodded as if that answered everything. On the Archive Moon, it did.

---

Toma returned to the memory garden where new deposits were indexed before descending to stone—recent records treated with the tenderness usually reserved for the old.

Wind moved through louvers. Deposit cases clicked where chains touched terrace stone. A world he'd never seen all of kept swallowing paper with the patience of geology.

A courier manifest crossed his screen in the public feed: **LENA VANCE — ARCHIVE MOON — PROVISIONAL — EXTENDED**.

Not his business yet.

He flagged it anyway.

People who traveled toward archives during absences were either lost or beginning to be useful.

Sometimes both.

---

At dusk he ate in the garden cafeteria with wind in the hollow mountains and vault climate hum in his teeth—another thing keepers maintained without applause.

The cafeteria overlooked a terrace where new oral deposits waited indexing—cases chained, labels handwritten, wind testing seals the way storms tested scaffold. Toma ate without tasting and read a public mirror thread forming around Cai's packet preview—keepers arguing page order, supervisors citing compliance, someone already typing *purge* where Toma had written *slope*.

He did not correct mirrors. Mirrors were not archives.

He flagged three posts for later citation and ate the last bite of food he'd bought out of habit rather than hunger.

He thought about Cai on a scaffold, trusting a hum.

He thought about names struck through in ink that had outlived everyone who'd held the pen.

He thought about the slope, which was not dramatic enough for bulletins and too consistent to be accident.

Tomorrow he would start Vault 9 in earnest—junction guilds on the Pilgrim Road, because Lena's question about who checked cables when storms rolled through was, annoyingly, a good question asked at the wrong speed.

Tomorrow someone would read page one and panic, page four and argue, page nine and misread.

Tonight he stacked his notes in order, labeled the box **FIRST GATE — ROSTER ATTRITION**, and felt the particular calm of a person who had turned urgency into work he knew how to do.

Before sleep he opened the Vault 9 request queue—junction guilds, Pilgrim Road, the place patterns went when gate theology failed. A cooperative deposit had arrived from Two Valleys: oral story, child slate, public roster update. Not his shelf. Not yet. He flagged it for file link anyway.

Patterns rarely respected gate theology.

They also rarely respected pilgrim schedules.

He wrote one line in his personal log before sleep—not for Cai, not for Lena, for the stone:

*Courier Vance will misread page nine within the week. Veya will scold. Jun will publish something. Cai will keep cutting dots. Document anyway.*

The names had waited decades.

They could wait until morning.

He closed the log, felt vault hum in his teeth one last time, and slept the way archivists slept—on a slope, not a knife.

Somewhere above stone, wind moved through louvers and worried deposit case chains on the terrace where new records waited swallowing. The moon kept indexing. Toma kept doubting. Both habits would outlast the week Courier Vance would misread.

That was the job.

That was enough for one night.
