# Chapter 13 — The Vellum Moon

The Vellum Moon kept what the network had decided not to remember.

That was not its official purpose. Official purposes were cleaner than that. The plaque at Dock Three said VELLUM RETENTION ANNEX / HISTORICAL DUPLICATE, NON-ACTIVE ROUTING, AND DEACCESSION REVIEW, which was the kind of sentence built to stand between a thing and the shame of what it did.

Toma, when Lena found him, called it the moon of thrown-away paper.

"Not paper only," he said, before she could ask. "Paper, vellum, copper, wax-sheets, spool-wire — two hundred years of formats invented by people sure theirs was the last one. We keep it all until someone important forgets why it matters. Then we keep it in a worse room."

He said this from beneath a shelving rack, where only his boots were visible: cracked brown leather, soles repaired with three different materials and one of them badly chosen. His voice came out muffled and cheerful and slightly offended. One hand emerged, holding a flattened packet of blue invoices tied in red thread.

"Wrong century," he said, and disappeared again.

Lena stood in the aisle with her pack on her shoulder and the dead panel from Vesper under her arm, and tried not to hate the place for existing.

The moon had no weather. It was rock wrapped in archive: tunnels cut into pale stone, air dried to the edge of pain, lamps kept low because light cost money and old ink was always threatening to become absence. Every corridor smelled of dust, glue, cold metal, and the sourness of machines maintained mostly by resentment.

The gravity was thin — not absent, not Tessom's borrowed not-down, but light enough that every movement argued with memory. Her bag pulled less than it should. When Toma finally slid out from under the rack, the blue packet drifted after him on its string before the weak pull remembered it and let it fall.

He was younger than his voice had suggested, or older in an unfinished way — thin, long-nosed, hair that had given up on a single direction, fingers stained by ink and old glue. His coat had once belonged to an office with a uniform code and now belonged to a man who kept things in the pockets because no shelf deserved his full trust.

"You're the courier," he said.

"You're Toma."

"I am several Toma-adjacent functions at present. Which Toma were you sent to find?"

"The one who keeps what the network throws away."

He considered that, then looked her over with more seriousness than he had given the blue invoices. "Who told you to say it that way?"

"No one."

"Good. If someone had, I'd have charged you more."

He walked past her down the aisle without checking whether she followed. Lena followed because every road she had left seemed lately to begin with someone turning their back and assuming she would know enough to come after.

The records had brought her here.

Jun's pages first. She had carried them since Vesper, folded behind the dead panel. *None of these go where the records say they go. Auxiliary, return, legacy, do not remove.* At first the words had seemed to warn about pipes and lines. Later, after Borren, after the *Wren* and the twenty-six names, they sounded like the true thing gone quiet and unstamped.

Then the marsh-list. She did not have the original; the frost-coated pilot had kept his scrap. But she remembered the lines. *Marsh-light gone green, out of season. Cooling-river milky three days. Sixth-day tide a quarter late — tell the next.* Nothing official. No seal. Just eyes and a hand sending the warning onward.

At Borren a pilot had said anyone reading the lists would not have cleared the *Wren.* Somewhere out past the dark, people who never moved had known what she needed to know. They had known it in time. They had failed only in reach.

That failure had led her sideways again. Dead formats did not go to Cardinal; they went to Vellum. A clerk at an intake window, shown the dead panel and Jun's cramped hand, had gone pale in the irritated way of a man who sees trouble that requires being exact.

*Not here,* he had said.

Which meant *here, but not where I am paid to admit it.*

Three counters, two sealed doors, one lift, and a bribe she could not afford later, she had been sent down-annex to Toma.

He stopped now before a door that did not look like a door — a wall panel that had given up being flush. He pressed his thumb to a latch under a strip of old label glue, and the panel opened with the intimate sigh of a room that preferred to be forgotten.

"Deaccession overflow," he said. "Formerly Duplicate Routing. Formerly Return Office Holdings. Formerly a corridor nobody important came down. The name changes whenever someone upstairs feels useful."

"What do you call it?"

"The room where proof goes to become clutter."

Inside, the shelves were lower and older and more crowded than the ones above. Boxes sat in ranks, not neatly but with the pressured order of too much preserved by too few hands. Some wore printed labels; some handwritten tags; some only grease pencil, half-rubbed by passing coats.

Toma lifted the lamp beside the door. "What, exactly, are we pretending to look for?"

"I'm not pretending."

"That will slow us down. Pretending gives shape."

She set her pack on the nearest crate and took out Jun's pages. He received them without reaching at first — only looked, the way a person looks at an animal that may bite or may be hurt. Then he took them by the edges.

"Cramped hand," he said. "Analyst?"

"Consensus analyst. Cardinal."

"Careful people, analysts. Terrible at handwriting. Afraid the ink might commit them before the sentence is done." He read the first page, then the second, and the cheer went out of him in small increments. "Where did you get these?"

"Vesper. Before I left the slow way north."

"Vesper." He glanced once at the dead panel under her arm. "That was yours too?"

"I took it."

"Of course you did." He turned the panel over. "First posting?"

"Yes."

"Better than the improved one?"

"Much."

"They always are." He handed it back with respect. "First lies are often the nearest thing to truth, because nobody has organized the room yet."

"They're not lies if they don't say enough to be false."

"That is an archivist's sentence. Be careful. It spreads."

He went back to Jun's pages. His eyes moved quickly now, no longer amused.

"Auxiliary return," he murmured. "Legacy. Do not remove. Return paths. VSP-South. Old registry references. These aren't gate lines."

"They behaved like gate lines."

"Everything behaves like a gate line if enough clerks stop remembering what it was for." He tapped one reference with a glue-dark fingernail. "This is not operations. This is returns."

"That's what it says."

"No." He looked up. "I mean *Returns.* Capital R. Old office family. Before the consolidations." He folded the pages carefully, exactly along Jun's creases. "What else?"

She hesitated. With a false manifest, with a leaking hull, she knew what to do. The lists felt almost private, though she had not written them. They had simply existed beyond her reach, and twenty-six people had died where they might have mattered.

"I don't have the paper," she said. "Only the wording."

Toma tilted his head.

She recited it. *Marsh-light gone green, out of season. Cooling-river milky three days. Sixth-day tide a quarter late — tell the next.*

At *tell the next,* Toma's face changed.

Not surprise. Not recognition either. Something closer to pain arriving late from a wound he had been ignoring because there had been work to do.

"Say it again," he said.

She did.

He turned away before she finished and began walking down the row, too fast for the weak gravity. His shoulder clipped a box. It lifted half an inch, pondered falling, and settled.

"What is it?" Lena asked.

"An old habit with new hands."

"That isn't an answer."

"I know. I dislike it already." He stopped at a shelf where the labels had been crossed out so many times the cardboard looked bruised. "There were systems for this. Local Returns. Seasonal Deviations. Unverified Stayer Reports. Corridor Listening." He ran a finger along the tags. "Nobody liked them, which is usually how you know a thing was doing work."

He pulled a box down. It was heavier than he expected; the low gravity tricked him, and Lena reached without thinking to catch the far end before it dragged him off balance. They set it on a worktable.

The box sighed dust.

Toma opened it.

Inside were ledgers, bound in cracked grey skin with thin metal clasps. Not forms, not station notices. Handwritten. Indexed. Stubborn.

Toma selected one with the care of a man lifting a sleeping child.

"Local Returns, Sennen-Tarn outer rim family," he said. "Year two hundred and ninety-four after consolidation, if the label can be trusted." He undid the clasp. "Here."

The pages were columns of small script, many hands. Place. Observation. Reliability. Prior occurrence. Sent onward to. Answer received. Lena leaned over the table. Her chest tightened slowly, the way it had at Borren.

*Green marsh-light, early season — forwarded to Anneth tide office.*

*Cooling-river milked at lower gate — acknowledged by Coreth pump authority.*

*Fifth-day echo absent from canyon return — query opened.*

*Late tide, sixth marker, repeated — compare with downstream flats.*

The handwriting changed every few lines. A keeper here. A canal clerk. A stilt-village watch. People who saw only their own square writing into a page that expected the next square to answer.

*So the next one knows what to watch.*

Lena put one hand on the edge of the table, and the table, being real, held.

"This," she said.

"Yes."

"This is what the lists are."

"No." Toma's voice had gone very quiet. "This is what the lists used to have."

He turned pages. Earlier entries were thick with answering marks. A report from one place drew a note from another. Two weak signals became a caution. A caution revised a route. They made it harder for a single place to be wrong alone.

Then the later pages thinned.

Columns were left blank. *Sent onward to* became *routed through central consolidation.* *Answer received* became *pending.* Pending became a mark Lena had seen on every dead board since Vesper — waiting dressed as movement. Toma skipped forward. Fewer hands. Cleaner forms. Less life.

He opened another ledger.

This one had the same title, but printed. Place remained. Observation remained. Reliability had become Priority. Sent onward to had become Sector Review. Answer received had disappeared.

"Who changed it?" Lena asked.

"That is the correct bad question."

"Toma."

"I know." He pulled another box. Then another. The room became a small storm of records. Ledgers opened under his hands; slips, rolls, indexes. Lena helped because standing still while another person searched had become unbearable to her. The weak gravity made the papers lift and drift at the corners, as though trying to leave before being read.

They found the same pattern again.

Local Returns in one century. Variance Review in another. Corridor Adjustment Office. Stayer Notices. The names changed; the shape underneath did not: small observations moving sideways and back.

Then, across copies separated by worlds and years, the offices vanished.

Not all at once. Worse than that. A name missing from one index. A column removed from a revised form. A budget mark zeroed without closure note. An instruction to retain anomalies only if confirmed by central review, leaving the edge reports first to become officially unworthy of record.

Toma grew more animated as the records grew emptier. The pain in him turned sharp and useful.

"Here," he said, stabbing a page. "Outer Rim Local Returns. Closed by incorporation into Central Routing Assurance."

He pulled another.

"Central Routing Assurance. No holdings after year four hundred twelve. No closure note."

Another.

"Stayer Notices. Reduced to civic folklore category. Folklore. That's what they called people writing down the weather that kept gates alive."

"Someone did this," he said.

Lena did not answer.

Toma turned another page. The paper crackled.

"Not the gate failures. Maybe not that. But this." He spread his hand over the boxes. "This was done. Whole institutions removed from the record. Not corrected, not replaced. Removed. You don't lose this many offices by mistake."

"No?"

He gave her a look. "You are asking the moon of thrown-away paper whether records disappear accidentally."

"I'm asking the man who keeps them."

"That man says accident leaves mess. This is not mess. This is a clean floor after the blood has been mopped."

*Three at once is a hand.*

She had hated that sentence on the Sennen dock. She heard its cousin now in Toma's voice.

"Who benefits?" Toma said.

"That question gets people killed lately."

"Not asking it does too."

"Who would have had authority?" she asked.

"Consolidation offices. Gate trusts. Core routing." He moved quickly now, drawing lines in air. "You centralize the records, you weaken the local offices, you retain only summaries, you declare edge reports unreliable unless confirmed by the center that cannot see the edge. Elegant. Horrible. Common."

"Why erase it?"

"Pick a motive. I have shelves for all of them." He lifted Jun's pages. "When auxiliary return lines stopped going where the records said, nobody existed whose job was to notice before a courier found a dead cable in her hand."

Lena thought of Jun under the banner at Cardinal: the curve stripped of its caveats. She thought of Varik at Khoury, turning hunger into one clean enemy. She thought of herself at Tellan, *this one,* sending twenty-six people toward a route already not live enough.

"What if the motive matters less than the missing work?" she said.

Toma paused. For the first time that afternoon, the quickness went out of him.

"The missing work did not go missing by itself."

"Maybe not."

"That is not an answer."

"I know."

He almost smiled. "You are worse than an analyst."

"I've been accused of less."

He closed the ledger in front of him, but he did not put it away. "I keep discards. I know the difference between decay and disposal. Decay leaves edges, rot, water marks, arguments in the margin. Disposal leaves identical absences in copies that never met each other." He tapped the printed ledger. "This is disposal."

*Disposal.*

Toma's reading fit the room: the missing columns, the reclassified offices, the budget ghosts, every notice that had ever said pending where a name should have been.

Some tired part of her wanted him right. If he was right, the thing had a trail. Trails could be followed. Walls built by hands could be named.

"Show me the oldest copy," she said.

"The oldest copy of what?"

"Whatever changed first."

Toma's eyes narrowed. "Now that is a useful bad question."

It took them another hour. Maybe two. The moon made time feel procedural. Lamps hummed. Toma muttered shelfmarks under his breath. Twice he vanished into side bays and returned with nothing but a worse mood. Lena's shoulders began to ache in the moon's strange gravity — not from weight but from the constant correction of a body built for somewhere else.

At last he found a cabinet with no label at all.

He stood before it for a moment without touching the handle.

"What?" Lena asked.

"This shouldn't be here."

"I'm learning that describes the whole moon."

"No. This shouldn't be here in the specific way that makes me wonder who hid it badly on purpose." He ran one finger along the lock. "No catalog number. No deaccession mark. No intake scar. Someone carried this into my room without telling the room."

"Can you open it?"

He looked wounded. "Courier."

The lock took him longer than he wanted. When it gave, he lifted the lid with ceremony he pretended not to feel.

Inside were thin folios wrapped in dark cloth, each tied with white cord. Not damaged. Not neglected. Preserved.

Toma did not speak.

He took the first folio and laid it on the table. The cord came undone reluctantly.

The top sheet was a copy on good paper, the ink still clear. A memorandum, unsigned at the top, but countersigned in a dozen small marks at the bottom.

Lena read the title.

*Transfer of Local Return Functions to Central Assurance Structures*

The words were so mild they were nearly invisible.

Toma made a sound that was not quite laughter.

"There," he said. "There's your knife in its polite sheath."

The memorandum did not say erase. It did not say silence. It said *duplication burden.* It said *efficiency.* It said *inconsistent local standards.* It said *reducing noise in central routing.* It said *improved confidence in verified reports.* It said the old offices had served well under pre-consolidation conditions, which was the kind of praise people gave a bridge before removing the stones.

Attached behind it were lists.

Office names. Staff counts. Duties. Transition assignments. Local Returns would become Regional Variance Intake. Stayer Notices would be incorporated into Civic Culture and Non-Critical Environmental Reporting. Gate Attunement Exceptions would move under Mechanical Assurance. Corridor Listening would become Historical Observation unless attached to active route fault.

Lena found herself breathing shallowly.

"When?" she asked.

Toma checked the date line, then the conversion mark. "Two hundred and thirteen years ago."

"Before any of this."

"Before this collapse." He turned the page. "Not before the failing began."

On the third sheet, someone had written in a different hand. A protest, formal enough to have been filed and personal enough to have survived the filing.

*Local return work cannot be incorporated without ceasing to be return work. The function depends upon bidirectional acknowledgment between local observers and adjacent stations. Central verification is not replacement. It is delay.*

Below it, another hand had written: *Concern noted.*

Lena closed her eyes.

*Concern noted.* Two words that could bury a world and still sound like manners.

Toma leaned over the page. "Veya."

"What?"

He pointed to the signature.

*Veya Marren, Acting Registrar, Outer Return Family*

"She wrote this?" Lena asked.

"She wrote several things, if the footnotes aren't lying." He flipped through the folio faster now. "Objections. Operational consequences. A predictive note. Here — 'failure to maintain local return acknowledgment will result in increased unobserved drift across slow corridors and edge gate families.'"

"What happened to her?"

Toma gave her a look. "You know what happened. Concern noted happened."

"Is she dead?"

"Likely. Two hundred years makes a habit of that."

But he kept looking through the papers.

There were more memoranda. More objections. Some signed by Veya, some countersigned by people whose names had been reduced to initials and transfer marks. One requested retention of handwritten local ledgers. One argued that summaries could not preserve negative evidence — the absence of a bird, the lateness of a tide, the silence where a town should answer. One page, near the end, had no formal heading at all.

It was a handwritten inventory of chairs.

Lena frowned. "Chairs?"

Toma read over her shoulder. "Twelve return desks, six high stools, three map benches, one cracked but serviceable, two listening tables, local make, sentimental designation not relevant to storage value." He paused. "Oh."

"What?"

"This isn't Veya Marren."

He touched the signature at the bottom.

*Veya Sol.*

"Relation?"

"Could be daughter. Granddaughter. Could be a name taken for the work." He read the small note attached to the inventory. "Requesting storage of furnishings from closed Outer Return room pending appeal."

"Did she get it?"

He searched the page.

"Denied. No storage value."

Lena looked at the inventory longer than the memorandum. The memorandum made her angry. The chairs made something in her sink.

"Where did the appeal go?" she asked.

"Nowhere, if the universe remains consistent."

"Toma."

He dug through the folio. "Lower Vellum. Not the moon — the city under the anchor. Marginal schools, old copying families, people who still teach children how to repair paper nobody will pay to preserve. If she survived, that's where she'd have gone. If her daughter survived. If anyone did."

"You can find her?"

"I can find the direction of the nearest person who will be angry you asked."

"That may be enough."

He began retying the folio, but Lena put a hand on it.

"Wait."

He did.

She looked again at the protest. *Local return work cannot be incorporated without ceasing to be return work.*

"They knew," she said.

"Somebody did."

"No. Not somebody." She tapped Veya Marren's signature. "Her."

"Yes."

"And they erased her."

Toma's face, which had been quick all afternoon, went still. "That," he said softly, "is the first sensible sentence you have said all day."

The anger had somewhere to stand.

And still, beneath it, something did not quite lie flat. The memorandum was not written like malice. Duplication burden. Confidence. Efficiency. Noise. She had seen frightened decent people reach for small correct acts inside large wrong ones.

But she was tired. The resistance in her was tired too. It did not become an answer. It only stayed there, a snag under the anger.

Toma closed the folio at last.

"You wanted records," he said. "Now you have a grave."

"I wanted a way to keep people from dying."

"Graves are useful, if read correctly."

"Can I take it?"

He looked genuinely scandalized. "The folio? Absolutely not."

"Toma."

"No. It has survived two centuries, five name changes, three cleanouts, and at least one person hiding it from people with better coats than mine. I am not handing it to a courier with a dead panel and a heroic inability to stay where records can reach her."

"I need proof."

"You need memory. Proof is what courts ask for when the harm has become comfortable." He tucked the folio under one arm. "I'll make you a copy."

"How long?"

"For a thing this dangerous? Correctly? Half a day."

"I don't have half a day."

"No one who needs records ever has time to keep them. That is why I have a moon."

She almost smiled. It hurt, so she stopped.

Toma carried the folio toward the door, then paused and looked back at the room. The anger in him had settled into purpose.

"You follow this line," he said, tapping the folio, "and it will lead to names."

After all the no ones, the word struck harder than she expected.

"What if names aren't enough?" she asked.

"They never are. That doesn't make them unnecessary."

Toma opened the hidden door.

"Where is Veya Sol?" Lena asked.

"If she lives, lower Vellum. If she doesn't, someone will have kept her irritation alive. Good irritation has descendants."

"You'll take me?"

"I will regret taking you."

"That wasn't the question."

"No," he said. "It was the answer."

They left the deaccession room together. Lena carried the dead panel, Jun's pages, the copied marsh-lines in her head, and now a door that was not the Tarn.

Lower Vellum. Veya Sol, or whoever remained of her.

Toma started down the corridor, folio under one arm, boots whispering dust off the floor.

Lena followed him toward the lift, toward the city under the archive.

Behind her, the old return ledgers waited in their boxes: marsh-light, cooling-river, late tide, answer received, answer pending, answer gone.

For now, that was the trouble.
