# Chapter 13 — The Vellum Moon

The Vellum Moon kept what the network had decided not to remember.

That was not its official purpose. Official purposes were cleaner than that, and usually printed on plaques in language no human mouth would choose for itself. The plaque at Dock Three said VELLUM RETENTION ANNEX / HISTORICAL DUPLICATE, NON-ACTIVE ROUTING, AND DEACCESSION REVIEW, which was the kind of sentence that had been built to stand between a thing and the shame of what it did.

Toma, when Lena found him, called it the moon of thrown-away paper.

"Not paper only," he said, before she could ask. "Paper, vellum, copper, old glass, punched tin, wax-sheets, clay pins, spool-wire, two hundred years of office formats invented by people who were positive their format was the last one civilization would ever need. We keep it all until someone important forgets why it matters. Then we keep it in a worse room."

He said this from beneath a shelving rack, where only his boots were visible: cracked brown leather, soles repaired with three different materials and one of them badly chosen. His voice came out muffled and cheerful and slightly offended, as if the shelf had made an argument he found beneath him. One hand emerged, holding a flattened packet of blue invoices tied in red thread.

"Wrong century," he said, and disappeared again.

Lena stood in the aisle with her pack on her shoulder and the dead panel from Vesper under her arm, and tried not to hate the place for existing.

The moon had no weather. It had no open water, no market smell, no children running underfoot, no birds correcting the morning. It was rock wrapped in archive: tunnels cut into pale stone, air dried to the edge of pain, lamps kept low because light cost money and old ink was always threatening to become absence. Every corridor carried the same smell — dust, skin, glue, cold metal, the faint sourness of machines that had once been warm and were now maintained mostly by resentment.

The gravity was thin. Not absent, not Tessom's borrowed not-down, but light enough that every movement argued with memory. Her bag pulled less than it should. Dust settled slowly, as though reconsidering the value of descent. When Toma finally slid out from under the rack, the blue packet drifted after him on its string before the weak pull remembered it and let it fall.

He was younger than his voice had suggested, or older in an unfinished way — thin, long-nosed, with hair that had given up on a single direction and fingers stained by ink and old glue. His coat had once belonged to an office with a uniform code and now belonged to a man who kept things in the pockets because no shelf deserved his full trust.

"You're the courier," he said.

"You're Toma."

"I am several Toma-adjacent functions at present. Which Toma were you sent to find?"

"The one who keeps what the network throws away."

He considered that, then looked her over with more seriousness than he had given the blue invoices. "Who told you to say it that way?"

"No one."

"Good. If someone had, I'd have charged you more."

He walked past her down the aisle without checking whether she followed. Lena followed because every road she had left seemed lately to begin with someone turning their back and assuming she would know enough to come after.

The records had brought her here, if anything could still be said to bring anyone anywhere.

Jun's pages first. She had carried them since Vesper, folded behind the dead panel, because a man who studied how stories hardened had written a thing too uncertain to publish and sent it after one stranger leaving the room. *None of these go where the records say they go. Auxiliary, return, legacy, do not remove.* At the time, the words had felt like a warning about pipes and lines and the old hidden nerves under the gate. Later, after Borren, after the *Wren* and the twenty-six names on the board, they had begun to feel like something else: not an answer, but a category. The true thing gone quiet and unstamped. The kind of thing that survived only because one person had become ashamed of not saying it.

Then the marsh-list. She did not have the original; the frost-coated pilot had kept his scrap, the way everyone kept the thing they said they meant to throw away. But she remembered the lines because they had cut into her harder than a knife would have. *Marsh-light gone green, out of season. Cooling-river milky three days. Sixth-day tide a quarter late — tell the next.* Nothing official. No seal. No authority. Just eyes and care and a hand sending the warning onward.

At Borren she had sat on the floor with Varik beside her, twenty-six dead because she had been right in the wrong-sized way, and heard a pilot say anyone reading the lists would not have cleared the *Wren.* Somewhere out past the dark, people who never moved had known what she needed to know. They had known it in time. They had failed only in reach.

That failure had led her sideways again.

A fuel-hand at Borren knew a retired records runner who knew a woman with a cousin on Cardinal's lower stacks who said old route registers did not go to Cardinal when they were replaced; Cardinal kept live chatter and proud models, not dead formats. Dead formats went to Vellum. A rumor at a wet gate said Vellum had closed two annexes and was pulping duplicate matter by season's end to save heat. A clerk at an intake window, when Lena showed him the dead Vesper panel and the pages in Jun's cramped hand, had gone pale in the irritated way of a man who sees that a stranger has brought him the kind of trouble that requires being exact.

*Not here,* he had said.

Which, Lena had learned, meant *here, but not where I am paid to admit it.*

Three counters, two sealed doors, one lift, and a bribe she could not afford later, she had been sent down-annex to Toma.

He stopped now before a door that did not look like a door. It looked like a wall panel that had given up being flush. He pressed his thumb to a latch hidden under a strip of old label glue, and the panel opened with the intimate sigh of a room that preferred to be forgotten.

"Deaccession overflow," he said. "Formerly Duplicate Routing, Pre-Consolidation, Non-Current. Formerly Local Variance, Unverified. Formerly Return Office Holdings, Transitional. Formerly a corridor nobody important came down. The name changes whenever someone upstairs feels useful."

"What do you call it?"

"The room where proof goes to become clutter."

Inside, the shelves were lower and older and more crowded than the ones above. The air had a different dryness, sharp enough to taste. Boxes sat in ranks, not neatly but with the pressured order of too much preserved by too few hands. Some wore printed labels; some handwritten tags; some only marks in grease pencil, half-rubbed by passing coats. A narrow track ran along the floor for a cart that had lost one wheel and kept the other three out of loyalty.

Toma lifted the lamp beside the door. "What, exactly, are we pretending to look for?"

"I'm not pretending."

"That will slow us down. Pretending gives shape."

She set her pack on the nearest crate and took out Jun's pages. He received them without reaching at first — only looked, the way a person looks at an animal that may bite or may be hurt. Then he took them by the edges.

"Cramped hand," he said. "Analyst?"

"Consensus analyst. Cardinal."

"Careful people, analysts. Terrible at handwriting. Afraid the ink might commit them before the sentence is done." He read the first page, then the second, and the cheer went out of him in small increments. "Where did you get these?"

"Vesper. Before I left the slow way north."

"Vesper." He glanced once at the dead panel under her arm. "That was yours too?"

"I took it."

"Of course you did." He turned the panel over. "First posting?"

"Yes."

"Better than the improved one?"

"Much."

"They always are." He handed it back with respect, which annoyed her by landing where comfort should have. "First lies are often the nearest thing to truth, because nobody has organized the room yet."

"They're not lies if they don't say enough to be false."

"That is an archivist's sentence. Be careful. It spreads."

He went back to Jun's pages. His eyes moved quickly now, no longer amused.

"Auxiliary return," he murmured. "Legacy. Do not remove. Return paths. VSP-South. Old registry references. These aren't gate lines."

"They behaved like gate lines."

"Everything behaves like a gate line if enough clerks stop remembering what it was for." He tapped one reference with a glue-dark fingernail. "This is not operations. This is returns."

"That's what it says."

"No." He looked up. "I mean *Returns.* Capital R. Old office family. Before the consolidations. The kind of thing they taught everyone was duplication until the duplication was all that told you what the first copy forgot." He folded the pages carefully, exactly along Jun's creases. "What else?"

She hesitated. It had become difficult to show people the thing she did not understand. With a false manifest, with a leaking hull, with a route board lying in its own font, she knew what to do. The lists were different. They felt almost private, which made no sense. She had not written them. They had not been sent to her. They had simply existed, somewhere beyond her reach, and twenty-six people had died where they might have mattered.

"I don't have the paper," she said. "Only the wording."

Toma tilted his head.

She recited it. *Marsh-light gone green, out of season. Cooling-river milky three days. Sixth-day tide a quarter late — tell the next.*

At *tell the next,* Toma's face changed.

Not surprise. Not recognition either. Something closer to pain arriving late from a wound he had been ignoring because there had been work to do.

"Say it again," he said.

She did.

He turned away before she finished and began walking down the row, too fast for the weak gravity. His shoulder clipped a box. It lifted half an inch, pondered falling, and settled.

"What is it?" Lena asked.

"An old habit with new hands."

"That isn't an answer."

"I know. I dislike it already." He stopped at a shelf where the labels had been crossed out so many times the cardboard looked bruised. "There were systems for this. Not good systems, before you get hopeful. Underfunded, locally jealous, written in five incompatible scripts, argued over by people with magnificent handwriting and no sense of mercy. But systems." He ran a finger along the tags. "Local Returns. Seasonal Deviations. Unverified Stayer Reports. Corridor Listening. Gate Attunement Exceptions. Nobody liked them, which is usually how you know a thing was doing work."

He pulled a box down. It was heavier than he expected; the low gravity tricked him, and Lena reached without thinking to catch the far end before it dragged him off balance. They set it on a worktable.

The box sighed dust.

Toma opened it.

Inside were ledgers, real ledgers, bound in cracked grey skin with thin metal clasps. Not forms, not station notices, not the purple-stamped machine-certainty of dead cargo validating its own useless stillness. Handwritten. Indexed. Stubborn.

Toma selected one with the care of a man lifting a sleeping child.

"Local Returns, Sennen-Tarn outer rim family," he said. "Year two hundred and ninety-four after consolidation, if the label can be trusted, which it resents." He undid the clasp. "Here."

The pages were columns of small script, many hands. Place. Observation. Reliability. Prior occurrence. Sent onward to. Answer received. Lena leaned over the table. Her chest tightened slowly, the way it had at Borren, not with fear this time but with recognition arriving before explanation.

*Green marsh-light, early season — forwarded to Anneth tide office.*

*Cooling-river milked at lower gate — acknowledged by Coreth pump authority.*

*Fifth-day echo absent from canyon return — query opened.*

*Late tide, sixth marker, repeated — compare with downstream flats.*

The handwriting changed every few lines. A keeper here. A road-watcher there. A canal clerk. A hill settlement. A stilt-village watch. People who saw only their own square of the world writing into a page that expected the next square to answer.

*So the next one knows what to watch.*

Lena put one hand on the edge of the table, and the table, being real, held.

"This," she said.

"Yes."

"This is what the lists are."

"No." Toma's voice had gone very quiet. "This is what the lists used to have."

He turned pages. Earlier entries were thick with answering marks. A report from one place received a note from another. Two weak signals joined to become a caution. A caution revised a route. A route revision returned to the first place with thanks, correction, dispute, sometimes apology. The pages did not make the world safe. They made it harder for a single place to be wrong alone.

Then the later pages thinned.

Columns were left blank. *Sent onward to* became *routed through central consolidation.* *Answer received* became *pending.* Pending became a mark Lena had seen on every dead board since Vesper, the word bureaucracy used when it wanted waiting to look like movement. Toma skipped forward. Fewer hands. Cleaner forms. Wider margins. Less life.

He opened another ledger.

This one had the same title, but printed, not written. The columns had changed. Place remained. Observation remained. Reliability had become Priority. Sent onward to had become Sector Review. Answer received had disappeared.

"Who changed it?" Lena asked.

"That is the correct bad question."

"Toma."

"I know." He pulled another box. Then another. The room became a small storm of records. Ledgers opened under his hands; slips, rolls, indexes. Lena helped because standing still while another person searched had become unbearable to her. The weak gravity made the papers lift and drift at the corners, as though trying to leave before being read.

They found the same pattern again.

Local Returns in one century. Variance Review in another. Corridor Adjustment Office. Peripheral Attunement. Stayer Notices. The names changed, but the shape underneath was the same: small observations moving sideways and back, not command from the center but correction from the edge. Not one voice. Many. Not certainty. Friction.

Then, across copies separated by worlds and years, the offices vanished.

Not all at once. Worse than that. A name missing from one index. A column removed from a revised form. A routing line redirected to an authority whose seal showed up nowhere else. A budget mark zeroed without closure note. An instruction to retain summaries only. An instruction to retain summaries for three years. An instruction to retain anomalies only if confirmed by central review, which meant the anomalies least likely to be visible from the center were the first to become officially unworthy of record.

Toma grew more animated as the records grew emptier. The pain in him turned sharp and useful.

"Here," he said, stabbing a page with one finger. "Outer Rim Local Returns. Closed by incorporation into Central Routing Assurance."

He pulled another.

"Central Routing Assurance. No holdings after year four hundred twelve. No closure note."

Another.

"Peripheral Attunement Board. Name persists in budget appendix for eighteen years after the last report. Ghost office. Money may have moved through it; work did not."

Another.

"Stayer Notices. Reduced to civic folklore category. Folklore. That's what they called people writing down the weather that kept gates alive."

He looked almost happy now, which Lena understood and mistrusted. There was a terrible relief in a pattern. Even a bad one. Especially a bad one.

"Someone did this," he said.

Lena did not answer.

Toma turned another page. The paper crackled.

"Not the gate failures. Maybe not that. But this." He spread his hand over the boxes, over the names and columns and blank places where answering should have been. "This was done. Whole institutions removed from the record. Not corrected, not replaced, not archived properly. Removed. You don't lose this many offices by mistake."

"No?"

He gave her a look. "You are asking the moon of thrown-away paper whether records disappear accidentally."

"I'm asking the man who keeps them."

"That man says accident leaves mess. This is not mess. This is a clean floor after the blood has been mopped."

The sentence should have repelled her. It did not, not immediately. That was the thing she noticed and disliked: how quickly the room warmed around a culprit. After weeks of dark gates and wrong tides and unfed children and boards that said pending because they had no living thing to say, here was a shape that held still long enough to accuse. Someone had removed the returns. Someone had made the network blind to its own edges. Someone had thrown away the papers, and a civilization that could no longer hear itself was now dying of the deafness.

*Three at once is a hand.*

She had hated that sentence on the Sennen dock because it comforted too easily. She heard its cousin now in Toma's voice and felt, to her shame, the smallest answer in herself lean toward it.

"Who benefits?" Toma said.

"That question gets people killed lately."

"Not asking it does too."

He was not wrong. That was the problem with almost everyone she met now. They were wrong in the direction of something true.

"Who would have had authority?" she asked, because if he was going to build the room around a hand, she might as well see whether the hand had fingers.

"Consolidation offices. Gate trusts. Core routing. Maybe the inner worlds." He moved quickly now, drawing lines in air. "You centralize the records, you weaken the local offices, you retain only summaries, you declare edge reports unreliable unless confirmed by the very center that cannot see the edge. Then one day nobody can prove the margins ever had authority at all. Elegant. Horrible. Common."

"You sound pleased."

"I am never pleased by good knife work. I only respect the grip."

"Why erase it?"

"To make the gate system look self-sufficient. To make the center necessary. To remove witnesses. To hide old failures." He lifted Jun's pages. "To make sure when auxiliary return lines stopped going where the records said, nobody existed whose job was to notice before a courier found a dead cable in her hand." He looked at her. "Pick the motive. I have shelves for all of them."

Lena thought of Jun, under the banner at Cardinal without yet knowing it: the curve stripped of its caveats, made to prove the opposite of what he meant. She thought of Varik at Khoury, turning hunger into work and fear into one clean enemy. She thought of herself at Tellan, a word at an elbow, *this one,* sending twenty-six people toward the live route that was already not live enough.

Everywhere she looked now, a thing could be true and still become the wrong tool in someone's hand.

"What if the motive matters less than the missing work?" she said.

Toma paused.

There. That was the first time she saw him truly notice her. Not as a courier with interesting contraband, not as a frightened woman carrying a dead panel, but as a person who might inconvenience his preferred shape of the room.

"The missing work did not go missing by itself."

"Maybe not."

"That is not an answer."

"I know."

He almost smiled. "You are worse than an analyst."

"I've been accused of less."

He closed the ledger in front of him, but he did not put it away. "Listen to me. I keep discards. I know the difference between decay and disposal. Decay is generous with evidence. It leaves edges, rot, water marks, arguments in the margin, mice if the century was badly managed. Disposal leaves procedure. Disposal leaves identical absences in copies that never met each other. Disposal leaves a clean category where a living office used to be." He tapped the printed ledger. "This is disposal."

The word struck the table and stayed there.

*Disposal.*

Lena could see it. That was what troubled her. Toma's reading fit the room. It fit the neatness of the missing columns, the reclassified offices, the budget ghosts, the summaries retained after the hands disappeared. It fit every notice that had ever said pending where a name should have been, every official sentence that gave absence the dignity of process. It fit the way the world now wanted culprits and kept finding categories instead.

And still some part of her resisted.

Not because she knew he was wrong. She did not. She was tired enough to want him right. If Toma was right, then the thing had a trail. If the thing had a trail, it could be followed. If it could be followed, maybe Arin was not simply a man behind distance and silence and closing roads; maybe he was behind a wall someone had built, and walls built by hands could be named, broken, answered.

That was the seduction in it. Not vengeance. Direction.

She hated that she recognized the taste.

"Show me the oldest copy," she said.

"The oldest copy of what?"

"Whatever changed first."

Toma's eyes narrowed, approving despite himself. "Now that is a useful bad question."

It took them another hour. Maybe two. The moon made time feel procedural. Lamps hummed. Dust drifted. Toma muttered shelfmarks under his breath with the intimacy of prayer and insult. Twice he vanished into side bays and returned with nothing but a worse mood. Lena's shoulders began to ache in the moon's strange gravity, not from weight but from the constant correction of a body built for somewhere else.

At last he found a cabinet with no label at all.

He stood before it for a moment without touching the handle.

"What?" Lena asked.

"This shouldn't be here."

"I'm learning that describes the whole moon."

"No. This shouldn't be here in the specific way that makes me wonder who hid it badly on purpose." He ran one finger along the lock. "No catalog number. No deaccession mark. No intake scar. Someone carried this into my room without telling the room, which is rude."

"Can you open it?"

He looked wounded. "Courier."

The lock took him longer than he wanted. That pleased her more than it should have. When it gave, he lifted the lid with ceremony he pretended not to feel.

Inside were thin folios wrapped in dark cloth, each tied with white cord. Not damaged. Not neglected. Preserved.

Toma did not speak.

He took the first folio and laid it on the table. The cord came undone reluctantly, as though unused to being asked.

The top sheet was not old in the way the ledgers were old. It was a copy on good paper, the ink still clear. A memorandum, unsigned at the top, but countersigned in a dozen small marks at the bottom.

Lena read the title.

*Transfer of Local Return Functions to Central Assurance Structures*

The words were so mild they were nearly invisible.

Toma made a sound that was not quite laughter.

"There," he said. "There's your knife in its polite sheath."

The memorandum did not say erase. It did not say silence. It did not say throw away the people who know when their marsh-light changes color. It said *duplication burden.* It said *efficiency.* It said *inconsistent local standards.* It said *reducing noise in central routing.* It said *improved confidence in verified reports.* It said the old offices had served well under pre-consolidation conditions, which was the kind of praise people gave a bridge before removing the stones.

Attached behind it were lists.

Office names. Staff counts. Duties. Transition assignments. Local Returns would become Regional Variance Intake. Stayer Notices would be incorporated into Civic Culture and Non-Critical Environmental Reporting. Gate Attunement Exceptions would move under Mechanical Assurance. Corridor Listening would become Historical Observation unless attached to active route fault.

All the little eyes of the network, reassigned into blindness one respectful sentence at a time.

Lena found herself breathing shallowly.

"When?" she asked.

Toma checked the date line, then the conversion mark. "Two hundred and thirteen years ago."

"Before any of this."

"Before this collapse." He turned the page. "Not before the failing began."

On the third sheet, someone had written in a different hand. Not a note exactly. A protest, formal enough to have been filed and personal enough to have survived the filing.

*Local return work cannot be incorporated without ceasing to be return work. The function depends upon bidirectional acknowledgment between local observers and adjacent stations. Central verification is not replacement. It is delay.*

Below it, another hand had written: *Concern noted.*

Lena closed her eyes.

*Concern noted.* Two words that could bury a world and still sound like manners.

Toma leaned over the page. "Veya."

"What?"

He pointed to the signature.

*Veya Marren, Acting Registrar, Outer Return Family*

"She wrote this?" Lena asked.

"She wrote several things, if the footnotes aren't lying." He flipped through the folio faster now. "Objections. Operational consequences. A predictive note. Here — 'failure to maintain local return acknowledgment will result in increased unobserved drift across slow corridors and edge gate families.' That is not nothing. That is a woman standing in front of a very large cart and saying perhaps do not roll this downhill."

"What happened to her?"

Toma gave her a look. "You know what happened. Concern noted happened."

"Is she dead?"

"Likely. Two hundred years makes a habit of that."

But he kept looking through the papers.

There were more memoranda. More objections. Some signed by Veya, some countersigned by people whose names had been reduced, over time, to nothing in the ledgers but initials and transfer marks. One document listed remaining personnel for reassignment. One requested retention of handwritten local ledgers. One argued that summaries could not preserve negative evidence — the absence of a bird, the lateness of a tide, the silence where a town should answer. One page, near the end, had no formal heading at all.

It was a handwritten inventory of chairs.

Lena frowned. "Chairs?"

Toma read over her shoulder. "Twelve return desks, six high stools, three map benches, one cracked but serviceable, two listening tables, local make, sentimental designation not relevant to storage value." He paused. "Oh."

"What?"

"This isn't Veya Marren."

He touched the signature at the bottom.

*Veya Sol.*

"Relation?"

"Could be daughter. Granddaughter. Could be a name taken for the work. Archivists breed more through grievance than blood." He read the small note attached to the inventory. "Requesting storage of furnishings from closed Outer Return room pending appeal."

"Did she get it?"

He searched the page with the answer already forming in his face.

"Denied. No storage value."

Of course.

Of course the desks were not worth keeping. Desks only mattered while bodies leaned over them, while hands passed slips from one place to the next, while someone wrote back *received* and meant it. Once the work was made unnecessary, the furniture became furniture. The room became space. The chairs became disposal.

Lena looked at the inventory longer than the memorandum. The memorandum made her angry. The chairs made something in her sink.

"Where did the appeal go?" she asked.

"Nowhere, if the universe remains consistent."

"Toma."

He dug through the folio. "Lower Vellum. Not the moon — the city under the anchor. There are marginal schools down there, old copying families, binding houses, civic recorders, people who still teach children how to repair paper nobody will pay to preserve. If she survived, that's where she'd have gone. If her daughter survived. If anyone did."

"You can find her?"

"I can find the direction of the nearest person who will be angry you asked."

"That may be enough."

He began retying the folio, but Lena put a hand on it.

"Wait."

He did.

She looked again at the protest. *Local return work cannot be incorporated without ceasing to be return work. The function depends upon bidirectional acknowledgment between local observers and adjacent stations. Central verification is not replacement. It is delay.*

It was all there, in the dryest possible language. Anneth. The canyon silence. The marsh-list. Jun's warning. The *Wren.* Every place that had seen one piece of the thing and had no living structure to make the piece travel sideways before it became a name on a board.

"They knew," she said.

"Somebody did."

"No. Not somebody." She tapped Veya Marren's signature. "Her."

"Yes."

"And they erased her."

Toma's face, which had been quick all afternoon, went still. "That," he said softly, "is the first sensible sentence you have said all day."

It should have satisfied her. It was the thing Toma had been leading her toward from the first missing shelf: a hand, a choice, a purge in the mild clothes of efficiency. A woman had warned them. The warning had been noted. The office had been closed. The chairs had been denied storage. Two hundred years later, gates were going dark and people were trading scraps by hand because the structure that once held such scraps had been made ridiculous and then made gone.

Someone had erased her.

The sentence was useful. It was almost certainly true in some way. It gave the anger somewhere to stand.

And still, beneath it, something did not quite lie flat.

Maybe because the memorandum was not written like malice. Maybe because the words were so ordinary. Duplication burden. Confidence. Efficiency. Noise. Maybe because she had seen enough frightened decent people reach for small correct acts inside large wrong ones to know that a knife could pass through a hundred hands without any one hand feeling the cut. Maybe because the most dangerous things she had seen lately did not arrive saying *I will harm you.* They arrived saying *I will help this fit.*

But she was tired. The resistance in her was tired too. It did not become an answer. It only stayed there, a snag under the anger.

Toma closed the folio at last.

"You wanted records," he said. "Now you have a grave."

"I wanted a way to keep people from dying."

"Graves are where civilizations admit what they valued too late. They are useful, if read correctly."

"Can I take it?"

He looked genuinely scandalized. "The folio? Absolutely not."

"Toma."

"No. It has survived two centuries, five name changes, three cleanouts, and at least one person hiding it from people with better coats than mine. I am not handing it to a courier with a dead panel and a heroic inability to stay where records can reach her."

"I need proof."

"You need memory. Proof is what courts ask for when the harm has become comfortable." He tucked the folio under one arm. "I'll make you a copy."

"How long?"

"For a thing this dangerous? Correctly? Half a day."

"I don't have half a day."

"No one who needs records ever has time to keep them. That is why I have a moon."

She almost smiled. It hurt, so she stopped.

Toma carried the folio toward the door, then paused beside the unlabeled cabinet and looked back at the room. The anger in him had settled now into purpose, which was calmer and more dangerous.

"You understand what this means," he said.

"I understand less than I did when I got here."

"Good. Understanding usually starts there." He tapped the folio. "But this much is plain: the network did not merely forget how to hear itself. It was taught to treat hearing as waste. Someone made local knowledge illegible, then called the silence efficiency. You follow that line, courier, and it will lead to names."

Names.

After all the no ones — no one has one, no one minding the whole, no one coming to sign, no one at the end of the wire — the word *names* struck her harder than she expected. Names were a kind of mercy. Names could be found, argued with, blamed. Names could be put on a notice and made to answer. Names could be carried to Arin like proof that she had not spent all this time going sideways for nothing.

"What if names aren't enough?" she asked.

"They never are. That doesn't make them unnecessary."

Toma opened the hidden door. The dry air of the outer corridor came in carrying dust and distant machinery.

"Where is Veya Sol?" Lena asked.

"If she lives, lower Vellum. If she doesn't, someone will have kept her irritation alive. Good irritation has descendants."

"You'll take me?"

"I will regret taking you."

"That wasn't the question."

"No," he said. "It was the answer."

They left the deaccession room together. Behind them, the shelves settled into the low lunar silence of things that had waited a long time already and could, if required, wait longer. Lena carried the dead panel, Jun's pages, the copied marsh-lines in her head, and now the shape of a room the network had once possessed and then politely emptied. For the first time since Borren, the question in her chest had acquired a door.

Not the Tarn. Not yet.

A different door first. Lower Vellum. Veya Sol, or whoever remained of her. The woman who might tell them whose hand had made a civilization decide that the small wrong things at the edge were noise.

Toma started down the corridor, folio under one arm, boots whispering dust off the floor.

"If you want to know how a world forgets," he said, "you do not begin with the missing page. You begin with the person who objected when the page was called unnecessary."

Lena followed him toward the lift, toward the city under the archive, toward the woman who had kept the objection or inherited it.

Behind her, in the hidden room, the old return ledgers waited in their boxes: marsh-light, cooling-river, late tide, silence in the canyon, answer received, answer pending, answer gone. Many small eyes, closed one by one. Many hands, written out of the work.

Toma thought they had found an erasure.

Lena thought so too.

For now, that was the trouble.
