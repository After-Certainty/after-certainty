# Chapter 16 — The Fixes Nobody Logged

Cai's world was held together by repairs that had learned to look natural.

Lena saw that before she saw the gate.

At first, the place looked almost whole. That was what made it hard to trust. After Vesper and Anneth and Drevin and Borren, Lena distrusted functioning things: a clean dock, a lit board, a gate arch with its throat alive and humming.

The world was called Ivera on the boards, though the old pilots on the slow route called it Cai's world because everyone who needed to get through it eventually said the same sentence: *take it to Cai.*

It was a hinge-world, too useful to ignore and too minor to remember. Three corridors crossed there. Before the dark, Ivera had been a transfer, a refuel, a bite eaten standing up, a gate crossed before the local hour could attach itself to memory.

Now the transfer yard was full of people trying to turn almost into enough.

The first thing Lena noticed was the scaffolding.

Not official scaffolding. This was improvised and exact. Wooden ribs met metal struts at joints packed with braided fiber and sealed with a clay Lena did not recognize. Copper lines ran along the undersides, patched by hand and marked with tiny bits of colored glass. At three points, someone had anchored a whole platform to old agricultural machinery turned upside down and convinced to become architecture.

It should not have worked.

It did.

Travelers moved across it in careful streams: locals first because they knew which planks sang and which kept quiet, outsiders after. Above them, the gate throat glowed with a dim blue pulse that did not match the rhythm Lena remembered from any working arch. The sound was wrong too — less hum than breathing through cloth. But people were passing through. Cargo was moving. A boy pushed a cart of water canisters over a bridge made from two cracked door panels and half a harvesting arm, and no one looked surprised.

That was the second thing Lena noticed.

Nobody looked surprised.

She stood at the edge of the yard with Veya's copied packet inside her coat, Jun's pages folded behind the dead scanner panel, and Toma's last words still irritating the inside of her skull.

"Cai will lie to you," he had said at the Vellum departure wicket.

"Everyone does."

"No, courier. Everyone omits. Cai lies with tools in his hands. It is more persuasive."

"Why send me to him then?"

"Because some lies are arranged around things no honest person bothered to preserve."

He had not come with her. Vellum, he said, could spare one obnoxious archivist only in theory. He gave her a list of names instead, three bad maps, and a sealed note addressed to someone called Lio.

"Do not open that," he said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You absolutely were. You have the face."

"I have many faces."

"You have two. Interference and exhausted interference."

He had been almost kind when he said goodbye. Veya had given no farewell. Only the copied packet and a look that said the work was not allowed to become smaller because Lena was tired.

*Observe. Send. Answer.*

The words had followed her through two gates and a half-dead ferry line. They followed her now into Cai's yard.

The gate pulsed again overhead.

A woman near the intake booth flinched. Only once. She buried it fast, bending to retie a child's shoe that did not need tying. Lena marked her, then marked the child, then the booth, then the booth's left support where three new bolts had been sunk around a crack someone had painted over in the local station color.

Not hidden. Not exactly.

Made uninteresting.

She joined the intake line.

The clerk at the booth was not human.

That was not unusual. Lena had worked with hinge-backed cargo crews, chorus diplomats, salt-lung pilots, glass-eyed counters from the Maween finance houses. But this clerk belonged to a people she had only seen twice: the revisers.

At first glance, he looked delicate and severe, tall even sitting, with a narrow head and four arms folded in pairs over the intake desk. His skin had the pale matte quality of old parchment, and along his throat and wrists ran faint lines like written corrections. His eyes were black at the center and copper around the edges. Every few seconds, the copper ring shifted shape — revising.

He had two mouths, one above the other. The lower one spoke. The upper one moved silently a moment after, forming almost the same words in a different order.

Lena had been told that was polite, among revisers. To speak once as meant and once as corrected.

"Purpose of transit," the clerk said.

His upper mouth shaped: *reason for entering, if purpose overstates certainty.*

"Route north," Lena said.

"Everyone's route is north lately."

"I need Cai."

That got her a look.

The copper rings in his eyes narrowed. "Cai is not an office."

Upper mouth: *Cai behaves as if not an office.*

"I was told to take it to Cai."

"Then someone has burdened you with local wisdom."

Upper mouth: *likely Toma.*

Lena almost smiled. "You know him?"

"All records people know all other records people by injury."

"I have a note for Lio."

The clerk's lower hands stilled.

Not dramatic. Not fear. But the same stillness she had seen in Veya when Toma opened the folio.

"Lio is also not an office," he said.

Upper mouth: *Lio is more nearly an office than Cai, which is regrettable.*

"I'm beginning to think no one useful is an office."

The clerk regarded her for a moment. Then, unexpectedly, his upper mouth smiled while the lower did not.

"Correct."

He stamped her intake slip without asking for a transit fee. The stamp was local, blue glass pressed into soft wax, and carried three marks: yard, temporary route, manual acknowledgment.

Lena stared at that last one too long.

The clerk noticed. Of course he did.

"Problem?"

"No."

Upper mouth: *yes.*

She looked up.

"What does manual acknowledgment mean here?"

"It means someone must know you passed."

Upper mouth: *and someone must answer if you do not.*

"Who answers?"

"That depends on where you fail."

It was the most honest intake sentence she had heard since Vesper.

The clerk slid the slip across the desk. "Cai is under the east throat. Follow the pipe noise until it becomes swearing."

"And Lio?"

"You will find Cai first whether you intend to or not."

"What's your name?"

The clerk paused, as if the question required deciding which of him was being addressed.

"Sael."

Upper mouth: *Sael Revised, if names are being formal.*

"I'm Lena."

"I know."

She looked at him.

He tapped the intake book with one long finger. "Word travels."

Upper mouth: *badly.*

Then he turned to the next traveler, and the line pushed her onward.

The east throat lay beyond the main yard, down a ramp that had once been a cargo chute. The walls changed as she descended. Panels had been removed and not replaced. Bundles of cable hung in hammocks of cloth. Old gate conduits ran exposed along the ceiling, some live, some dead, some wrapped in handwritten warnings.

*Do not lean here unless you enjoy consequences.*

*If it hums, tell Maro.*

*If it stops humming, tell Cai first.*

*Do not improve without asking.*

That last one made Lena stop. The words were painted above a valve assembly where six different pipe sizes met in one joint. Around the valve, dozens of tiny scratch marks had been cut into the wall, each paired with initials and dates. Memory marks.

She touched the wall beneath them.

The pipe shivered faintly under her fingertips. Somewhere deeper in the throat, metal coughed, then settled.

The swearing began a level below.

She followed it through a narrow service passage and into a chamber where the underside of the gate filled the air like a held storm.

Cai was inside a machine.

Legs visible below a half-open resonance housing, arms somewhere inside the machinery, voice echoing through the casing. He was broad and grey-haired and bare to the elbows, with a burn across the back of one hand and a wrench gripped between two fingers while the rest of the hand worked blind among parts Lena would not have touched for money.

Three younger mechanics stood around the housing with the expression of apprentices watching a priest commit crimes.

"No," Cai said from inside the gate. "No, because if you tighten the upper clamp to spec the whole throat shears when the pulse hits the third harmonic. Spec was written before the east line learned to limp. Gods preserve me from literate children."

One apprentice, a woman with shaved hair and oil down her cheek, said, "The manual says—"

"The manual is a history of conditions that no longer obtain."

"That's what you said last time."

"And yet the manual has not improved."

A spark jumped. Everyone flinched except Cai.

"Better," he said.

A man beside Lena cleared his throat. "Cai."

"What?"

"Courier."

"Which one?"

"The one with the dead scanner."

The swearing stopped.

Cai slid out from under the housing on a board with wheels mismatched by at least three decades. He looked up at Lena from the floor.

"Absolutely not," he said.

"I haven't asked."

"You have the face of a person who will."

"You're Cai."

"Against advice."

"I'm Lena."

"I know. You argued with the Anneth Watch, turned two children on a Drevin kill-list into cargo, upset Nadi, annoyed Varik, survived Borren, and went to Vellum, which suggests courage or a deficiency in self-preservation severe enough to resemble it."

The apprentices stared at her.

Lena said, "Word travels."

"Badly," Cai said. "But loudly."

He stood. He was shorter than she expected, built for stability over grace. His hair stuck up in grey tufts. His eyes were bright and furious and tired.

"I need to know what you've changed," Lena said.

"No."

"In the gate."

"No."

"What fixes aren't logged."

The room went very quiet.

There it was.

Not guilt exactly. Not fear. The apprentices looked down. The man who had announced her developed a sudden interest in a pressure gauge.

Cai wiped his hands on a cloth already beyond rescue. "There are no unlogged fixes."

"Cai."

"There are no unlogged fixes."

"You just said the manual doesn't apply."

"The manual is interpreted locally."

"By unlogged fixes."

"By judgment."

"What's the difference?"

"Paper."

The apprentices made the smallest possible collective motion. Not laughter. Agreement with survival instincts.

Lena took Veya's packet from inside her coat.

Cai saw it and did not look away quickly enough.

"I have records from Vellum," she said. "Old local returns. Gate attunement exceptions. Offices that used to collect unseasonals and compare them across places." She held his eyes. "I think you're still doing pieces of that. I think the corrections aren't in any log I can read."

Cai's face changed one word at a time.

It was hurt.

He looked at the apprentices. "Out."

One of them began, "But the third harmonic—"

"I said out, not abandon cognition. Watch the upper clamp from the outer board and if the pulse doubles, vent line four."

They went.

The chamber emptied until it was only Lena, Cai, the exposed underside of the gate, and the sound of something huge being kept alive by means no office would ever approve.

Cai lowered himself onto an overturned crate.

"Veya still alive?"

"Yes."

"Mean?"

"Yes."

"Good. Death would only improve her reputation, and she has enough."

"You know her?"

"I know of her. I know enough to avoid knowing more." He rubbed the burn scar on his hand with his thumb. "Show me."

She gave him the packet.

He read faster than she expected. Not skimming. Mechanic-fast, trained to find the load-bearing break in a crowded thing. He paused at the teaching sheet.

*Observe. Send. Answer.*

His mouth tightened.

"Damn her," he said softly.

"Veya?"

"No. Whoever wrote a true thing that small. It's bad manners."

He handed the packet back.

"I have logged everything required."

"That isn't what I asked."

"It is what keeps this gate open."

"For whom?"

"For everyone on those platforms above us."

"And for the people on the next world who don't know what you changed?"

Cai looked away.

The gate pulsed.

This close, Lena felt it in her teeth. It was not the clean thrum of Vesper before the silence. It came unevenly, catch and release, like a body with scar tissue around the lung.

"You think I don't know?" Cai said.

"I think you know exactly."

"Then ask a better question."

"What changed first?"

He stared at her.

"What?" she said. "I'm trying to learn."

"That is an extremely irritating defense."

"What changed first?"

Cai leaned back, eyes on the gate housing. "Return tone."

"From where?"

"Depends which year you want the lie to begin."

"The useful one."

He barked a short laugh. "Fine. East throat, six years ago. Before Vesper. We started losing return tone on the agricultural arc. Tiny delay. Not enough to stop transit. Not enough to fail inspection. But enough that cargo arrived with heat-stress in goods that shouldn't have had it. Seeds germinated inside sealed packets. Milk turned early. Once a crate of winter bulbs bloomed in transit and came out smelling like a funeral."

"Did you report it?"

"Yes."

"And?"

"Central said within tolerance."

"Was it?"

"On the gauges, yes. In the cargo, no."

"What did you do?"

"I shortened the buffer window."

"Logged?"

"Interpretive adjustment within local authority."

"Cai."

"No."

"What else?"

He looked at the gate again.

"Added a pulse-dampener on the lower arc. Old harvest regulator, stripped and reversed. Told central we replaced a worn bracket."

"Did that fix it?"

"For eight months."

"And when it stopped?"

"Bypassed the third harmonic trigger."

"Logged?"

He smiled without humor.

"You know the answer."

"What did central see?"

"A gate operating within acceptable irregularity."

"And adjacent worlds?"

"They saw Ivera still open."

"Did they know how?"

"No."

"Could they have used the fix?"

"No."

She heard the lie before he finished it.

"Some could."

His eyes sharpened.

"Which?" she asked.

"No."

"Cai."

"I said no."

"Twenty-six people died on the *Wren* because someone had bad water and wrong light on a list that did not reach Tellan. If there was a fix that could have told—"

"This would not have saved the *Wren.*"

"You don't know that."

"Yes, I do. Because I know what my fixes fix, and I know what they don't." He stood abruptly. "This dampener works because I know Ivera's throat like my own bad knee. Put it in Anneth and maybe the gate folds a passenger line inside out. Copy without the conditions and people die."

"So someone has to compare the conditions."

He stopped.

His face went still.

He sat again, slower.

"You have become annoying in a precise way," he said.

"I've had teachers."

"Veya?"

"And grief."

"Better teacher. Worse assignments."

The gate made a soft thunk overhead. Cai looked up automatically, counted under his breath to four, and relaxed only when the second pulse came clean.

"Line four," he called toward the passage.

A distant apprentice shouted back, "Stable."

Cai grunted.

Lena waited.

At last he said, "There is a book."

"Logged?"

He gave her a look.

"Right."

"Not a book. A mess. Notes. Failed configurations. Temperature shifts. Which fixes held under which moods. What we told the office versus what we did. Forgetting is expensive."

"Who sees it?"

"No one."

"Cai."

"My apprentices. Eventually."

"That's not no one."

"It is no one with authority."

"That may be an advantage."

He almost smiled, despite himself. "You sound like Vellum."

"Good irritation has descendants."

"Gods help us."

He stood and motioned her to follow.

They went deeper into the service throat.

The passage narrowed until the gate's sound became less a noise than a climate. Cai led her through a crawl-door, down six iron rungs, across a plank over a gap Lena did not inspect. At the end was a small room carved into the foundation stone.

It had no plaque.

It had shelves.

On the shelves were notebooks.

Not beautiful. Not archival. Oil-stained, heat-warped, swollen at the corners, tied with cord, labeled in Cai's blocky hand and older hands. Lena saw dates going back forty years, maybe more.

He took down the newest notebook and put it in her hands.

It smelled of metal, grease, smoke, and human refusal.

"Do not romanticize that," he said.

"I wasn't."

"You were thinking it smells like refusal."

She closed her mouth.

"It smells like bad ventilation."

She opened the notebook.

The first page was a table of failures.

Not formal, but disciplined. Date. Symptom. Conditions. Action taken. Result. Reported as. Follow-up.

*Reported as.*

That column made her throat tighten.

*Pulse lag at east throat under cold rain. Added copper braid at lower ring. Reported as insulation replacement.*

*Cargo heat creep on agricultural arc. Shortened buffer. Reported as scheduling adjustment.*

*Return echo split after third transit. Manual delay inserted. Reported as traffic smoothing.*

*Blue-white gate flare on high wind. Reversed regulator. Reported as worn bracket replaced.*

There were failures crossed out. Fixes marked *DO NOT REPEAT.* One page contained only the words:

*Never again under summer humidity. Ask M before touching.*

Another:

*Worked here. Killed sheep at Rallo. Why?*

Lena looked up.

Cai's face was expressionless.

"Rallo copied it?"

"I gave it to them."

"Logged?"

"No."

"What happened?"

"They saved the passenger line and lost a livestock hold. Forty-three sheep, one shepherd's hand to frostbite. No human deaths, which is how people sleep again." He took the notebook from her, found the page, and tapped the note. "That is why I do not hand out fixes like seed."

"But you wrote why."

"I wrote the question."

"Did Rallo reply?"

"No."

"You sent," Lena said.

"I warned."

"Not the same."

"I know that now."

He said it sharply, but not to her.

The room's single lamp flickered. Cai thumped the wall beside it with two fingers. It steadied.

Lena tapped the Rallo page. "Then ask them again. Not the fix. The humidity. The livestock hold. What failed under which conditions."

"That is still dangerous."

"So is this page with no reply."

He looked at her then, a tired and unwilling respect moving behind the irritation. "You have become very troublesome."

"I started there."

"No. You started sharp. Trouble requires context."

He returned the notebook to the shelf.

From above came a shout.

"Cai!"

He was moving before the second syllable.

Lena followed him up the rungs and across the plank. By the time they reached the main chamber, the gate's pulse had changed. Faster. Too shallow. The apprentices were at their stations with the focused terror of people who had rehearsed disaster but would still prefer theory.

"Lower ring climbing," shaved-hair said.

"Vent four?"

"Open."

"Manual?"

"Ready."

Cai went to the panel. "No manual yet."

The pulse sped again.

Travelers in the yard above began to notice. Lena heard it through the throat: the shift when a machine became personal.

Sael appeared at the chamber entrance, four arms full of intake books.

"Third harmonic variance," he said.

Upper mouth: *close the line before it chooses for us.*

"Not yet," Cai snapped.

"What changed?" Lena asked.

Cai pointed at the board. "Pulse climb, lower ring heat, return tone thin."

"No. What changed from last time?"

"No time."

"Make time."

His eyes flashed. Then the mechanic in him took over.

"Humidity low. Cargo load heavier. More human transit. East wind."

Sael's upper mouth moved silently; the lower said, "And intake rate outran acknowledgment by thirty-seven names."

Cai swore.

"What?" Lena asked.

"People moved faster than acknowledgment," Sael said.

Cai was already turning. "Close the outer queue."

"That will panic the yard," shaved-hair said.

"Better than killing it."

Lena grabbed the intake slip still tucked into her sleeve.

*Manual acknowledgment.*

"Don't just close it," she said.

Cai rounded on her. "Courier, if this is a philosophical—"

"Tell them what changed. Tell them who's through, who waits, who is watched. Don't say suspended. Don't say review."

Sael's eyes flickered copper.

The upper mouth said, very softly, before the lower: *yes.*

Cai stared at Lena for one breath. Then he pointed at Sael. "Yard notice. Human rate paused. Cargo continues after ring cools. Names already through posted. Names waiting held with return count. No one loses place."

Sael was already writing.

"Manual pause. Ring cooling. Transit order retained. Acknowledgment active."

"That's ugly," Cai said.

"It is accurate," Sael replied.

Upper mouth: *and harder to riot against than ugly lies.*

The first notice went up through the speaking tube.

A voice in the yard repeated it badly. Sael corrected through the tube. The voice repeated it better. Above, the crowd sound rose, struck the edge of panic, then broke unevenly into complaint instead.

Cai worked the panel. Shaved-hair opened the lower bleed. Another apprentice, shaking visibly, counted pulses aloud. The gate fought them with that horrible shallow breathing through cloth. A small thing almost failing.

"Manual acknowledgment list?" Cai snapped.

Sael held up two books with one pair of hands while writing with the other. "Split by transit batch. Reply marks assigned."

"Who answers if the ring jumps?"

"You."

"Wrong. If the ring jumps, I'm under the housing. Maro answers."

Maro, the apprentice with oil on her cheek, looked up.

Cai did not soften. "You know the lower ring better than I do now."

"I—"

"You do. Answer if it jumps."

Maro swallowed. "Yes."

The gate pulse slowed.

Not fixed. Not calm. Slowed.

Lena realized she had stopped breathing only when she had to start again.

The next ten minutes were ugly and precise. People shouted. Sael revised notices as fast as the situation changed. Cai cursed in smaller and smaller units. Maro answered twice, both times before Cai could. Each time he corrected only the part that needed correction and let the rest stand.

At the end, the gate remained open. Barely. But open. Above, complaint settled into furious patience.

Sael came down from the speaking tube with chalk on all four hands.

"That was almost competent," Cai said.

Upper mouth: *thank you.*

Lower mouth: *go sit on a blade.*

Cai grunted. "Maro."

The apprentice stiffened.

"You answered the second jump correctly."

She stared at him as though praise were a tool she had never seen used in the field.

"Write it," he said.

"What?"

"The conditions. Low humidity, human rate, east wind, manual pause, lower bleed response, ring jump behavior. All of it."

"In the book?"

Cai's jaw worked.

Then he said, "No."

Lena felt her chest drop.

Cai looked at her. "Not only in the book."

He turned to Sael. "Make a copy for Lio."

Sael's upper mouth smiled first.

"Lio is going to be insufferable."

"Lio is already insufferable."

"True."

"And send a short version to Rallo."

At that, the apprentices felt the room shift. Sael felt it. Lena felt it like a lock gate moving against old pressure.

Rallo.

The place that had not replied.

Maro said, carefully, "What do we ask them?"

Cai looked at her for a long moment. "Whether their humidity was high when the sheep froze."

The sentence was almost absurd, and it felt like a door.

Sael wrote it down.

Not a fix. Not a command. A question shaped so a reply could return.

*Whether their humidity was high when the sheep froze.*

Cai sat down heavily on his crate.

"You look pleased," he said to Lena.

"I'm trying not to."

"Try harder."

"You just asked them."

"I sent a technical query."

"That is what asking wears when a mechanic is embarrassed."

Maro made a sound and turned it into a cough.

Cai pointed at her without looking. "Do not become entertained by couriers. It leads to poor work."

"Yes, Cai."

Sael gathered the intake books. "Lio should see her."

"I know," Cai said.

"You have delayed because you dislike admitting Lio was correct about the acknowledgment marks."

"I have delayed because Lio enjoys being correct more than safety permits."

Upper mouth: *true but irrelevant.*

Lower mouth: *I will tell them you said so.*

"Tell them whatever your second mouth can survive."

Sael left, which somehow took longer than human leaving because every movement seemed to finish twice.

Lena turned to Cai. "Who is Lio?"

"A clerk."

"That means less to me than it used to."

"Good." Cai rubbed both hands over his face. "Lio keeps the slips the offices stopped keeping. Names. Who replies. Who lied last time. Who owes an answer after nearly killing someone else's sheep."

"An office?"

"Offices get closed. Lio is a habit with a desk."

"What does Lio do differently?"

Cai smiled, and it carried real fondness.

"They answer."

Outside, the yard settled into its delay. Somewhere above, a retained-order board said *we still know you are waiting.*

Cai stood.

"Come on, courier. Let's take you to the habit with a desk."

They climbed back toward the yard by a side passage. Halfway up, Maro called after them.

"Cai?"

He turned.

"Do I write the near-fail as third harmonic variance or manual-rate overload?"

He looked at Lena once, annoyed by the witness.

Then back at Maro.

"Both," he said. "And write that we don't know which mattered more."

Maro nodded and bent to the notebook.

They emerged into the transfer yard.

Above them the gate breathed unevenly, alive by repair and at least three lies still pretending to be local interpretation. The scaffolding held. The passengers waited with names retained. Cargo moved slowly under the cooling ring. A child kicked the same bolt across the platform three times and was retrieved by the same exhausted mother three times.

Near the intake booth, Sael was rewriting the notice in larger script.

*Manual pause. Ring cooling. Transit order retained.*
*If your name is listed, you are still held in sequence.*
*If your name is missing, tell Sael, not the crowd.*

At the far end of the yard stood a little office built between two supports of the improvised scaffolding. It looked temporary in the way only permanent things look when no one has admitted what they are. Its walls were patched from five materials. Its door was half-open. Above it hung no sign.

People came and went from it in ones and twos: a pilot with a folded chart, a woman carrying a box of cracked insulators, two children with a slate, an old man who appeared to be delivering onions and left without them. No one lingered unless asked. No one saluted. No one queued in the formal way.

Cai stopped before the door.

"Lio," he called.

A voice inside said, "If that is Cai, I'm busy."

"You're always busy."

"Then become less surprised."

"I brought the courier."

A pause.

"The Vesper courier?"

"She has collected several place names since, but yes."

"Does she listen?"

Cai glanced at Lena. "Intermittently."

"Better than most. Send her in."

Cai did not enter.

Lena looked at him. "You're not coming?"

"Absolutely not."

"Why?"

"Because Lio will be right about something and I am at my limit for the day."

From inside, the voice said, "I heard that."

"You were meant to."

Lena stepped through the door.

Lio was not what she expected either.

They were human, or close enough that the difference did not announce itself. Maybe sixty, maybe older, with short dark hair silvered at the temples, spectacles pushed up on their head, and ink on their sleeve. Their desk was three crate lids laid across two file cabinets. On it sat stacks of slips, route scraps, tiny hand-drawn maps, broken seals, two bowls of onions, a child's slate, and an intake book open to a page of names connected by lines.

Lio looked up and smiled as if Lena were late to a meeting Lena had not known existed.

"Finally," they said.

Lio picked up Toma's sealed note from the desk. Apparently Sael had sent it ahead already.

"Toma says you are carrying Veya's worse truth, Jun's cowardly pages, a dead Vesper panel, and a question that has begun walking north faster than you are." Lio tapped the paper. "He also says not to mistake finding us for the answer."

"Toma is becoming repetitive."

"Archive years count double." Lio leaned back. "Show me what you have."

Lena took out Veya's packet.

Then Jun's pages.

Then the copied lines of the marsh-list she had written from memory after Borren.

Lio did not reach immediately.

Then they took the pages and began to read.

Outside the gate pulse continued, uneven but holding.

When they finished, they set all the pages side by side.

Not stacked.

Compared.

Only the hand holding Jun's page had tightened when it reached *None of these go where the records say they go.*

"Cai showed you the notebooks?" Lio asked.

"Some."

"And denied everything first?"

"Yes."

"Good. I would worry if he had become reasonable."

"What is this place?"

"A bad idea that has not yet failed."

"That's not an answer."

"It is more honest than most." Lio touched the marsh-list. "We've been getting versions for weeks. Eleven through pilots, peddlers, school packets, one marriage contract, and a crate of onions."

"The onions?"

"Excellent carrier. Nobody inspects onions for treason because they are already punishment enough."

"You knew about the lists?"

"Some."

"You didn't send them to Tellan."

The room went still.

Lio did not defend.

"No," they said. "We did not."

"Why?"

"Because we did not have Tellan in the line."

"Why not?"

"Because Tellan's gate office stopped answering five years ago, its pilots prefer paid freight to unpaid warning, and the only person we trusted there died last winter."

"That's not enough."

"No," Lio said. "It wasn't."

Lena sat without being invited.

The chair creaked. Nobody apologized for it.

"The lists could have saved them," she said.

"Yes."

"And you're still sitting here."

Lio's eyes met hers.

"Where should I be sitting?"

Lena had no reply and hated them briefly for asking.

Lio placed one finger on the marsh-lines, then on a blank column ruled for reply marks that had never been filled. "We can compare what arrives. We cannot yet answer fast enough without becoming the kind of desk that killed the old lines."

Lena looked at the pages side by side.

"What do you need from me?" she asked.

Lio smiled faintly.

"There. Better question."

"What do you need?"

"Not you alone."

"No."

The word came out before she thought. Not refusal. Correction.

Lio heard it.

"Good," they said. "Then we may get somewhere eventually."

They opened the intake book and turned it toward her.

Names. Lines. Places. Some solid, some dotted, some crossed with marks Lena did not know yet. Cai to Rallo. Rallo unanswered. Sael to Vellum. Vellum to Lio. Marsh packets west. West pilot to Tarn? A question mark. Tarn circled twice.

Lena's breath caught.

Lio noticed but did not comment.

At the edge of the page, in a different hand, someone had written:

*A. Sol? South Lock? winter-skin, black water pulse, moths low.*

The handwriting was not Lena's.

It was not Arin's either, not as she remembered it. But memory changed handwriting in the mind. She stared at the A until it became too much and not enough.

"What is that?" she asked.

"Recent packet through west marsh line. No full name. Lock reports from the Tarn family. Very good observations. Poor routing. We've been trying to identify the sender."

The room lost sound.

Lena touched the page with one finger.

*A. Sol? South Lock?*

No. Not Sol. A different A. A boy at a family table telling her fast was not the same as easy. A man by water, probably, because of course he would be by water.

"What did it say?" she asked.

Lio's eyes softened.

"Skin first. Gauge late. Winter-skin under summer. Moths low. Black water pulse at North Lock. Answer if you see the same."

Lena closed her eyes.

For a moment she was back in their mother's kitchen with peppermint on the table and Arin's hand on the back of a chair, saying *No, it's just fast,* and she was younger and angrier and already leaving.

*Answer if you see the same.*

He had been sending.

She opened her eyes.

"His name is Arin," she said.

Lio did not move.

"My brother. His name is Arin."

Cai, from the doorway behind her, said very softly, "Ah."

Lena had not heard him come in.

Neither had she heard Sael, standing behind him with the Rallo query in all four hands.

Lio looked at the map of names and lines and then at Lena.

"Then," they said, "your brother may already be holding a line we have not known how to reach."

Lena looked at the pages: Veya's returns, Cai's notebooks, Jun's warning, the marsh-list, Lio's lines, Arin's winter-skin.

"What do we do?" she asked.

Lio picked up a blank slip.

"We answer him," they said.
