# Chapter 14 — Purge

Lena did not steal page nine.

She found it in a public annex the way you found things on the Archive Moon—by being persistent until a system designed for patience mistook persistence for authorization.

Cai had posted an excerpt to a keeper coordination corridor with **HISTORICAL CONTEXT** headers and a warning about contemporary appeals. Someone downstream had mirrored page one. Someone else had mirrored the whole packet with the file tags sloppy and the fear intact.

She read it on a bench in the annex with her courier bag between her knees and the hollow mountains humming through stone.

The annex had emptied for night cycle—bodies gone to berths, arguments gone to mirrors, only archivists and couriers with private questions left in the long lightless hours when paper felt louder than voices.

Page one: names struck through.

Page four: Meridian oversight thinning across forty years.

Page nine: a list.

---

At first it read like documentation—forty-three institutions, dates, budget notes, vendor contract numbers repeating like a chord in a song you couldn't unhear.

On the second reading, in the annex's night-quiet when the intake ring had gone dim and only vault climate moved air through louvers, the list became footsteps.

Not metaphor. Body knowledge. The sound a corridor makes when someone walks it on purpose at an hour when honest people sleep—measured, unhurried, not hiding, not announcing. The list was that sound on paper. Institution after institution. Year after year. Same slope. Same ink patience. Same contract digits where guild language used to be.

Lena's shoulders tightened. Her hand wanted a throat to grab. The annex gave her only benches and stone hum and forty-three entries that did not care whether she slept.

**INSTITUTIONS SHOWING ROSTER SLOPE — RELAY GUILDS / JUNCTION KEEPERS / OVERSIGHT CIRCLES**

Forty-three entries.

Dates.

Budget notes.

Vendor contract numbers repeated like a chord.

She read it once as Toma had meant it—as documentation, gradual, administrative.

She read it again as a courier who had watched **SEPARATIST SABOTAGE** climb a public wall while gates went quiet and routes shifted and her brother's messages stopped answering.

The second reading had faces.

Not names she knew. Roles. Functions. The kind of people who left marks on housing seams and schedules in guild ledgers and hum in their teeth on scaffolds.

Stopped paying.

Stopped naming.

Stopped expecting anyone to notice.

On the third reading the list sounded like a plan.

She walked the annex twice more hunting context Toma would have put on page twelve—habit language, vendor trial notes, deferred reviews. The annex had summaries. Summaries were where institutions hid slopes behind single verbs.

*Modernized. Consolidated. Pared.*

Each verb a small theft of attention.

She copied three appendix tables into her slate and felt the theft add up across decades—not one knife, a thousand dull cuts.

---

A keeper mirror comment thread had already started under Cai's packet.

**SUPERVISOR KELL:** Historical. Not current roster work.

**CAI:** Contemporary hum disagrees.

**ANON KEEPER:** Page nine — is anyone else seeing pattern?

**ANON KEEPER 2:** Seeing pattern isn't the same as seeing perpetrator.

**SEPARATIST REPOST:** *They thinned us on purpose.*

Lena read the thread with her stomach tight. Fear didn't wait for archivists. Fear mirrored first and asked citation later.

One keeper—maybe Cai, maybe someone who typed like him—replied: *They thinned us by not looking. Different crime. Same corpse.*

The thread exploded in smaller angers.

Lena did not post.

She saved the thread anyway.

She copied page nine into a clean slate and highlighted every budget verb—*consolidated, pared, deferred, trial successful*—until the page looked like a weather map of administrative boredom.

Boredom as weapon was harder to hate than malice.

That was why she almost preferred malice.

She stood and walked the annex length once more because her body needed motion when her mind wanted a villain. Boots on stone sounded like corridor footsteps—the list in her ears now, impossible to unhear. A night archivist nodded without curiosity. The moon's night shift treated fear as intake: file it, don't feed it.

Lena fed it anyway.

She pulled page twelve on a public terminal—habit language, vendor trial notes, Toma's closing line about malice versus habit—and read it fast enough to pretend she'd earned page nine.

*Assume habit. Habits are harder to fight and easier to document.*

Her jaw ached from clenching. Habit didn't get you through a fringe corridor when the map moved. Habit didn't read **DELIVERED** on a brother's queue. Habit was true and useless and she was tired of true things that required patience she couldn't afford.

She copied page twelve into the same slate as page nine and felt the two pages argue in her pocket.

---

She called Jun because Drift Markets had taught her that fear wanted a model, and models at least had edges you could argue with.

She made the call from the annex stairwell—stone acoustics, bodies passing above, nowhere to pace except up and down three steps like a person trying to convince herself she wasn't about to trade Toma's patience for Jun's decimals.

Jun answered on the fourth ring, background noise of a field office—charts, voices, the soft clatter of someone who treated belief as weather and weather as data.

"Vance." His tone was professionally pleased. "You're on the moon. I wondered when archive curiosity would beat route pride."

"I have a list."

"I have a revised cascade. We should compare notes."

"I don't want your cascade."

A pause. Not offense—recalibration. "Then tell me what you have."

She read him page nine without the Meridian softening, without page twelve's habit language, without Toma's voice in her ear saying *do not conflate*.

When she finished, Jun was quiet long enough that she checked the connection.

"That's institutional," he said finally. "That's coordinated."

"It's a list of institutions that thinned the same way."

"Across jurisdictions. Across decades. With vendor language where guild language used to be." Paper rustled on his end. "That's not erosion in the public sense. That's capacity removal. Someone benefits from fewer eyes on relays."

"Separatists?"

"Separatists benefit from chaos narratives. This benefits from *normalization*." Another pause. She heard him typing—sorting habits she could picture without wanting to. "I'm building a working chart. Give me ten minutes."

"I didn't call for a cluster."

"You called with forty-three entries and a gate bulletin. That's a cluster invitation whether you like the grammar or not."

She waited with the annex bench cold through her coat, page nine glowing on her slate, vault hum in her teeth.

Jun came back with numbers the way pilots came back with weather.

**COORDINATED CAPACITY REMOVAL — PROVISIONAL CLUSTER — 0.76**

"Seventy-six isn't certainty," he said. "It's enough to publish if you're careful."

"It's enough to feel true."

"Feeling true is my job. Being true is Toma's." A beat. "Where is he on this?"

"Slow."

"Archives are always slow. That's not the same as wrong."

"My brother isn't a symptom."

"I didn't say he was. I said the story that fits is getting larger." His voice softened a fraction—not warmth, calibration. "Lena. If sabotage is wrong—and I think the uneven stress pattern is pointing the wrong way—this is the next best shape. Coordinated thinning precedes coordinated failure. The list is mirrored in keeper corridors. I can pull mirrors and cross-jurisdiction feeds by morning."

She wanted to argue.

She wanted to believe him.

Both wants felt true, which was how misreads got their teeth.

"Walk me through the cluster," she said, because if she was going to ignore Toma she should at least understand what she was trading patience for.

"Relay guild attrition entries link to junction keeper attrition entries above 0.6 across jurisdictions. Vendor contract **7-A** appears in eleven of forty-three lines—not proof of single actor, proof of repeated compliance grammar." Paper again. "Oversight circle minutes show deferred reviews preceding strike-throughs in sixty percent of sampled cases. That's not malice. That's—"

"Capacity removal."

"—institutional habit with systemic outcome." A beat. "The distinction matters in publication. It may not matter to your brother."

"It matters to me because I need a story that moves." She heard herself and hated the sentence. "Not publish. Move. Routes. Bodies."

"Then move toward Cai, not Jun." Jun's voice stayed precise. "Cai maintains. Jun measures. Toma documents. You're a courier. Carry the right packet to the right hands."

"I don't have the packet."

"You have page nine. That's already more than most couriers carry." Another pause. "Zero point seven six is provisional. I won't publish without your confirmation or without Toma's citation chain. But I won't pretend the shape isn't there."

"That's almost kindness."

"That's methodology." Softer: "Don't make it true yet. Verify."

She stared at the annex wall. Verify meant wait. Wait meant **DELIVERED** without **READ**.

"I'll call you tomorrow," she said, and ended the call before methodology could sound like care.

---

She walked the intake ring after the call with Jun's revised confidence living in her slate like a second heartbeat.

**COORDINATED CAPACITY REMOVAL — PROVISIONAL CLUSTER — 0.76**

She hated that she understood his grammar.

She hated more that page nine had given him better fuel than Vesper suspension alone.

Her body knew the number before her mind admitted it—pulse up, bag heavier, the old Drift habit of treating decimals as weather that could crush you. Zero point seven six wasn't proof. It was permission. Jun would treat it as permission to publish. She'd treat it as permission to stop pretending patience was a route.

At a public thread kiosk she skimmed border rhetoric—separatist pamphlets she'd filed under *wrong-border* since the Ribbon, now blooming with new verbs. *PURGE. CONSOLIDATION. SILENCE AS WEAPON.*

Someone had already connected dots she hadn't said aloud.

Fear moved faster than archives.

She took the tram to the lower ring and stood at a public kiosk where Drift Markets rates still flickered—fringe premiums, corridor waits, a ledger row for **ARCHIVE MOON — PROVISIONAL BERTH** that made her wince. Even here, economics kept time.

A pilot she didn't know argued with a clerk about manifest weight. Nobody asked about page nine. Everybody acted like the world had already chosen a story.

Then the collision happened in front of her—not on a screen, in a body corridor between kiosk and tea wall.

A separatist pamphleteer in a weathered coat had set up a folding table with leaflets and a hand-drawn chart: *CAPACITY REMOVAL TIMELINE — THEY KNEW*. Bold margins. Confident arrows. Contract numbers circled in red.

**7-A** appeared four times on the chart—relay guild, junction keeper, oversight circle, fringe station—connected by arrows Lena recognized as Jun's cluster grammar drawn by hand for bodies instead of screens.

Across the corridor, a keeper-mirror poster had gone up on the public board—Cai's packet excerpt, page one names, **HISTORICAL CONTEXT** header, supervisor Kell's comment thread printed small beneath. Someone had annotated the margin in keeper hand: *Not current roster work.*

The pamphleteer pointed at the poster. "They thinned us on purpose. Your own archive says so."

A woman in keeper thermal wear—not Cai, someone who dressed like him—stepped between table and board. "Your leaflet cites contract **7-A**. So does our vendor compliance sheet. Same digits. Different story."

"Same crime."

"Same *digits*." The keeper's voice stayed level. Lena recognized the exhaustion in it—scaffold hours, pressure in her knees, no time for theater. "Your pamphlet calls it border warfare. Our supervisor calls it historical. Toma calls it slope. I call it Tuesday I haven't slept through yet."

The pamphleteer shoved a leaflet at a passing researcher. "Forty-three institutions don't slope by accident."

"Forty-three institutions forgot to count." The keeper tapped the poster. "Ask me again when you've read page twelve."

Nobody won. Nobody left. The corridor kept moving around them—bodies with places to be, tea to buy, berths to afford.

Lena stood with her bag strap cutting her shoulder and watched the same contract number—**7-A**—sit on leaflet and poster like a chord both sides could hear and harmonize into different wars.

She didn't take a pamphlet.

She photographed both.

A clerk at the tea wall watched the argument without intervening—the moon's habit of letting collisions happen in public where both sides could be indexed later. Lena bought tea she didn't want and stood close enough to hear the pamphleteer's pitch to a passing student.

"Forty-three institutions," he said, tapping **7-A** circled in red. "Same contract. Same decade. You think that's erosion? Erosion doesn't rhyme."

The student took a leaflet. The keeper on the poster board typed a reply on her slate—Lena caught the glow: *Page twelve. Read order matters.*—and walked away before the pamphleteer could turn it into victory.

Lena drank bitter tea. Her hands were steady. That frightened her too.

She stayed ten more minutes—courier habit, witness before flight—and watched two more bodies get pamphlets. The keeper poster board gained a second annotation: *READ PAGE TWELVE BEFORE PAGE NINE* in block letters someone had written with anger and care in equal measure.

Contract **7-A** sat on leaflet and poster and in her slate like a digit that had learned to multiply stories.

When the pamphleteer folded his table for night shift, he said to no one in particular: "They can't call it erosion forever."

The keeper thermal woman, leaving, said to someone on her slate: "They can't call it warfare without reading."

Lena walked to the tram with both sentences in her pocket.

---

Toma found her in the east wing corridor that evening, reading vendor contract language with her lips moving.

"You got page nine," he said.

Not a question.

"Cai's corridor leaked."

"Cai's corridor leaks because keepers are frightened and frightened people share paper like warmth." He stood beside her without inviting her into the office. "What do you think it says?"

Lena looked up. "That relay capacity was removed on purpose. Across institutions. Across time. That someone wanted fewer keepers before the gates started failing."

Toma's face did the thing patient people's faces did when they heard a familiar misread arrive on schedule.

"That is one reading."

"It's the reading that explains why my brother is behind a corridor that won't answer."

"It explains your fear. It does not explain the ink." He tapped the slate. "Strike-throughs are budget hands. Vendor contracts are compliance hands. No signatures of malice. No covert ops grammar. No sudden zeros. Slopes."

"Slopes can be engineered."

"Slopes can be neglected."

"Neglect and engineering look the same from the bottom."

He considered that. "Yes," he said. "That is why archives exist. And why archives are slow."

She wanted to shake him. "People are flying fringe corridors because the map moved. Jun's models say belief is locking in. Cai is on a scaffold trusting a hum. And you're telling me to wait for Vault 9."

"I'm telling you not to give fear a face it hasn't earned."

"You gave me a list of forty-three faces."

"I gave you forty-three institutions that forgot to count." His voice stayed level. "Those are not the same noun."

Lena stood. Her legs ached from the annex walk. "Jun thinks—"

"Jun thinks in clusters because clusters are his job." Toma's gentleness was worse than shouting. "You think in routes because routes are yours. I think in slopes because slopes are what the stone shows. We are all reading the same page nine. We are not reading the same story."

"Jun has a cluster at zero point seven six."

Toma closed his eyes briefly—not defeat, recalibration. "Jun has a cluster at whatever number makes publication possible. Do not conflate his cluster with my list."

The sentence landed where she'd been trying not to listen.

*Do not conflate.*

She heard it and let it slide off because Jun's number had teeth and Toma's patience felt like permission to keep waiting while Arin stayed in **DELIVERED** without **READ**.

"I'm not conflating," she said. "I'm connecting."

"Connecting is Jun's word for publication."

"Connecting is my word for not standing still."

She left before he could answer gently again.

In the corridor she passed a keeper mirror kiosk flashing Cai's packet—page one names, thread growing—and forced herself not to stop. If she stopped she'd post. If she posted she'd become weather for Jun's cluster. She kept walking, bag heavy, page nine burning in her slate like a corridor she could hear but not see the end of.

Toma watched her go and said *do not conflate* once more to the empty corridor—not for her, for himself. Present fear was valid. Past record was relevant. Jun's cluster was publication grammar. Page nine was stone grammar.

He'd seen all three collide before. The collision never produced villains on schedule.

---

She took the tram to the lower annex where keeper mirrors lived next to separatist threads—an accident of indexing that felt like a joke the moon was making at her expense.

She pulled thread history on vendor contract **7-A** and found praise language: *efficiency*, *modernization*, *reduced duplication*.

She pulled oversight circle minutes from a century ago and found worry language buried in appendices: *single-point dependency*, *vendor lock-in*, *loss of guild memory*.

Warnings ignored politely.

She pulled nothing that named a villain.

She pulled everything that named a pattern.

A century-old oversight memo recommended extending vendor trials because *guild memory is costly*. A separatist pamphlet from last week called the same contract numbers proof of border warfare. Same digits. Different stories. Both felt true if you were tired enough.

Lena copied the memo into her **PURGE** folder and hated that the folder existed.

She walked the annex until her legs ached, pulling any file that mentioned **7-A**—vendor contract language appearing like a refrain across worlds that had never shared a border dispute. A maintenance chief on a fringe station wrote: *We kept the guild marks but lost the guild names. New hires think the dots are decorative.*

Decorative.

That word made her angrier than separatist pamphlets.

She sat on the annex floor with her back against stone because benches had started feeling like permission to wait. Her courier bag held page one, page nine, page twelve, the pamphlet photo, the keeper poster photo, Jun's cluster layer, and three appendix tables—weight she could measure in shoulders.

A fringe pilot two rows over complained about **ARCHIVE MOON — PROVISIONAL BERTH** fees loud enough for the whole annex to hear. Lena didn't join. She'd chosen this berth. She'd chosen this list. She was choosing what it meant with every copy she made.

She pulled one more file—oversight circle minutes, Year 208, appendix buried: *Recommend extending vendor trial — guild memory costly — single-point dependency risk noted.*

Noted.

Not acted.

The minute could have been Meridian Item 16's cousin. Same handwriting discipline. Same fear of honest verbs.

She tagged it **PURGE — SUPPORT** and hated herself a little for the tag.

Another hour. Another file. A junction keeper oral log—voice transcript, Year 222—describing vendor inspectors who photographed seals without touching them: *They called it modernization. We called it watching paint.*

Lena listened with one earbud and watched her own reflection in the annex glass—courier patch, tired eyes, a woman who'd started this trail with a self-opened query six days before Cai's chain and had ended it in a folder named for the wrong conclusion.

She wasn't stupid. She knew Toma would call it misread.

She knew page twelve existed.

She knew Jun's zero point seven six was provisional.

She knew all of it and still felt the list pacing in her ribs because knowing didn't move cargo and didn't read **DELIVERED** and didn't put a body on Orinreach Basin within reach of her hands.

Practical truth: she needed a story strong enough to walk on until a better one arrived.

The purge story arrived first.

---

At midnight in her berth capsule she opened Jun's cluster chart layer on page nine institutions and watched lines connect—relay guilds to junction keepers to oversight circles, a web tight enough to feel intentional.

Jun had sent a message an hour ago:

*If sabotage is wrong, this is the next best story. Coordinated thinning precedes coordinated failure. I can publish provisional by morning.*

She typed: *Don't publish yet.*

His reply: *Then help me make it true.*

A second message followed before she could throw the slate across the capsule:

*Cluster holding 0.76 overnight. Separatist pamphlets citing same contract digits as keeper mirrors. Belief convergence accelerating. Call me when you're ready to verify, not when you're ready to sleep.*

She stared at the words until the viewport darkened and the hollow mountains became silhouette.

Help me make it true.

That was the courier's old sin—wanting a story that fit enough to walk on.

She pulled up Arin's message stack again—six weeks of silence after **DELIVERED**, one photograph of a mark, storms, tourists. Ordinary weather on his side. Extraordinary silence between them.

She typed a line she didn't send: *I'm building a story. Tell me if it's wrong.*

Deleted it. Queued nothing. Her brother didn't need her theories. He needed her to show up—or to stop sending theories dressed as love.

If purge was wrong and erosion was right, the enemy wasn't separatists or administrators with secret maps.

The enemy was habit—the kind that let you stop paying keepers while still trusting green lights.

That didn't fit in a pamphlet.

That didn't give her a throat to shout at.

It might still be true.

She thought about Toma's *do not conflate* and heard herself answer it in the dark: *I'm not conflating. I'm done waiting.*

She thought about Jun's *coordinated* and Cai's dot on a housing seam.

She thought about Arin on Orinreach Basin sending pictures instead of answers.

She thought about the corridor collision—pamphleteer and keeper, same **7-A**, different wars—and how both sides had sounded tired enough to be sincere.

She drafted a message to Toma she didn't send:

*What if the slope is the weapon?*

Instead she saved page nine to her personal archive, tagged it **PURGE — PROVISIONAL**, and felt the wrong conclusion settle into her chest with the grim satisfaction of a breakthrough.

It wasn't proof.

It wasn't names she could knock on doors for.

It was better than silence.

It was worse than patience.

Outside, vault climate hummed. Inside, Jun's cluster lines glowed. Somewhere on the Pilgrim Road a junction held because someone checked a cable. Somewhere on Orinreach Basin her brother climbed or didn't climb.

Lena closed her eyes and let the list become footsteps in a corridor at night—engineered, coordinated, faceless on purpose.

She tried once more to read page twelve slowly—the way Toma would have wanted—sitting up in the capsule, slate brightness low, habit language unspooling in honest sentences about malice versus neglect.

*Assume habit. Habits are harder to fight and easier to document.*

The sentences were true. They were also cold. They did not warm a berth. They did not open **RESERVED**. They did not make Arin answer.

She closed page twelve and returned to page nine because fear, like couriers, moved toward what fit.

Tomorrow she would ask Veya what historians called it when institutions forgot slowly.

Tomorrow she would still need a villain.

Tonight the purge story felt like the first story strong enough to carry her back toward a gate.

She opened her route ledger before sleep—not archive, routes, the ledger that still knew her job when theories didn't. **ORINREACH BASIN — LONG — RESERVED**. **FRINGE — VIABLE — NADI**. **FIRST GATE — WAIT**.

Bodies before conclusions. She couldn't fly tonight. She could plan a body moving.

She added a ledger row: **ARCHIVE MOON — EXTEND BERTH — 48H** and winced at the fee. Waiting had decimals too.

She knew—somewhere under the satisfaction—that Toma would call it misread and Veya would call it cemetery and Cai would still be on a scaffold cutting dots into housing that supervisors called visually sufficient.

Knowing didn't help yet.

She slept with page nine glowing in her slate cache and separatist verbs in her teeth, dreaming of corridors with faces at the end that the archive refused to name.

At 03:14 her body woke her before the alarm—courier habit, not courage. The capsule viewport showed hollow mountains in silhouette. Vault hum in stone. Footsteps in her head again, forty-three entries pacing a corridor she couldn't see the end of.

She opened the slate in the dark.

Jun's cluster lines. Page nine list. Pamphlet photo. Keeper poster. Page twelve habit language sitting like a rebuke she refused to read slowly.

*Do not conflate.*

She whispered it once to the capsule glass and didn't believe it.

She typed to Nadi: *Still on moon. Found a list. Might be wrong. Might be enough.*

Reply five minutes later: *Lists don't fly. Eat. Sleep. Fly when it hurts less.*

Lena ate a second protein bar she didn't taste and slept again until the alarm—jaw still set, story still wrong, story still strong enough to stand on until a better one arrived.
