# Chapter 18 — Whose Hand

By the third slow road after Merrow, Lena could no longer pretend the lists were only lists.

At first she had wanted them to be small.

That shamed her, but it was true. Small things could be carried — folded, tucked behind a dead scanner panel, copied by lamplight, traded for passage. Small things did not demand that she become anything other than a courier with good eyes, a sore shoulder, and an old habit of moving before grief found its full weight.

The lists had begun that way. Scraps. Warnings. Local oddities. *Marsh-light gone green, out of season. Cooling-river milky three days. Sixth-day tide a quarter late — tell the next.* The kind of thing a frightened settlement might write because no official board was answering and the world had become too strange to trust memory alone.

Then Veya had shown her the old return work.

*Observe. Send. Answer.*

Then Cai had shown her the hidden fixes.

*What we told the office versus what we did.*

Then Lio had placed pages side by side and made her see comparison as a kind of handwork.

Then Merrow had gone dark above a village doing nearly everything right, and the failure notes had left by two runners with the line Sera had added: *Need outgoing acknowledgment if received.*

Now, on the slow roads beyond Merrow, the scraps kept arriving with that same shape.

Not identical. That would have made her suspect an office, and lately she trusted offices least when they were most coherent. These were not stamped forms. They came on paper, vellum, slate rubbings, cloth tags, thin strips of bark. Some written cleanly, some badly. One described a wrong wind for half a page before mentioning that the lower ferry rope had sung under no load, which seemed to Lena the more alarming fact.

But beneath the mess, a pattern held.

*What changed.*
*When.*
*Who saw it.*
*Who else saw the same.*
*Where sent.*
*Answer if received.*
*Tell the next.*

Not official. Not standardized. Taught.

That was the word Lena avoided for almost a day after it came to her.

Taught meant a teacher.

A teacher meant a hand.

And she knew, before anyone told her, whose hand it was.

The road from Merrow to the old Strake crossing was not a road in the gate sense. It ran along a low ridge, lost interest near a bog, became three planks over tea-colored water, returned as a track between stone walls, and offered the choice between a longer climb with bad footing and a shorter descent everyone advised against.

Lena traveled with six others because Sera had insisted.

"You are not required to perform solitude at professional standard," Sera had said when Lena tried to leave alone.

"I move faster alone."

"You also collapse more privately."

"I'm not collapsing."

"No. You are preserving collapse in a travel-safe form."

That had been unfair enough to be useful.

So she left Merrow with Yov, one of Sera's road hands, two young runners carrying the official-not-official failure packets, a Halen wheelwright whose wagon had become storage for shared supplies, a woman from the fruit convoy who knew three ruined bridges by reputation, and a boy of fourteen who had attached himself by carrying water briskly enough that no one had the heart to detach him.

His name was Tev. He had written *bedding-food* in the Merrow flour count and had not stopped arguing about it since.

They made poor speed and good records.

At every stopping place, Lena asked.

Not loudly. Not with Varik's talent for gathering a room. She wanted edges: peddlers, boat hands, mill clerks, old women with baskets, children who knew where adults hid notes. She asked for unseasonals, local things, scrap-lines moving hand to hand.

The first night, at a sheep shelter with one wall missing and a roof that had negotiated terms with the rain, Yov produced three waxed slips from his coat.

"Didn't think they mattered," he said.

Lena looked at him.

He shrugged. "Didn't think they mattered enough to give a stranger until she asked like they might."

The slips had come from ridge farms two valleys east.

*Lambs born with winter-coat in warm week.*
*Upper moss brittle though wet.*
*Well-stone sweating before rain, not after.*
*Tell the next.*

The second slip included a reply mark from a place called Annor Field.

*Saw brittle moss also. Sheep fine. Watching wells.*

The third, newer, said:

*Well-stone sweating here too. Ask lake people if black spring early.*

Lake people.

Lena read the line twice.

"Where did this come from?" she asked.

Yov pointed generally east, which was not an answer but seemed to be a philosophy.

"Who wrote *ask lake people*?"

"Don't know. Came through the oat mill with a flour tally. Mill woman said it came from a school packet before that. Or a boat. She was busy being annoyed by barley."

"*Ask lake people,*" Lena said.

"Good people to ask about water, I suppose."

"Yes."

She tucked the slips into her packet, hands steady only because she made them be.

The next morning, at the Strake crossing, the bridge keeper had nailed a board beside the toll box.

*No origin sorting.*
*No passage claims without need.*
*If carrying local changes, register with Mari or leave copy in tin.*

The tin was an old tea tin, dented, tied to the post with wire.

Inside were eleven scraps.

Lena took them out one by one, copied them by hand, and returned them in order because by then she had learned that order was sometimes the only clue left.

A quarry settlement reported iron dust clinging to compass glass.

A canal village reported two mornings of upstream foam.

A hill shrine reported bells sounding flat in fog, though the bell metal had been tuned that spring.

A child had drawn three moths beneath a bridge and written, in careful block hand:

*LOW AGAIN. TEACHER SAYS ANSWER.*

Lena sat on the bridge step for a long time with that scrap in her hand.

Tev crouched beside her. "Is that important?"

"Yes."

"How important?"

"I don't know yet."

"People keep saying that when they mean *very.*"

"That is because *very* is not a measurement."

"It could be if everyone agreed."

She looked at him.

He looked pleased with himself.

"Don't become a philosopher," she said.

"Is that paid work?"

"No."

"Then I won't."

The bridge keeper, Mari, came out with a kettle and a limp. She was perhaps fifty, perhaps older, with a red birthmark across one cheek and the expression of a woman refusing to be impressed by weather.

"You copying the tin?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Good. Take the moth one."

"I can't take the original."

"Not original. Fourth copy. Children make them now. They like being official."

"They are official?"

"No." Mari poured tea into a cup and handed it to Lena. "That's why they're useful."

Lena took the tea. "Who told you to keep the tin?"

Mari leaned on the railing and looked down at the narrow river. "Nobody told me."

"Who suggested it?"

"That's different."

"Who suggested it?"

"Packet came through from west marsh. Said wrong signs should be left where roads narrow, because people stop where roads narrow."

That was Arin.

Not the west marsh. Not the hand perhaps. But the sentence.

*Because people stop where roads narrow.*

It sounded obvious only after someone had loved a place enough to learn where bodies hesitated — the kind of thing he would have said to Lena years ago, annoyed that she kept calling the gate easy.

"Did it have a name?" she asked.

"No. Most don't."

"What did it have?"

Mari thought. "A mark. Three water lines and a slash."

Lena turned sharply.

"What?"

"Three water lines. Slash leans left. Ugly little thing. I remember because a seal that uneven should apologize."

Jaro's mark, from Arin's packet. North Lock.

The line had come through Jaro or Arin or the Tarn. The thought moved through Lena's body before it became language. Not proof. Not surprise. Recognition.

A lock mark from the Tarn had told a bridge keeper far from the Tarn where wrong signs should be left. A lake beyond a dark gate had sent not only observations, but practice.

*Leave copies where roads narrow.*

*Tell the next.*

*Answer if you see the same.*

Lena stood too fast. The tea sloshed over her hand, hot enough to sting.

Mari raised one eyebrow. "You know the water-lines?"

"I know someone who might."

"That is the least useful kind of knowing."

"It's what I have."

"Then keep it dry."

She gave Lena a piece of cloth for the tea and would not accept thanks.

By the time they reached the old Strake ferry, the thread had begun to pull.

Not physically. No single cord, no central route, no hidden office with a map and pins. Only scraps, signs, repeated closing lines, bridge tins, mill ledgers, flour tallies, gate failure notes, and the occasional onion crate because Lio had not exaggerated the usefulness of onions.

But a thread did not need to be straight to be real.

The ferry was out.

Not gone. Out. A distinction the ferryman made with great moral force while standing beside a boat half-pulled onto the mud, its hull patched in six places and its left runner removed.

"Out means repairable," he said. "Gone means poets get involved."

"How long?" Yov asked.

The ferryman spat into the river, considered the spit's journey, and said, "Depends what you mean by time."

Yov sighed. "Always bad when they start there."

The delay trapped them at Strake for a day.

Lena would have resented the wait once. Now she used it like a table.

She spread the scraps in the ferry house, weighted them with pebbles, and began arranging them. Not by place, severity, or route — offices and Varik and courier habit all wanted those — but by what had come back changed.

Unanswered.

Answered once.

Answered, then corrected.

The last pile was smallest.

It was also the most alive.

*Saw brittle moss also. Sheep fine. Watching wells.*

*Not our marsh-light. Ours blue-green under storm. Yours old-metal? ask frogs.*

*Frogs stopped here too.*

*Black water pulse North Lock. Answer if same.*

*No black pulse. Silver film on well. Similar cold?*

*Similar cold. Not same.*

This was not rumor.

Rumor wanted sameness. Rumor smoothed the world until every wrong thing became one hand, one enemy, one shape to hate without bending close.

The lists were doing the opposite.

They preserved difference.

*Not same. Similar. Watching. Ask. Answer.*

Lena stared at those words until the ferry house faded around them.

That was the hand. Not the handwriting, not the seal, not even the water-lines. The refusal to make the unseasonals too quickly one thing.

Arin.

She did not know when certainty entered. It was not dramatic. It settled the way a load settles when tied correctly: pressure distributed, movement reduced, the body aware that something had become carryable.

Arin would write that way.

He would not say the marsh-light was the same as the lake. He would ask whether it was. He would irritate everyone into saying exactly what they meant by cold. He would distrust any sentence that traveled too well.

*No, it's just fast.*

Gods, she had hated him for that.

Now the sentence sat beside the scraps like an old tool she had used wrong her whole life.

Tev watched from the opposite bench, chin in hand.

"You look like you found a ghost."

"No."

"A tax?"

"Closer."

He came around the table and looked at the piles. "Why those?"

"Those answered."

"I thought they were all messages."

"Messages go out. Answers come back changed."

"That sounds harder."

"It is."

"Then why do it?"

Because otherwise the *Wren* dies. Because otherwise Varik wins the room. Because otherwise Arin stands by wrong water forever.

Lena said, "Because a message alone can leave someone lonely with the truth."

Tev considered this.

"That's bad?"

"Yes."

He nodded, as though filing a category.

A woman in a patched blue coat entered with a basket of boiled eggs and a stack of slips tied in thread. She stopped when she saw Lena's piles.

"You're the Vesper courier," she said.

"I'm Lena."

"People say both."

"People are greedy."

The woman smiled faintly. "I'm Rennet. I run eggs, thread, and bad news between Strake and the lower farms. Mari said you were copying the tin."

"Mari says many things."

"All of them like she's tired of you already. That's affection."

Rennet set down the eggs and the slips. "These came with a ridge boy at dawn. Said they were to go to anyone keeping the answering pages."

Lena's hand went still.

"Answering pages?"

"That's what he called them."

"Who taught him that?"

"Do children admit sources where you come from?"

"No."

"There you are."

Rennet untied the thread and laid the slips beside Lena's piles.

More observations. More wrongness. One from Low Aster: an old bell rung without being struck during a gate flicker thirty miles away. One from a ferry line east of Merrow: three families arrived carrying Merrow's failure notes, asking whether Halen traders elsewhere had been blamed. At the bottom of that one, in a different hand:

*Merrow says: Halen convoy not cause. Repeat if needed.*

Lena touched the line.

Already moving. A community stranded behind a dark gate had still managed to send a sentence that could protect a stranger in another yard.

She copied the Merrow line three times.

Rennet watched.

"You know where these started?" Lena asked.

"Which?"

"The answering pages. *Tell the next. Answer if same. Leave copies where roads narrow.*"

Rennet's face shifted. "West water, I think."

Lena looked up.

"Or north water," Rennet said. "Hard to say. Marks came through the marsh first; marsh folk said lake people. Lake people say everything as if the rest of us are fools for living near solid ground."

"Lake people where?"

Rennet shrugged. "Tarnward."

There it was again. The thread, tugging.

"Did you see a name?"

"No. Initial maybe. A. Something. The first packet I saw had a note on the back. Here."

She sorted through the slips, found one older than the rest, and placed it in front of Lena.

The paper was creased, traveled. At the top: winter-skin, moths low, black water pulse. Below, after a line break, a note in a different register.

*Do not make these one thing too soon. Same-looking wrongness may have different causes. Send the difference. Ask for answer. The road will lie if only movers speak. Stayers know when the familiar first goes strange.*

Lena read it once.

Then again.

The ferry house went quiet, though it had not been loud before. Tev stopped moving. Rennet looked at the eggs in her basket. Yov snored on the bench by the stove, oblivious.

Lena did not need the signature.

There was none.

She did not need the seal.

It was there, faint at the corner: three water lines, one uneven slash.

She knew the hand because she knew the wound.

That was Arin. Not the boy at the table. Not the brother reduced to the one line she had kept as accusation for years. The man the Tarn had made: precise, stubborn, suspicious of speed — and now sending that suspicion outward so other people could use it.

While she moved, Arin had stayed.

Recognition did not feel like relief.

It felt like being forgiven in a language that did not ask whether she deserved it.

She folded the slip carefully and pressed her thumb against the water-line mark.

"Arin," she said.

Not loudly. Barely sound at all.

Tev heard. "Who?"

"My brother."

Rennet's eyes lifted.

"The lake person?"

"Yes."

"He's yours?"

The phrasing struck her oddly. *Yours.* As if people could be carried in the possessive.

"No," Lena said after a moment. "I'm his."

That embarrassed her immediately, but not enough to take back.

Rennet, being kinder than most strangers, pretended to inspect an egg.

Tev said, "Does that mean he started the lists?"

"No."

Lena knew that as soon as she said it. Marsh-watchers, Anneth tides, moth counts, Cai's notebooks, Veya's screw, Sera's acknowledgment line — none of it began with one hand. But Arin had made something the scraps could travel through.

Outside, rain began to tap the ferry house roof, light at first, then harder. The patched places revealed themselves one by one. Yov woke when water hit his forehead and swore at the ceiling as though betrayal required literacy.

Rennet moved the eggs. Tev rescued two pages from a drip. Lena gathered the answered pile and weighted it again on the drier side of the table.

The ferry remained out until morning. By then the rain had washed the road into ruts and the river had risen by half a hand — "not ideal but lacking poetry," said the ferryman. He and three others wrestled the repaired runner back onto the hull while everyone else carried goods across in smaller loads.

Lena used the delay to copy Arin's note — six times, not perfectly. *A copy is not a replacement. It is a promise to answer.* So she answered.

Under his closing lines she wrote only:

*Received. Seeing similar cold, not same. Ask who sees different. Answer back.*

She made six copies of that too.

Rennet read one over her shoulder.

"That's a lot to ask of a person with wet boots."

"Yes."

"Good. People with wet boots are underrated."

"Will you carry one?"

"To whom?"

"Where roads narrow."

Rennet smiled. "That sounds like a tax."

"It is."

"Paid in?"

"Answer."

"Expensive."

"Very."

She took two.

The ferry crossing took most of the morning. They went in pairs, then with cargo, then with the Halen wheelwright's storage wagon half-disassembled. At the center, with rain in her eyes and the far bank sliding too slowly closer, Lena felt the old fear of crossings return: between one side and the other, all categories were temporary.

Not here. Not there. Not safe. Not lost.

In transit.

The *Wren* had been in transit.

She gripped the side of the ferry until her fingers hurt.

On the far bank, a runner was waiting.

Not for her, at least not by name. He was waiting for the ferry because everyone waited at narrow places now, and because waiting places had become where the lists changed hands. He was older than most runners, with one sleeve pinned empty at the elbow and a leather tube slung across his back.

"Mari sent word," he said to the ferryman, then saw Lena. "You the one answering pages?"

Lena almost denied it. Not because it was false. Because it was too much.

"Yes," she said.

The runner took the tube from his back. "Then this is for you, or for whoever you are becoming."

Tev whispered, "That's dramatic."

"It's runner language," the man said. "We get bored."

Inside the tube were pages from Lio.

Not many. Not the living map itself. Lio knew better than to send that fragile thing onto wet roads. These were extracts, copied in their neat, severe hand: lines connected, sources noted, questions pending, acknowledgments received. A short letter wrapped around them.

*Lena,*

*If this reaches you by Strake, the Merrow packets arrived through the lower mill path. One copy already moved to Ivera, one to Vellum, one west. Sera's acknowledgment line is being repeated. Cai has answered Rallo. Humidity difference likely mattered. He is furious, which indicates usefulness.*

*Three new Tarnward packets arrived by west marsh and bridge tin. The hand you named is very likely the same source, or close to it. Not commanding. Teaching. Better: asking in a way that makes others more exact.*

*That is rare.*

*Do not rush to him only as sister. I know that is a cruel sentence. Answer the line too — carry what the place knows.*

*If you find him, do not bring him out of what he sees. Bring what he sees into relation.*

*Those who know a place before it becomes strange to others, and those who can carry strangeness without making it one thing too soon — we need both. Not a center. Not a hero. Bring them into contact if you can.*

*— Lio*

Below the signature, in a smaller hand that must have been Sael's, was written:

*If "contact" sounds too neat, revise before repeating.*

Lena laughed.

It came out suddenly enough to make the runner smile.

"Good news?"

"No."

"Bad?"

"No."

"The expensive kind, then."

"Yes."

She folded the letter and put it with Arin's note.

For a while, she could not move.

The others unloaded behind her, mud sucking at boots, ferry rope creaking, Tev arguing that *bedding-food* should be an official emergency category because the phrase improved morale. The world, unreasonable and damp, continued.

Lena stood under a leafless tree with Lio's letter in her hand and let the act of recognition finish.

Arin was not merely waiting.

She had imagined him waiting because guilt prefers still images. Arin by the lock. Arin by the lake. Arin looking at a board that did not answer. Arin hurt because she had not come. Arin dead because the mind tried to prepare the body.

But Arin had not only waited.

He had stayed.

And staying, in the world after the gates, had become work.

He had known the lake before the gauge. He had listened when children counted moths. He had believed old people's knees more than central measures. He had learned one place's wrongness well enough to teach other places how to describe their own without turning it into theory.

While she crossed worlds, he deepened one.

Neither was enough alone.

That was the recognition.

Not that her brother was alive. She still did not know that, not fully. A line was not a body. A seal was not a hand.

The recognition was that Arin was not waiting for her to invent what came next. He had been building the other half while she moved.

Lena looked back at the ferry house across the river, blurred by rain. Behind her: Vellum, Veya, Cai, Lio, Sera, Merrow, Vesper. Ahead, through slow roads and broken crossings, the Tarn.

Between them, scraps moved — badly, late, damp, misquoted, copied by children, left in tins.

It was ridiculous.

It was insufficient.

It was the first thing that had looked alive.

Yov called, "Road's not getting shorter while you brood."

"It might," Tev said. "Roads are behaving badly."

"Do not encourage her."

Lena tucked Lio's letter away and shouldered her pack.

The pack was heavier than it had any right to be. Veya's copies. Jun's pages. Merrow's failure notes. Cai's Rallo query. Lio's extract. Arin's note. Rennet's replies. The moth child. The bridge tin copies.

They started up the far road.

By afternoon the rain stopped and a cold wind came through the stone walls. The road climbed into country that had once served the fast gate indirectly — farms, smithies, inns declared obsolete and now crowded again, not all pleased by resurrection.

At a crossroads inn called The Bent Nail, someone had painted a new board beside the door:

*LOCAL CHANGES RECEIVED HERE*
*NO SABOTAGE CLAIMS WITHOUT WITNESS*
*NO WITNESS CLAIMS WITHOUT NAME*
*NO NAME POSTED WITHOUT CONSENT*
*ASK FOR ANSWER*

Beneath that, in smaller script:

*If in doubt, ask Elu. If Elu is asleep, wake someone nicer.*

Lena copied it.

Inside, the common room held more maps than tables: mud maps, chalk maps, torn maps with corrections, memory maps that started with *it used to be* and crossed themselves out. Pins marked bad bridges, rumor-heavy towns, food holds, safe wells, Halen danger, gates unstable, dark, or not to be trusted even if lit.

At the far wall hung a sheet titled *ANSWERS RECEIVED.*

It listed thirty-two lines.

*Merrow failure note received at Ivera. Halen not cause repeated to east yards.*
*Rallo humidity high during sheep loss. Cai query answered.*
*Strake bridge tin copied west.*
*Tarn winter-skin seen also at Upper Venn? awaiting second sign.*
*Moths low confirmed at Pike School and Low Aster.*
*Green marsh-light not same as blue storm-light. Do not combine.*
*Drevin Halen detentions unverified as sabotage. Treat as danger to people, not proof of cutting.*
*Varik phrase "emergency local distribution" used at Sarn to seize private medicine; harm reported. Awaiting details.*

Lena went cold.

There it was.

His words traveling better than he did. Opening a hold at Khoury. Authorizing harm at Sarn.

She copied the line with a hand that wanted to shake and did not.

"*Do not combine,*" she whispered.

"What?" Tev asked.

"Nothing. Everything."

The innkeeper, presumably Elu or someone nicer, came over with a tray of cups. "You're Lena."

"I am increasingly tired of being announced by strangers."

"That means yes?"

"Yes."

"Good. A packet came for you."

"Of course it did."

The innkeeper gave her a folded sheet sealed with blue glass.

Sael.

The note was brief.

*Lio says the line is tightening. Cai says that is structurally meaningless and emotionally obvious. Veya says stop accepting metaphors before they do work. Toma says he said it first, which is untrue.*

*We have enough fragments to know the lists are not random. We do not have enough to make them safe.*

*The Tarn hand teaches exactness without central claim. If it is your brother, do not make him a symbol. Symbols are where living work goes to be admired instead of answered.*

*We are routing toward the old Osprey groundline. It may reach within two days of the Tarn if the lower locks still run. It will need people trusted on both sides of the road — who stay and who move.*

*This is not a request that you become that person.*

*This is an observation that you are inconveniently placed.*

*— Sael Revised*
*lower mouth approved reluctantly*

Lena smiled despite herself at the signature.

Then she read the note again and stopped smiling.

Trusted on both sides.

No. Too much. Too clean. Too close to making her the bridge again.

But perhaps trust meant first carrying without owning.

*Bring them into contact if you can,* Lio had written.

Not hold them together alone.

The next morning, The Bent Nail sent out five packets: east to Ivera, west toward the marsh, back to Merrow, north by the Osprey groundline, and one on the wall under *ANSWERS PENDING*.

Lena took the north packet.

Not because it was hers. Because it was going toward the Tarn. Because it carried Arin's line and her answer beside it. If Arin was holding one end, reaching him did not mean pulling him away from it.

The road north from The Bent Nail did not deserve the name. It was more an agreement among rocks. The group thinned there: Yov stayed to help repair a culvert; the Halen wheelwright turned east with the Sarn warning; Rennet, reappeared with more eggs and no explanation, took two packets west. Tev tried to stay with Lena until every adult face seemed to channel Sera from miles behind.

"You're going back to Merrow," Lena said.

"I'm useful."

"Yes. There."

"I could be useful here."

"You are fourteen."

"That is temporary."

"So is being alive if you follow me."

"That's manipulative."

"Yes."

He glared at her, then held out a scrap.

"What is that?"

"For Merrow. Bedding-food, plus waiting-place, because people keep starving at ferries while arguing about time."

She took it.

"Your categories need work," she said.

"I wrote *revise after answer* on top but then I ran out of room."

"Good."

"Will you answer?"

"If I can."

"That means maybe."

"Yes."

"Better than no."

"Worse than yes."

He looked older for a moment. Merrow had done that to him and would keep doing it. "Most true things are."

She folded the scrap and put it with the others.

Then she went north.

For a day, she traveled with only the Osprey runner, a woman named Dalla whose entire personality seemed to consist of walking uphill and disapproving of audible breathing. They shared dried roots, silence, and one argument about whether a path counted as passable if using it required crawling under thorn vine.

Dalla said yes.

Lena said language had obligations.

Dalla said obligations were easier downhill.

At dusk they reached the groundline house.

It stood in a hollow between two ridges, half-buried in moss, built before the gates had made such structures embarrassing: a low stone building with a rusted mast and black ceramic insulators running north into the trees. The door stuck. Dalla kicked it twice and called this maintenance.

Inside, the air smelled of mouse, damp wire, and old lightning.

The groundline apparatus filled the main room: hand cranks, signal drums, a message plate, switches with labels worn nearly blank. Most of it was dead. Some had been repaired recently.

That stopped Lena in the doorway.

New wire. Fresh resin. A replaced handle wrapped in cloth. A slate hung beside the message plate with careful instructions:

*Crank six slow before sending.*
*Wait for lower tone.*
*Do not send if upper tone answers first.*
*Short packets only.*
*Confirm receipt by repeat mark.*
*If no repeat, send by road.*

At the bottom:

*Line lies if no one answers.*

No signature.

Three water lines. One slash.

Lena put one hand against the doorframe.

Dalla, seeing her face, softened by perhaps one degree. "Yours?"

"My brother's."

"Useful family."

"You have no idea."

"Rarely do."

The runner went to the apparatus and set her packet on the plate.

"Will it reach?" Lena asked.

"Maybe."

"How far?"

"Old Osprey line branches before the dark. If Tarn lower locks still keep a listening house, it may knock within a day's walk. If not, we will feel foolish."

She checked the slate, cranked six slow, and waited.

For a long moment, nothing.

Then a tone came from the wall.

Low. Faint. Real.

Dalla's eyebrows lifted.

"Lower tone," she said.

Lena could not breathe.

The runner began sending.

Not words. Code. Short pulses through an old line gates had made obsolete and somebody — Arin, or someone taught by him — had repaired enough to listen.

Not fast like a gate.

Not beautiful.

A series of knocks traveling through a damaged world.

Lena stood beside the apparatus and thought: this is what we have instead of certainty.

Knock.

Wait.

Answer.

The first packet sent.

Dalla waited for the repeat mark.

Nothing.

The silence lengthened.

Lena's hope climbed too high and became dangerous.

Then the wall clicked back.

Once. Twice. A pattern.

Dalla listened, head tilted. "Repeat mark received."

Lena sat down hard on the nearest bench.

The bench held.

The world did not become safe. The gates did not open. Arin did not walk through the door. The Tarn was still beyond dark, and a repeat mark was not a voice, not a body, not arrival.

But somewhere north, the packet had reached someone.

Someone had answered.

Dalla sent the second packet: Merrow failure note condensed. Halen not cause. Need acknowledgment.

Repeat mark.

Third: Cai-Rallo humidity query result. Conditions matter. Do not copy fixes without local comparison.

Long pause.

Repeat mark.

Fourth: Arin-origin line acknowledged. Lena answering. Moving north by Osprey groundline. Bring what stays and what moves into contact if possible. Do not leave place. Repeat: do not leave place only to meet me.

Dalla looked at her before sending that one.

"You sure?"

"No."

"Best available?"

"Yes."

She sent it.

This time the silence after was longer.

So long Dalla checked the line.

So long Lena had time to imagine all the ways a sentence could die: wrong branch, broken relay, unread plate, Arin gone, Arin alive and angry, words arriving too late.

Then the wall clicked.

Not the repeat mark.

Something longer.

Dalla straightened.

"What?" Lena asked.

"Message."

The code came slowly. Whoever sent it was far, unpracticed, or forcing old equipment to behave beyond dignity. Dalla wrote as she listened.

*Received.*
*Holding.*
*Need people who can answer the line.*
*Do not come alone.*
*A.*

*A.*

Only that.

Lena stared at the letter until it blurred.

A line was not a body.

A mark was not proof of safety.

*A* could be copied. *A* could be sent by another hand. *A* could be old.

But she knew.

Not with certainty. She distrusted certainty now. This was something older and more frightening: recognition shaped by enough difference to stay true.

He was alive enough to answer, or his work was alive enough to answer for him.

Lena pressed both hands over her face.

No sob came. Not yet. The feeling was too large and too full of tasks.

Dalla looked away with a runner's tact.

After a while, Lena lowered her hands.

"Can we send back?"

"Short."

Lena nodded.

She thought of what to say as sister.

*I'm coming. I'm sorry. I should have answered sooner. I kept your notes. You were right about fast. I am afraid you are dead even while I answer you.*

All true.

None first.

Lio: answer the line too.

So she said, "Send: Received. Lena moving north. Not alone. Stay. Keep sending difference. We will bring the lines together."

Dalla wrote. "That all?"

Lena swallowed.

"No. Add: I see it now."

Dalla did not ask what.

She sent.

The repeat mark came back faster this time.

Lena stood there until her legs steadied.

Outside, night had come over the ridges. No gate light, no city glow. Only the mast and the line running north, thin unless one knew to look.

The thread had pulled taut.

Not tight enough to trust.

Taut enough to follow.

By morning, they had made twelve copies of the exchange. Not the personal line — Lena kept that back. The rest went to Lio, Merrow, Vellum, Cai, the bridge tins, the west marsh.

*Holding. Need people who can answer the line. Do not come alone.*

On the back of each copy, in her own hand:

*Do not make this one thing too soon.*
*Answer with what your place knows.*
*Carry what another place needs.*

Dalla read that and nodded once.

"Long," she said.

"Yes."

"Good."

They left after first light, following the ceramic insulators north until the road dropped into a valley where three old routes crossed. If the line held, Lena might reach the outer Tarn approaches in days instead of weeks.

If.

The word no longer felt like weakness.

At the ridge top she turned back once. The groundline house was almost invisible below — a stone seam in moss.

She adjusted her pack.

It still held too much. It would hold more.

At the next crossroads, Dalla asked, "Where?"

Lena looked north, toward the line, the Tarn, Arin's water and the hand she knew before anyone named it.

Then west, east, south — Merrow's packet line, Ivera through Lio, Vellum's copies and Sera's notes.

One road would get her closer to Arin.

One web of roads might bring what he needed into contact with what she carried.

She understood that choosing between those two was the old mistake.

"North," she said. "But not alone. Send to Lio: whoever has been answering twice — their notes, not their theories. Not to me. To the Osprey groundline and every road-narrowing between here and Ivera."

Dalla stared. "That's a lot of hands."

"Yes."

"And if they won't come?"

"Then the line finds out who will."

They started down the north road.

Behind them, the old line clicked once, receiving something Lena could not see. Ahead, the Tarn remained hidden behind distance, water, dark, and the fact that recognition was not yet repair.

Arin was not waiting.

He had been building the other half.

Now the work was to bring the moving and staying lines into contact.

Whether they could cohere stayed beyond the next ridge.

For now, she walked north carrying his answer in her pack and his hand in the line, and every road was no longer only a way toward him.

It was a place where someone might need to leave a tin, where someone might stop, where the next true word might wait for a hand.

Lena walked, and the slow road did not feel like delay.

It felt like the only speed that could still learn.
