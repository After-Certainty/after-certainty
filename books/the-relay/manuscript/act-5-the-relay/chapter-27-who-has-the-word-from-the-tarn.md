# Chapter 27 — Who Has the Word From the Tarn

The first person to carry the new practice like news was not a courier.

That irritated Lena more than it should have.

She had spent most of her life believing movement was a trade with rules: receive, carry, deliver, leave. The road punished confusion. A courier could be late, wet, bribed, frightened, stubborn, hungry, misdirected, or wrong, but the job itself remained legible. Something moved because someone took it from one hand to another. The line was clean even when the road was not.

Then Sera left Vesper with six pages wrapped in oilcloth, three food ledgers under one arm, a cup tied to her belt by a strip of blue cloth, and the calm expression of a pilgrim who had once walked into towns with nothing but faith and now had the bad manners to bring paperwork.

Lena watched her from the gatehouse step.

"You are holding that wrong," Nadi said behind her.

"I am not holding anything."

"I meant your face."

Sera turned at the lower bend as if she had heard them, though she could not have. She lifted one hand. Not farewell. Not blessing. A small practical signal: received, carrying, likely to become inconvenient.

Then she went down into the road.

The road took her badly.

That was one of its better qualities. It did not care that Sera had once been welcomed in half the settlements between Merrow and Halen as the woman who could make a frightened room sit long enough to remember it had neighbors. It did not care that she knew the old songs, the illness rites, the way to ask for water without making the giver feel smaller for having less than she wanted. It did not care that people trusted her voice before they trusted most forms, officials, keepers, repairs, or promises.

Mud came for everyone.

By noon her hem was soaked, one boot had begun to take in water, and the oilcloth packet looked as if it had already survived a small war over meaning. Lena, walking beside her because no one had yet convinced her that not carrying meant staying politely behind, watched Sera stop under a road shelter where three families, two goats, a coil of cable, and a man with a bad burn on one wrist had arranged themselves according to discomfort and suspicion.

The shelter had once been painted green. Weather had reduced it to a more honest color. The roof sagged near the center. A noticeboard hung beside the entry with three old route warnings, one newer warning about washed stone, and a charcoal insult aimed at whoever had written the washed-stone warning too late to save a wheel.

The families knew Sera before they knew the pages.

That mattered.

A woman with a baby tied against her chest stood when Sera came in. "Merrow?"

"Still there," Sera said.

The woman's shoulders dropped before she could discipline them. "Nessa?"

"Found. Not well. Alive."

The woman sat down too quickly, as if her knees had accepted the answer before the rest of her body knew what to do with it.

Only then did Sera unwrap the oilcloth.

Lena saw the change come over the shelter. It was not trust. Not yet. It was the old reflex people had learned around paper: fear that it would command them, hope that it would excuse them, resentment that it had arrived from elsewhere pretending to know the shape of the room. The burned man looked toward the door. One of the goats began chewing the edge of a feed sack with more conviction than courtesy.

Sera placed the first page on an overturned crate and put her cup beside it.

"Do not trust this," she said.

The shelter grew wary in a different way.

"That is not usually how pilgrims begin," the burned man said.

"I am expanding."

Lena leaned against the doorframe and said nothing. She had learned, with effort and frequent failure, that the first explanation often stole the answer from the person who needed to give it.

Sera touched the edge of the page. "This came from Low Cairn, Oss, South Lock, Ivera, Merrow, and the Tarn. It is incomplete, wet, argued over, and probably wrong in places."

"Then why bring it?" the woman with the baby asked.

"Because the wrong parts are named."

No one loved that. Lena liked it more for being unloved.

The burned man stepped closer. He did not touch the page. "I cannot read all that."

"Good," Sera said. "Then someone else will have to read with you."

His eyes sharpened. "I don't need pity."

"No. You need the part you saw written beside the parts you did not."

He stared at her, then at the page, then at the people in the shelter who had suddenly become the sort of people who might be asked something.

Sera waited.

That was the thing Lena had not understood about her before Merrow. Sera's waiting was not passive. It did not leave the room alone. It held a place open and made people decide whether they would enter it. She had once done this with tea, blankets, the right kind of silence, a hand on a shoulder no one else had thought to touch. Now she did it with a damp manifest, a cup, and a blank corner at the bottom of the page.

The burned man looked toward the noticeboard. "The washed stone wasn't washed."

One of the children stopped whispering.

He saw that and scowled, which made him look less ashamed and more like himself. "It was undermined from below. Road looked good until weight came through. First wagon passed. Second passed. Third had iron hinge pieces and dropped at the left wheel. We called it washed because the rain was there and the rain did not object."

Sera slid the pencil toward him.

"I said I cannot read all that."

"You do not have to read all of it to write the part you know."

He looked at the pencil as if it had been rude first.

The woman with the baby said, "I can write it."

He looked at her.

She lifted her chin. "You can say it. I can write. The page can be annoying to both of us."

This, Lena thought, was how it spread. Not with a declaration. Not by law. Not because anyone finally understood the system. One person with a burn on his wrist knew a road had lied under rain. One woman with milk on her sleeve could write the sentence. One pilgrim put the pencil where both of them had to see it.

The woman wrote:

*Road reported washed stone. Actually undermined before rain. Heavy hinge pieces made wrongness show. Question for Low Cairn and Oss: did heavy pass after good-looking road?*

Under who may be harmed she added:

*People who believe first wagon proves road.*

The burned man nodded once, grudging and exact.

Sera did not praise them. Praise would have made the page hers.

She only wrapped the corner with a scrap of dry cloth and said, "Who can carry this to the lower cable team?"

No one answered quickly.

That too was honest.

The child who had stopped whispering raised one hand, then lowered it when his mother saw. The goat finished with the feed sack and began considering the noticeboard.

The burned man said, "I can get it to Jost. He owes me."

"Does he owe you enough to read it?" Lena asked before she could stop herself.

The man looked at her. "No one owes anyone that much."

Sera smiled faintly. "Then ask for less. Ask him to add before passing."

The man took the page.

He did not look converted. That, too, was promising.

They left the shelter under a sky the color of old tin. Sera walked for almost a quarter mile before she spoke.

"You are trying not to ask whether I know what I am doing."

"I am succeeding."

"Poorly."

"Yes."

Sera adjusted the oilcloth packet beneath her arm. "I used to tell people to trust each other."

Lena looked at her.

"I did," Sera said. "In plague rooms. Flood kitchens. Bad winters. After gates failed before anyone wanted to admit gates could fail. Trust each other. Stay in the room. Tell the truth gently if you can and ungently if you must. Bring the soup before the argument becomes theology."

"That sounds useful."

"It was."

Sera said it without modesty. Lena liked her more for that.

"Useful things become proud too," Sera said. "People trusted me while I was there. Then I left, and the room either remembered how trust felt or it did not. Feeling is a poor archive. It saves lives and then evaporates before anyone can ask what it knew."

The road dipped. Water ran across the stone in a thin sheet. Sera stepped through it without pausing.

"This gives trust somewhere to sit when I leave," she said.

The sentence went into Lena quietly and stayed.

They reached Halen two days later by a road that should have taken one day if the old world had still been available to flatter itself. By then the pages had multiplied in ways Lena did not entirely approve of. Copies came on manifest backs, seed sacks, chapel notices, feed tallies, old school slates, and once on a torn strip of curtain because the woman who sent it said the curtain had been hiding a mold stain for years and might as well become useful at last.

The practice did not spread evenly. Nothing honest did.

At Korrin Bridge, a keeper refused the page because it had no seal and then copied three lines from it onto his load board when no one was looking. At East Fen, two sisters argued for twenty minutes over whether "who may be harmed" included people who chose stupidity freely, and Sera told them yes, but they could write freely after stupidity if precision mattered to them. At a winter storehouse near the Sedge Road, a clerk tried to assign numbering to the copies and Nadi, arriving by some unrelated route with mud to the thigh and murder in her posture, wrote on the numbering sheet: *first error — believing sequence means authority.*

No one knew where Nadi had come from.

No one asked wisely.

Halen had changed less than Lena expected, which was one of the crueler ways change announced itself.

The chain-link still leaned where the old pen had been, though someone had cut two more gaps through it since Lena last saw the place. The relief yard beyond the south hall was crowded but not frantic. Barrels stood under tarps. Children chased each other between stacked crates with the exhausted cheer of children who had learned where adults stopped seeing them. A chalkboard near the kitchen listed meals, routes, gate reports, and one line in a hand Lena recognized with a tightening she did not enjoy:

*PURPOSE NOW. HOPE LATER.*

Varik had not become less himself.

That was almost offensive.

He stood near the serving line with his sleeves rolled, handing a bowl to an old woman while listening to three people speak at once. He had lost weight. So had everyone. His hair was damp from work or rain. There was ash at one cuff. Nothing in him looked defeated, exposed, monstrous, or ashamed enough to satisfy a simpler story. People still turned toward him when they entered the yard. They still softened when he saw them. He remembered names. He noticed the man whose left hand shook and passed the bowl into his right without making a lesson of it. He asked after a fever. He told a child that her brother had been sent with the north convoy, not lost in it, and the distinction mattered because he made it matter.

Lena hated, for a moment, how much she still understood his usefulness.

Sera saw him and stopped.

"Do you want to leave?" Lena asked.

"No."

"Do you want me to?"

Sera looked at her, almost amused. "No."

"Do you want to become profound before entering?"

"No."

"Good."

Sera stepped into the yard.

Varik saw her after the old woman, before the next bowl. His face changed with real warmth. That was the worst of it, or one of the worsts. He looked pleased.

"Sera," he said.

"Varik."

"You made it out of Merrow."

"Merrow made it out of itself. I was present and irritating."

"I would expect no less."

A few people near the line smiled. They liked him. They liked her. The world had not arranged itself cleanly enough for one affection to cancel another.

Varik's eyes moved to the oilcloth packet in Sera's arms, then to Lena.

"Courier," he said.

"Still arguing with the title," Lena said.

"Good titles survive argument."

"No they don't."

His smile remained. "Some do."

Someone called his name from the chalkboard. A young man with a strip of cloth tied around one eye had a page in his hand and the look of a person desperate for the nearest confident adult.

Varik turned toward him at once. "What is it?"

"The north kitchen says the Low Cairn route is holding. The lower yard wants to send seed through before dusk. They say if we wait, rain closes the road. They say Tarn reports water rising but not at Low Cairn. I don't know which matters."

There it was. The old shape of the world, begging to be answered by a person with a steady voice.

The yard bent toward Varik.

Not dramatically. No one genuflected. No villain's music entered the air. A woman kept stirring a pot. A child dropped a spoon and was scolded for it. Someone cursed at a barrel lid. But the attention moved. Lena felt it because she had once moved with it. Here was a man who could take panic and give it a sentence.

Varik read the page. He read quickly, but not carelessly. He had never been careless. That was part of the danger.

"Do not send the seed by Low Cairn before dusk," he said.

The young man exhaled.

"Use the north road in two small loads," Varik continued. "Keep the kitchen line as scheduled. If rain closes the road, hold the third load at the chapel store. Tell the lower yard no carts. People only, hands free. If they insist on speed, they will create the delay they fear."

It was a beautiful answer.

Clean, memorable, mercifully shaped. It gave frightened people work they could do. It reduced argument without sounding like it despised argument. It probably saved at least one bad decision before supper.

The yard relaxed around it.

Lena did too, before she caught herself.

Sera looked at the page still in the young man's hand.

"Who wrote that Tarn report?" she asked.

The young man blinked. "What?"

"The water rising. Who wrote it?"

"Does that matter?"

"Yes."

Varik looked at Sera. Not warning. Not anger. Attention.

The young man glanced between them, suddenly wishing he had chosen a different yard, crisis, or century. "It came by relay. I don't know."

"Then the answer is not ready," Sera said.

A small impatience moved through the nearest listeners. Lena recognized it. People who had just received relief did not enjoy having relief made conditional again.

Varik said gently, "The seed cannot wait for perfect attribution."

"No," Sera said. "But it can wait for named uncertainty."

The phrase irritated everyone, including Lena, which suggested it might be useful.

Sera unwrapped one of the oilcloth pages and placed it on the edge of the serving table, between bowls, where it absolutely did not belong. A kitchen woman gave her a look that would have curdled uncommitted milk. Sera moved two bowls herself and did not apologize.

"Write Varik's answer," Sera said to the young man.

He looked relieved.

"Then write what it cannot see."

The relief died.

Varik's expression did not change quickly enough to be invisible.

Lena saw it: the smallest tightening around his eyes, not because he had been contradicted, but because the room had been offered a second center and might take it.

"What it cannot see," the young man repeated, as if the words had been assigned to his enemy.

Sera nodded toward the chalkboard. "You said Tarn reports water rising. Who has the word from the Tarn?"

No one answered.

The yard did not like that either.

A woman with flour on both forearms said, "Marrin came from the east table. He had the Tarn scrap."

"Marrin left for the lower yard," someone else said.

"No, he came back. I saw him by the barrels."

"He had a blue strip on his sleeve?"

"That was not Marrin. That was Teth."

"Teth can't read water reports."

"Teth can read numbers."

"Water is not numbers," said the kitchen woman, as if the entire yard should have known better.

The conversation frayed at once.

It should have been failure. In the old room, it would have been. Varik's answer had been usable; this was mess. But Sera did not rescue them from the mess. She only kept the page flat with one hand and waited as the yard found the thread it had lost by assuming someone else held it.

A large man carrying two empty flour sacks came up behind Varik and clapped him once on the shoulder with the unthinking familiarity of someone who liked him and was not making a statement about history.

"Good hold on the seed," the man said. "But who's got the word in from the Tarn?"

There it was.

No triumph. No public defeat. No gasp from a crowd realizing it had been deceived. The man did not withdraw his hand from Varik's shoulder as if touching him had become shameful. He squeezed once, friendly, impatient, and turned toward the yard.

"Come on," he said. "Who carried it?"

Varik looked at the man's hand on his shoulder.

Then at the yard.

Then at the page.

Lena felt something in the air shift so slightly that, had she not spent the last week watching bad gates hold by fractions, she might have missed it. Varik had not lost them. They had not stopped liking him. His answer still stood, useful and probably necessary.

But it no longer ended the room.

A girl near the barrel stack lifted a folded strip of paper. "I have Tarn."

Everyone turned.

She seemed horrified by the consequence of possession. She looked fourteen, maybe fifteen, with a scarf wrapped around her hair and ink on her thumb. She held the strip away from her body as if it might accuse her personally.

Sera beckoned her forward.

The girl came.

"What does it say?" Sera asked.

The girl swallowed. "Tarn south water rising before visible rain. Upper marsh full before Low Cairn report. Cause likely underrun, not rain only. Watch reed bend before road wet. If Ivera heat changes without rain, answer."

"That does not say no seed," the young man said.

"No," Sera said.

"It does not say send seed."

"No."

The kitchen woman wiped both hands on her apron. "It says the rain report is late to what the water already knew."

Sera looked at her. "Yes."

The woman glared, not at Sera but at the thought. "Then Low Cairn may look open while the road underneath is already changing."

The young man stared at Varik's answer on the page.

"It was still useful," he said, defensive before anyone accused him.

Varik spoke then. "Yes."

Everyone looked at him again, but differently this time. Not less. Differently.

He stepped closer to the serving table. "Write the hold. Then write the condition."

Sera's hand stayed on the page.

Varik did not reach for the pencil. That, Lena thought, was the first generous thing he had done without making it into generosity.

The young man wrote badly because people watching made handwriting worse.

*Seed not through Low Cairn before dusk. North road two small loads. Third held at chapel if rain closes road. No carts. People only, hands free.*

He paused.

The girl with the Tarn strip leaned over and added:

*Condition: Tarn says underrun may arrive before visible rain. Ask Low Cairn whether reed bend changed before road wet. If yes, no third load even if north road looks open.*

The kitchen woman added without asking:

*Who may be harmed: people waiting for seed if we keep seed too safe; people under washed road if we send too fast. Both are real. Do not let one erase the other.*

The large man still beside Varik said, "That is too long."

"Good," said the kitchen woman.

Varik smiled at that.

It was small. Real, as far as Lena could tell. Perhaps he saw the loss. Perhaps he saw the usefulness. Perhaps those were not yet separate in him. He looked, for a breath, less like a man being displaced than like a man watching the room do something he could not have made it do by answering well.

Then someone asked him whether the chapel store had dry floor.

He answered because he knew.

The page stayed on the table.

The yard moved around both.

That was how Varik faded.

Not by being unmasked. Not by confession. Not by a crowd turning cold. He did not fall. No one stripped his words from the chalkboard. A child still brought him an empty bowl to stack. The old woman he had served earlier touched his arm on her way out and thanked him. He bent to hear her because he was still the sort of person who bent to hear old women.

But when the next question came, it did not go straight to him.

It went first to the page.

Or to the person who had carried the page.

Or to the place the page had failed to name.

Or to the kitchen woman who knew what waiting cost in grain.

Or to the girl with ink on her thumb, now guarding the Tarn strip with the miserable dignity of someone who had become necessary before she felt ready.

Lena stood at the edge of the yard and felt no victory at all.

Only space.

Sera looked tired.

That was new. Not tired from walking; everyone was tired from walking. This was the specific exhaustion of a person watching an old gift become insufficient and feeling grateful instead of offended.

"You are all right?" Lena asked.

Sera watched the yard. "No."

"Is this one of those pilgrim answers where no means yes if held gently enough?"

"No."

"Good."

Sera's mouth curved. "That word really is a problem."

Near the serving table, the young man crossed out *before dusk* and wrote *before lower marsh answer*, then looked around as if expecting punishment.

No one punished him.

Someone did argue immediately.

That seemed healthier.

They slept that night in the old schoolroom above Halen's south hall, though sleep remained more theoretical than practical. The floorboards remembered every footstep from the yard below. Rain came and went. Someone coughed in the corner. Sera sat awake near the window, repairing the torn edge of an oilcloth wrapper with thread pulled from the hem of her own sleeve.

Lena woke before dawn and found her still there.

"You should sleep," Lena said.

Sera bit the thread. "That advice has been circulating for years. It has not improved with repetition."

Lena sat beside her.

Below them the yard had quieted into the ugly peace of people too tired to quarrel usefully. One lantern burned near the chalkboard. *Purpose now. Hope later.* Someone had added beneath it, in smaller writing:

*Answer if changed.*

Lena wondered whether Varik had seen it.

She suspected he had.

"You gave them something to trust each other with," Lena said.

Sera looked at the oilcloth in her lap. "I gave them something to fight over without pretending the fight was betrayal."

"That may be the same thing."

"Only in bad weather."

"It is usually bad weather now."

"Yes."

Sera tied the thread off. Her fingers were steady, but the skin around her eyes looked bruised by wakefulness.

"I thought trust meant staying soft enough for another person's truth," she said. "That is still true. But softness without a place to put what arrives becomes another kind of disappearance. People tell the truth in a room, and then the road forgets it. Or the road carries it, and the room cannot tell whether it survived the journey."

She folded the repaired oilcloth once, then again.

"This does not make trust safer," she said. "It makes trust answerable."

From Sera, the word *answerable* sounded almost like prayer and almost like accusation.

Lena leaned her head back against the wall. Through the floorboards, a murmur rose in the yard. Not panic. Not quite work yet. The first voices of morning finding the unfinished page.

"Do you miss when people just believed you?" Lena asked.

Sera considered.

"Yes."

Lena laughed quietly.

Sera's smile was small and sad enough to be trusted. "I also miss being young enough to think that was the point."

By midmorning Halen had produced six new pages, three bad copies, one argument about whether people should sign by name or work, and a child's drawing of the Low Cairn arch with a milk can on one side and a goat on the other for reasons no one could justify. Sera preserved the drawing because the child had marked the side where the road dipped before any adult mentioned the dip.

The practice moved out from Halen in unsatisfying directions.

A relief runner took the seed page toward the chapel store and added, halfway there, that the north road had two kinds of mud and only one had been named. A kitchen woman sent a copy to Merrow because the line about food being too safe had offended her into usefulness. The girl with the Tarn strip refused to surrender the original until someone showed her how to copy the hand and the smear around it, because she said the smear proved the page had come by wet road and wet road meant time.

Varik watched all this from the chalkboard.

Once, Lena saw a young mother ask him whether the chapel store was safe.

"It was dry last night," he said.

The mother waited.

Varik looked toward the table where the pages lay.

"Ask whether anyone has seen it since the rain," he added.

The mother nodded and went to ask.

No one applauded him for becoming less complete.

That may have saved the moment from becoming sentimental.

Later, near the loading arch, Lena found him alone for the first time since they arrived. He was tying a bundle of route strips with twine. The strips were practical, legible, and still very much his. Halen would continue to need people who could make work move before understanding finished putting on its boots.

He did not look up when she approached.

"Are you here to tell me I was wrong?" he asked.

"No."

"That is disappointing. I had prepared several gracious replies."

"I'm sure they were excellent."

"They were brief."

"That seems unlikely."

He smiled and finished the knot.

For a while they stood with the loading arch before them. It was not live. Not dead either. Thin, as too many things were now. A repair hand near the base listened to the housing with one ear pressed against a wooden cup, as if the gate were a wall with neighbors behind it.

"You gave them useful answers," Lena said.

"Yes."

"You still do."

"Yes."

He glanced at her then, and she saw annoyance, weariness, humor, and something that might have been grief if it ever stopped moving long enough to be named.

"They want the longer thing now," he said.

"Not always."

"No. Not always." He looked back at the yard. "When the longer thing frightens them enough, they will still come to the nearest person willing to sound certain."

"Yes."

"You should worry about that."

"I do."

"Good."

The word hung between them, overused and still not empty.

Varik picked up the bundle of route strips. "I was never trying to make them stupid."

"I know."

That answer cost more than she expected.

He heard the cost. Of course he did. He had always heard more than she wanted him to.

"But I liked," he said, with a quietness that did not ask forgiveness, "how quickly fear became useful when I gave it a shape."

Lena said nothing.

The repair hand at the gate moved the wooden cup and listened again.

"I told myself the shape could be corrected later," Varik said. "After people ate. After the road cleared. After the worst hour. After purpose had done what hope could not."

"And later kept moving."

"Yes."

He almost smiled. "You have become less satisfying to talk to."

"I have had help."

"So I see."

Someone shouted from the yard: "Who has the word from the Tarn?"

Varik's eyes moved toward the sound before Lena's did.

The girl with the inked thumb shouted back, "Not me. Mine is old. Ask the blue-sleeve carrier."

Another voice: "Blue sleeve went to chapel."

"Then ask chapel whether the word changed."

"Who has chapel?"

"Not Varik," someone called, not unkindly. "He has route strips."

A ripple of laughter crossed the yard.

Varik stood very still for one breath.

Then he laughed too.

Not loudly. Not as performance. A small, startled sound, gone almost as soon as it arrived.

Lena looked away in time to let him keep it.

When she looked back, he was carrying the route strips toward the loading line. No one made way for him as if he were the only person with an answer. They made just enough room for another useful body with full hands.

That was all.

It was enough.

Sera left Halen that afternoon with fewer pages than she had brought and more work than she could carry. This seemed to satisfy her. The pages had begun moving without her permission. Some of them would be ruined by rain. Some would be cleaned into danger. Some would arrive after the decision they might have changed. Some would be used by people who wanted credit for asking better questions. Some would be ignored because certainty, when frightened, did not always care that a better tool had entered the room.

Sera knew this.

She went anyway.

At the edge of the yard, the woman with flour on her arms pressed a wrapped heel of bread into Sera's hand. "For the road."

Sera accepted it.

The woman hesitated. "Should we call this something?"

"No."

"People will."

"Yes."

"What do we say when they do?"

Sera looked back at the yard: the chalkboard, the serving line, Varik's route strips, the page on the table with three hands bent over it, the girl with the Tarn strip refusing to let an older man copy the smear too neatly.

"Ask what the name is hiding," Sera said.

The woman considered this and looked displeased enough to remember it.

Lena and Sera walked out under a sky that had not chosen between rain and restraint. Behind them Halen did not transform. It did not become a city of shared wisdom because six pages had made its people more annoying to one another. It remained hungry, frightened, useful, proud, and full of people who still preferred a clean answer when the baby cried or the road closed or the arch thinned.

But the next time someone asked for the answer, another voice asked where it had come from.

The next time someone said *safe*, someone else asked for whom.

The next time someone said *send it*, a child asked whether the road had answered.

These were not noble changes. Noble changes looked better on walls. These were irritating changes. They slowed good people down. They made kindness show its arithmetic. They made competence leave fingerprints. They made faith carry a pencil.

Sera's pace slowed near the old chain-link, where the Halen pen had once taught Lena how easily rescue could become sorting. The gaps in the fence had widened. Someone had tied strips of cloth through the cut places so children would see the sharp wire before running through.

Sera touched one blue strip with two fingers.

"I told people to trust each other here once," she said.

Lena waited.

"They did," Sera said. "Some. For a while. Then the line moved, the bowls moved, the names moved, and trust stayed behind like a song no one had written down."

She looked at the road ahead.

"Now the song is uglier."

"Good," Lena said.

Sera gave her a tired look.

"I know," Lena said. "I am also worried about the word."

They walked on.

By dusk, a runner caught them at the lower bend. He was young, breathless, and furious about having been sent after two women who did not believe in waiting near roads where messengers could find them easily. He held out a folded page, spotted with rain and flour.

"From Halen," he said. "For Merrow, Vesper, Tarn if it gets that far, and anyone who thinks route strips mean permission."

Sera took it and opened it under the shelter of her own sleeve.

The writing was crowded. Varik's hand appeared first, clean and narrow:

*North seed held at chapel after marsh answer changed. Route strips remain useful only with last local answer attached.*

Below that, the flour-armed woman had written:

*Kitchen line delayed by hold. Hunger worsened before road improved. Do not call delay wisdom unless mouths counted.*

The girl with the Tarn strip had added in crooked letters:

*Old word from Tarn not enough. Ask changed word.*

At the bottom, in a different hand, someone had written:

*Who has the word from the Tarn?*

No signature.

No authority.

Only the question, already traveling.

Sera folded the page carefully along someone else's crease.

For a moment she held it against her chest, not as relic, not as proof, only as a thing that had come back altered and therefore alive.

Lena thought of Varik at the loading line, route strips in hand, still useful and no longer final. She thought of the girl guarding the smear. The kitchen woman naming mouths. The man at the road shelter admitting the road had lied before the rain. Sera, who had once carried trust in her own body and now handed people a way to keep it from disappearing when she left.

The world did not become less frightened.

But fear had lost one of its privileges.

It could no longer always choose the nearest clear voice and call the choosing wisdom.

The runner shifted impatiently. "Is there an answer?"

Sera looked at Lena.

Lena looked down the road, where the next shelter waited in rain-colored dusk, and beyond it another road, another gate, another room that would not know whether to trust a page until someone made the page troublesome enough to deserve it.

"Not yet," Lena said.

The runner groaned.

Sera handed the page back to him. "Good. Carry that."

He stared at her.

"Carry what?"

"That there is not an answer yet."

"That is a terrible message."

"Yes," Sera said. "Write who hates it when you arrive."

The runner looked to Lena, perhaps hoping couriers had remained reasonable somewhere.

Lena almost apologized for the profession.

Instead she said, "Add before passing."

The runner muttered something about adults that sounded suspiciously like Ren by way of Halen and tucked the page inside his coat.

He ran back toward the yard.

Sera watched him go until the rain took him into its general opinion of distance.

Then she lifted the bread heel the flour-armed woman had given her and broke it in two. She handed half to Lena.

It was stale, dense, and better than most doctrines.

They ate while walking.

Behind them, someone in Halen was asking again for the word from the Tarn.

Ahead of them, the road had not yet answered.

For once, that did not stop anyone from carrying the question.
