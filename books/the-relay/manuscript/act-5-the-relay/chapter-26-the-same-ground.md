# Chapter 26 — The Same Ground

The first ground Lena and Arin stood on together was mud.

That seemed unfair, after everything.

She had imagined the Tarn too many ways to trust any of them: a black lake in a bowl of stone, a cold platform beyond a dead gate, a door she had failed to take when taking doors had still seemed ordinary. In the longest nights of the slow way she had imagined it as accusation, as home, as weather, as proof that next time had been a lie all along. She had imagined reaching it with her hand still closed around a packet of peppermint drops gone soft in their paper, arriving breathless and too late, or arriving to find nothing, or arriving to find Arin standing exactly where she had left him years ago, unchanged enough to make every mile she had taken feel like betrayal.

The world, having developed a taste for pettiness, gave her mud instead.

It took half a day to reach the lower Tarn road from the crossing that had held badly enough to risk them. Not Vesper. Vesper still refused all romance. They came through a little service arch below Oss that had no canopy, no proper waiting room, and a warning board so crowded with corrections that the original instructions had become an archaeological question. The arch had held three light crossings since morning, none consecutive. It would carry people only after the south sluice answered, before the third heat pulse, with no heavy cargo, no carts, and no one stupid enough to stop inside the stone to admire the fact that it worked.

Pellan's milk cans went first.

This had been her condition, delivered through three copies and one insult, and no one had found a better one. The empty cans crossed, came back with condensation on them from air that smelled different, and the keeper at Oss wrote: far side present, damp, cold, unwelcoming. Acceptable.

Then a lock hand crossed with no luggage and returned looking offended by survival.

Then Arin went.

He did not look at Lena before stepping through, which was either courage or cruelty and probably neither. He was not going home. That was the word everyone wanted and the word he had refused so often that eventually the room had stopped offering it. The Tarn was not the place he belonged instead of everywhere else. It was the place that still knew things no other place knew, and it had to be asked again in person because paper had begun to arrive faster than understanding.

Still, when he crossed, Lena felt the old world tear slightly under her ribs.

For one breath he was in the arch. For one breath the far platform thinned around him until he looked like a badly remembered man standing inside weather. Then the hum caught. The shape of him steadied on the far side.

He turned back.

Not rescued. Not lost.

There.

"Next," the Oss keeper said.

Lena stepped through before anyone in the room could make the crossing mean something large enough to damage it.

It did not feel like anything.

That was the old insult of the gates, and it survived even here, under every condition and caveat and wet page the world had written around it. No thunder. No falling. No celestial witness to the fact that a road had once again pretended not to be distance. One step on worn stone beneath Oss; one step on rougher stone that had taken the weather of the Tarn; cold air against her face; the smell of peat smoke, wet iron, old water, and reeds.

Arin stood three paces away, looking past her.

For a moment she thought he was avoiding her face. Then she saw what he was seeing: the gatekeeper's hand on the arch, not triumphant, not relieved, just feeling the stone the way a person feels a pulse under a fevered wrist. Behind the keeper, beyond the little gate shed, the Tarn road fell away toward the lower locks, grey under a low sky. Water glimmered between reed beds in wrong places. A bell-buoy sounded once, thin and off its rhythm.

Lena had come all this way to reach the Tarn, and the Tarn did not rise to meet her.

It lay there, cold and stubborn and busy being itself.

"Welcome," Arin said.

She turned to him.

"You sound like you hate saying that."

"I hate saying most things before I know what they mean."

"So you have remained consistent."

His mouth almost moved.

The gate keeper cleared her throat. "You two going to stand in the entry like officials, or should we send someone around you?"

Arin stepped aside at once.

Lena did too, which felt like progress, though not enough to write down.

They took the lower road because the upper path had drowned two planks and was refusing apology. The road was not a road the way Lena thought of one. It was an agreement between stones, mud, old boards, and people willing to remember where last season's edge had been. Arin walked it without looking down often enough to make her furious. Lena watched every foot placement and still slid twice before the first bend.

The second time Arin caught her elbow.

She stiffened before she meant to.

He let go before she needed to ask.

"Left of the black reed," he said.

"There are six black reeds."

"Then not the shiny one."

"I hate this place."

"You have been here nine minutes."

"I'm quick."

"That was always the trouble."

The sentence should have cut. It did, but less cleanly than it once would have. There was mud under her boot, his hand still near but not touching, a gate behind them that had carried her only because a dairy woman measured time by milk and a road hand wrote load matters on the outside of his shirt. There was no clean place left for old accusations to land.

Still, they landed.

The lower lock appeared through fog the way memory appears when it has been waiting without forgiveness: low stone walls slick with moss, iron gates stained dark from generations of water, a narrow keeper's house with smoke slipping from its chimney, and the lake beyond it, black and flat and too large for the sky it held. Tourists had called the southern mirrors beautiful. Lena could see why, which made her trust them less. The water gave back the clouds so perfectly it seemed to be withholding its own face.

Bel was waiting on the lock wall with her sleeves rolled above the wrist and a slate tucked under one arm. Mala stood below, one boot in the water, jabbing a reed pole into the current with the expression of a woman personally disappointed in physics. Ren sat on an upturned crate near the keeper's door, copying something with violent concentration. He looked up when he saw Arin and then Lena, and his face rearranged itself around several questions he had clearly been told not to ask first.

He asked the worst one anyway.

"Did you bring the pepper drops?"

Arin closed his eyes.

Lena stared at the child.

Bel said, "Ren."

"What?" Ren said. "He said she always sent them. I am testing continuity."

"That is not what continuity means," Arin said.

Ren brightened. "Good. Cai will be upset."

Lena began laughing then, which was inconvenient because she was still on uncertain ground and laughing in mud turned out to involve risk. She bent forward with one hand on her knee and laughed until the cold air hurt in her chest. No one joined her exactly. Bel's mouth softened. Mala snorted once into the reed bed. Arin watched her with an expression so careful it made her want to look away.

She did not.

"I did," Lena said.

The words came out rougher than laughter should have left them.

She opened the inner pocket of her bag and took out the packet. It was smaller than she remembered. The paper had softened, dried, softened again, and gone creased at the edges from all the miles where her body had kept it warm. The old tag was still tied around it. *Next time,* in her hand. Beneath it, cramped and smaller: *Next time was too late. I know.*

Arin saw the writing before he took the packet.

His face changed. Not much. The Tarn had made a religion of not wasting visible weather. But the change was there: a closing, then an opening too small for anyone else to name.

Ren leaned forward shamelessly.

Mala said, "Child, develop one decent instinct before winter."

Ren leaned back, but only in body.

Arin held the packet in both hands, the way he had once held the Tarn sheet in Vesper before setting it down. He did not open it.

"You carried these the whole way?"

"Yes."

"All those roads?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

It was nearly the same question Lena had asked him in the gatehouse over the notice he had carried all the way to Vesper. Why. Because it never answered. Because he had not known how to put it down. Because a thing could become too late and still be the only honest object left in your hand.

She could have said any of that. She was tired enough, and perhaps changed enough, not to improve the answer.

"Because I bought them for you," she said.

Arin looked down.

After a moment he untied the tag, opened the paper, and found the drops inside mostly fused into one uneven lump of sugar and peppermint and pocket dust.

Ren's face filled with reverence.

"That," he said, "is a geological event."

Mala said, "Do not eat history."

Arin broke off a small piece and put it in his mouth.

Everyone watched him despite themselves.

He shut his eyes once, which proved something about age and nothing about self-command.

"Still good?" Lena asked.

"No."

He broke off another piece and handed it to her.

She took it.

It tasted of peppermint, paper, old lint, and the strange humiliating mercy of things that had survived badly instead of not surviving at all.

Bel waited until both of them had swallowed before saying, "Good. Now that the holy sugar has been received, the lake is still wrong."

That was the Tarn's version of tenderness, apparently.

They worked because no one knew what else to do with feeling.

Lena learned the lower lock by being put in the way of it. Bel gave her the slate and told her to stand where the east wall dipped. Arin told her not there, two stones down, unless she wanted to record the eddy from the spill crack instead of the main pull. Mala told Arin to stop teaching her like a tourist and let her make one wrong mark so the correction had somewhere to live. Ren, from his crate, wrote: *Courier nearly measured crack. Adults emotional. Reliability: high.*

"Do not write that," Lena said.

"I already did."

"Cross out emotional."

Ren considered this. "No."

Arin did not smile. His ear betrayed him.

The work was simple only in the way all precise work looked simple to people not doing it. They lowered the west sluice half, then a thumb more when the water climbed too fast along the lower wall. Bel timed the change against Dalla's last repeated Ivera pulse. Mala read the reeds. Arin stood with one palm near the iron handle, not touching until the vibration under the wall changed. Lena watched the water, then watched Arin watching the water, then realized she had been watching the wrong thing.

"Stop looking for what he knows," Bel said beside her.

Lena startled.

Bel had not raised her voice. She did not need to. Some people had the gift of making ordinary correction feel like a door opening under your feet.

"I'm not."

"You are. He hates it less than he should because he missed you, but it is still annoying."

Arin, without turning, said, "I hate it an appropriate amount."

Bel ignored him. "Look at the water."

"I am looking at the water."

"No. You are looking at the water as if it is carrying a message away from you. Look at it before it becomes useful."

Lena looked.

The water did not become clearer because someone had told her to see it. It remained exactly what it had been: dark, moving in narrow sheens along the stone, curling where the sluice opened, tugging at reed stems, holding the reflection of a grey sky so perfectly that the moving part hid under the mirrored part. She watched until her eyes gave up trying to make it a sign. Then, irritably, something changed. A faint tremor under the smoothness, not visible so much as betrayed by the smallest shiver of reed shadow.

"There," she said before she was sure.

Arin's hand moved to the handle.

"Wait," Mala said.

He waited.

The tremor came again, then settled.

"Now," Mala said.

Arin adjusted the sluice no more than the width of two fingers.

The water changed. Not dramatically. It would not have impressed a council, a crowd, or any person waiting for a miracle. The reed shadows steadied by a fraction. The lower wall stopped humming under Lena's boot.

Bel made a mark on the slate.

Lena understood suddenly why Arin had stayed, and why she had hated him for it. Not because the Tarn was small. Because it was endless in a way a moving person could not bear. Every inch asked to be known before being summarized. Every change had a neighbor. Every correction insulted something else. The work did not end at departure because departure had never been its measure.

She looked at Arin.

He was watching the water, not her. His face had gone quiet, but not distant. Present. All the way present.

She had mistaken that, for years, for refusal.

Maybe he had mistaken her motion for escape because that had been easier than asking what it cost to leave a place before it finished telling you what was wrong.

By afternoon the fog lifted enough to show the southern mirrors.

Lena and Arin walked the dyke alone because Bel ordered everyone else to develop business elsewhere and because Ren announced that he could supervise from a distance and was immediately given three buckets to carry. The dyke curved along the lower edge of the lake, mud on one side, black water on the other, the old boards slick where the moss had taken them. The sky appeared beneath them as much as above them. Twice Lena stepped around a reflected cloud as if it were a hole, and twice Arin noticed and said nothing, which was the most irritating form of mercy available to him.

At the far bend they stopped where an old marker post leaned over the water.

Lena knew the place from his messages before she knew it from memory: the angle of the mirrors, the stair hidden in reflection, the path tourists followed wrong for twenty minutes while Arin watched and judged them privately. It was not grand. That made it harder. It was only a lake path, a leaning post, a stain of silver film near the reeds where the disturbance had thinned after Ivera's correction. The whole argument of their lives had been waiting here in objects too ordinary to defend themselves.

Arin crouched and touched the mud near the edge.

"Still soft," he said.

"That seems to be its governing principle."

"It remembers weight longer than dry ground."

"Of course it does."

He looked up.

She heard herself then. The old edge in it. The old assumption that when he said something about mud, weather, locks, he was also saying something about her. Maybe sometimes he had been. Maybe sometimes she had made him.

"I'm sorry," she said.

The words came out too quickly to be ceremonial.

Arin stayed crouched, his fingers in the mud.

"For the mud?"

"For thinking staying meant you hadn't chosen."

He did not move for a long time.

The lake gave back the sky. A bird called once from the reed bed and received no answer Lena could hear.

"I thought leaving meant you chose lightly," he said.

"Yes."

"I was wrong."

"Yes."

His mouth twitched despite everything.

She let him have it because he had said the first clean thing.

"Not entirely," she added.

"There it is."

"I did like the dry stone."

"I know."

"And the houses with two floors."

"I was aware."

"And the part where my coat did not smell of peat smoke."

"That was vanity, not philosophy."

"It can be both."

He stood, wiping mud on the side of his trousers in a motion so old she could have found it blind. "I thought if I stayed, I was carrying the real weight."

"You were carrying a real weight."

"I know." He looked out at the water. "That was the part that made me stupid."

Lena leaned against the marker post. It shifted under her hand, rotten at the base, and Arin reached to steady it before he reached to steady her. She noticed. He noticed her noticing. Neither of them used it.

"I thought the road made you less answerable," he said. "Because you could leave before the consequences arrived."

"Sometimes it did."

"Yes."

"And sometimes leaving meant I carried consequences for places that never knew my name."

"Yes."

He said it carefully, not as concession but as correction already written somewhere and now read aloud.

Lena looked down at the mud on her boots. His mud. Her boots. Neither phrase held.

"You took the easy road," he said.

She turned toward him sharply before she could stop herself.

He did not flinch. "I said that because I needed your road to be easy. Mine hurt less if yours did not."

The sentence opened in her chest and found old rooms.

She thought of the first morning at Vesper, the candy cart, the woman afraid of the arch, the gate becoming a wall. The Osprey's cold gallery. Harl's chalk line. Sennen's feverish arch. Anneth's tide-stained trestle. The Halen girl looking through chain-link. Varik with a bowl in his hand. Sera's road. Jun's charts. Cai's notebooks. Nadi's ledger. The manifest under Lio's torn sleeve. Every road she had taken that had not absolved her of anything.

"I needed you to be hiding," she said. "Because if staying was work, then leaving was not automatically courage."

Arin looked at her, and for the first time since she had stepped through the arch, he looked tired in a way that belonged only to them.

"Were you brave?" he asked.

"No."

He nodded.

She waited.

"Were you?" she asked.

"No."

The lake went on being beautiful in a way that helped no one.

Lena laughed once, softly.

"Good," Arin said.

"That word is becoming a problem."

"Ren will regulate it."

"Ren should regulate nothing."

"He has already started a column."

"Of course he has."

They stood beside the leaning post, two grown people with mud on their boots and peppermint in their teeth, failing to become reconciled in any way a song would recognize.

After a while Arin took the old candy tag from his pocket. He had kept it. She had not seen him do that.

*Next time.*

*Next time was too late. I know.*

The paper had gone soft at the fold. He turned it over. The back was blank.

"I hated this," he said.

"The candy?"

"The tag."

"I know."

"You sent next time like it cost nothing."

"It cost less than coming."

"Yes."

She could have defended herself. There had always been reasons: routes, money, work, weather, the convenience of a road that never required them to decide. She had used all of them privately. None had aged well enough to bring here.

"I'm sorry," she said again.

He folded the tag along its old crease. "I answered you with weather."

"You always answered with weather."

"I know. It let me say nothing while feeling honest."

"That is a very Tarn crime."

"Yes."

He gave the tag back to her.

She did not take it.

"No," she said.

His hand stayed between them.

"I carried it here. You carry it next."

The old shape of the job rose in her as she said it. Receive. Carry. Pass. For once it did not feel like escape. It felt like an exchange. Not the surrender of a burden to someone cleaner, not a heroic transfer at the edge of a gate, only a small damaged paper moving from one hand to another because neither hand could make it whole alone.

Arin looked at the tag.

Then he put it in his pocket.

"All right," he said.

They walked back when Bel rang the lower bell twice, which meant either needed immediately or everyone is being foolish near water. In practice these appeared to be the same category.

A page had arrived while they were gone, carried not by drum but by a boy from the road with one shoe and enormous resentment. It was from Low Cairn, copied on feed tally again, with Pellan's hand recognizable by its refusal to flatter paper.

*Low Cairn held six light crossings after rain.*

*Seventh crossed too heavy despite warning. Arch thinned, recovered after stop. Person responsible says they thought warning meant carts. Warning now reads: no heavy means no heavy person carrying two milk cans and pride.*

*Question for Tarn: south water rose after rain before Ivera heat. Is lake ahead of throat or road behind report?*

Ren had already written beneath it: *Question rude but useful.*

Bel looked at Arin. "Well?"

Arin read the page, then looked at the lake.

Lena felt the room — not a room now, but lock wall, mud, reeds, people, water — begin to do the thing Vesper had learned to do badly. It looked less impressive outdoors. There was no crate at the center, no stove, no old notice, no gathered cast arranged by pressure and need. There was a lock wall, a slate, a page flapping in the wind, Ren holding it down with both hands while pretending this did not make him important.

No single person answered.

Bel went to the measure posts. Mala went back into the water, swearing at the reeds before they could disappoint her. Arin crossed to the lower handle. Ren cleared a space on the slate. The road boy sat on the crate and began removing mud from his one shoe with the grave concentration of someone who had earned the right to be useless for three minutes.

Lena stood where she was.

"Courier," Bel said without turning.

The word hit differently here. Not smaller. Not less. Different.

Lena stepped forward.

"What do you need?"

"Not need. See." Bel pointed with her chin to the path above the lower bend. "Road report says rain reached Low Cairn before the water rose here. But the road boy came by upper marsh. Ask him where the marsh was already full."

"I can do that."

"Yes," Bel said. "That is why I said it."

Lena went to the boy.

He did not want to answer at first because eating cold oatbread was apparently a sacred duty, but he did answer, and the answer mattered. The upper marsh had been full before the rain reached Low Cairn. The water had moved under reed and road, below the visible weather, and the road had reported rain because rain was the part road people saw.

Lena brought that back.

Arin listened, then looked at Mala. Mala jabbed the reed pole once, hard, and said, "Underrun."

Bel wrote it.

Ren wrote it too, misspelled, crossed it out, glared at the word as if it had failed him morally, and wrote it again.

The answer they sent was not elegant.

*Tarn south water rose before visible rain. Upper marsh full before Low Cairn report. Cause likely underrun, not rain only. Watch reed bend before road wet. If Ivera heat changes without rain, answer. If Low Cairn sends heavy pride again, reduce crossings and name pride as load.*

Ren added: *Pride weighs more when denied.*

Bel crossed out *more* and wrote *differently.*

Ren looked betrayed but copied the correction.

The road boy took the page back after he finished his bread. Lena expected Arin to hand it to him. Arin expected it too; she saw it in the way his hand moved first. Then he stopped and looked at her.

Not asking permission.

Asking whether she saw the shape.

Lena took the page from Bel, read it once more, then handed it to the boy.

"Carry it dry if you can," she said. "Wet if you can't. Add where the road lies."

The boy squinted at her. "Roads don't lie."

"They do when people ask them the wrong question."

He considered this, decided it was irritating enough to remember, and tucked the page inside his coat.

Arin watched him go.

"You stayed," he said after the boy disappeared beyond the reed bend.

"For one question."

"Yes."

"You passed."

"For one answer."

"Yes."

Neither of them made more of it, which allowed it to remain true.

Evening came cold. The lake lost the sky by degrees until the mirrors became water again and then something darker than water. Bel lit the lower lamps. Mala hauled herself out of the reed bed with both boots full and announced that anyone who admired the lake after dark should be required to drain their own socks. Ren fell asleep on his crate with the slate against his chest and had to be relieved of the pen before he wrote on himself in dreams.

Lena and Arin sat on the lock wall while the last light went.

Not close enough to make a scene. Close enough that their shoulders would have touched if either of them leaned in a direction neither chose.

"The gate will not hold long," Arin said.

"No."

"You may have to go back the long way."

"I know."

He looked at her then. "You still mean to leave tomorrow."

"Yes."

The word hurt. It also fit.

There had been a time when she would have made leaving sound like necessity, because necessity asked less of the person left behind. There had been a time when he would have made staying sound like judgment, because judgment hurt less than asking someone to return. The lake gave them no cover now. The road had taught her too much. Vesper had taught him too much. The first pages had come back and ruined both of them for their old defenses.

"I need to carry the Low Cairn pages to Vesper," she said. "Jun needs the pattern before someone improves it into nonsense. Sera needs the Merrow names before saved starts lying again. Nadi will pretend not to need the road addition and then use it before supper. Cai will hate the milk timing better if I describe Pellan's face."

"That is a good reason to go."

"Yes."

"I hate it."

"Yes."

He looked back at the black water.

"I need to stay until the south water answers twice more," he said. "Bel can read it, Mala can insult it, Ren can terrorize the spelling, but I saw the Ivera shift at Vesper and here. I need both in my hands before I leave again."

"That is a good reason to stay."

"Yes."

"I hate it."

His mouth moved. "Yes."

There it was, then. Not rescue. Not return. Not one sibling arriving at last to absolve the other of the road they had taken. The same ground under them, and the ground still choosing mud. The old opposition did not vanish. It changed weight. Move and stay were no longer arguments pretending to be identities. They were tasks, each incomplete without the other, each capable of becoming cowardice when mistaken for virtue.

Lena leaned back on her hands and felt the cold stone through her palms.

"I thought when I got here," she said, "something would end."

Arin breathed out through his nose. "That was optimistic."

"It was private. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"No."

The answer surprised a laugh out of him.

It was not much of a laugh. It was rusty, suspicious of itself, and brief. It was also the first sound he had made all day that belonged only to him and not to the work.

Lena looked away, granting him the privacy of it.

After a moment he reached into his pocket and took out the peppermint lump. He broke it with difficulty against the lock wall. One piece skittered away into the dark.

Ren, asleep, said, "I saw that," without opening his eyes.

"No you didn't," Arin said.

"Reliability medium," Ren murmured.

Lena pressed both hands over her face.

Arin handed her a piece. This one had more lint than the first.

She ate it anyway.

They sat there until the lamps came on fully and the lake stopped pretending to be sky. Behind them, Bel moved through the lock house, setting the night slate. Mala cursed softly while emptying her boots. Somewhere down the road, a boy with one shoe carried a page toward Low Cairn, and beyond him other pages would move badly through damp coats and road shelters and half-held arches, gathering answers no one would own for long.

No one knew whether the practice would survive the crisis that made it necessary. That uncertainty sat with them too. It sat on the lock wall, in the cold, between the siblings and behind them and under their boots. Lena found she did not need to push it away.

The work had not brought her brother back.

Her brother had not stayed unchanged for her to find.

She had not come to the Tarn to rescue him, and he had not come to Vesper to forgive her. They had moved toward each other through a world that kept refusing clean arrivals, each carrying half of a weight they had spent years misnaming as the other's escape.

Now they sat on the same stone, eating ruined candy in the dark, while the water below them answered one hand differently than another and both answers mattered.

After a long while Arin said, "Next time?"

Lena looked at him.

He was not smiling. Not exactly.

The old words should have been impossible now. They had been too late once. They could become too late again. Neither of them was innocent enough to use them as a promise.

But a phrase could be corrected without being destroyed.

Lena took the last scrap of candy paper from his hand, turned it over, and wrote on the blank back with Bel's stub of pencil.

*Next time: answer if changed.*

Arin read it.

Then he folded the paper and put it back in his pocket.

"Better," he said.

The lower bell rang once in the dark.

Not warning. Not safety.

Only the watch changing hands.

Lena stayed until it did.
