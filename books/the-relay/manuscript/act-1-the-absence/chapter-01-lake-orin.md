# Chapter 01 — Lake Orin

Tourists stood at the shoreline of Lake Orin turning in circles, trying to decide which city was real.

Across the water, Meridian Arc hung overhead. On clear mornings the same apartment towers showed in the sky above and in the lake below, mirrored so cleanly that newcomers sometimes lost track of which way was up.

Locals glanced once, found their bearings, and kept walking.

Lena had learned to read the place the way other people read weather. People called it the Ribbon—the linked districts and transit rings where Northhook sat beside Lake Orin and Meridian Arc hung overhead, one city's towers reflected in another's water.

She was not quite a local. She had a berth at Northhook Transit for another six days, a manifest half-checked, and the faded compass rose on her shoulder that marked her as courier. At dawn her manifest slate lit in her palm—three repriced corridor premiums for route insurance—and she'd tapped through without reading. Couriers learned that after the first year: the Ribbon pushed too many fees, feeds, and revisions to read every one, and you flew on what you could verify, not what pinged loudest. Most mornings she walked down to Lake Orin before checking messages. Today she stood on this particular railing when she needed to think without being asked what she was thinking about.

The weather board over Meridian Arc had already shifted blue; atmospheric pumps would drag a storm through by evening. East of the lake, silver maintenance kites moved between the mirror faces in slow spirals—wing surfaces catching light that wasn't quite sunlight. One folded into a dart and vanished into a maintenance shaft. Children pointed. Adults didn't.

A school group walked the lower path, slates in hand. Their teacher pointed at a lit balcony overhead—then at the same balcony inverted in the lake.

"Overhead," she called. "Not the lake."

The children tapped their slates without breaking stride—the real tower, the block they'd pass on the walk home, not the upside-down copy in the water.

One girl hung back. "Is Meridian Arc real or a trick?"

"Both show up." The teacher kept moving. "Only one has stairs. That's why we practice on foot."

A tourist on the lower stair bent double and vomited into a drainage grate. His partner apologized to nobody in particular. The teacher didn't pause the exercise—Northhook's version of orientation day.

Above the kites, two figures waved from a Meridian Arc balcony. Someone waved back a second later.

Lena had tried that once with her brother Arin, when he'd visited on leave. They'd spent twenty minutes arguing about which of them was upside down.

Her courier bag hung against her hip, heavier than it needed to be—a private packet for a mirror-tech firm that paid well and asked few questions.

A message from Arin sat in her queue, six days old, marked read but not answered.

Six days without a reply wasn't unusual for Arin. Six days with Vesper closed was.

*Storms over the southern mirrors again. You'd laugh if you saw the tourists trying to navigate by reflection.*

Arin lived on Orinreach Basin, a week's freight south of the southern mirrors. Normally she reached him through Vesper Gate—the corridor run that turned that week into a shuttle day.

His messages were about storms, mirror work, tourists in trouble. Hers were route changes, freight delays, berths she'd slept in and left.

She'd meant to answer from the Ribbon, where relay capacity was decent and berths lasted long enough to pretend you had time.

Messages could wait until she reached the dock.

She walked back up from the railing through the lower path, past the school group now counting stairs aloud, past a vendor selling reflection cards to tourists who'd forget them by afternoon. Northhook's transit ring sat above the lake level—courier berths and freight docks on the curved upper deck while people walked beneath.

---

At Northhook Transit the manifest argument was already waiting.

The argument had started over three kilos.

Not much—nothing that should have mattered on a habitat where scales and manifests argued with each other half the time—but the client's manifest had been wrong, the scan had been right, and the clerk had treated the difference as Lena's problem because couriers were easier to blame than the ledger.

"You weighed it wet," the clerk had said.

"It's sealed."

"Seals absorb."

She'd pulled up the prior scan from the junction before Northhook, the dated lines, the witness line. Her voice had stayed level because level was cheaper than rage. The clerk had sighed the way clerks sighed when procedure required them to pretend to listen.

"Prior scan's from Meridian junction," she said. "Same seal, same mass. Your scale's the one that's drunk."

"My scale is calibrated."

"Then calibrate it again while I stand here."

Behind the clerk, a freight crawler reversed with a warning tone that made everyone in line step sideways without looking up. Northhook had learned that sound the way other cities learned sirens. Lena kept her eyes on the clerk's screen.

"You want a supervisor?" he asked.

"Stop billing me for your scale."

He tapped something. "Supervisor's on the transit corridor. Could be twenty minutes."

"I've got twenty minutes."

The woman in line behind her had leaned in. "Is that about Vesper?"

"Three kilos," Lena said.

"Everything's about Vesper today." The woman had a child's hand in hers and a manifest case at her feet. "My cousin says separatists. My husband says maintenance. I don't know who's right. I know the fruit rots."

"Meridian's still open."

"Meridian's always open." The woman said it like a complaint and a prayer. "It's Vesper that eats the week."

Then the bulletin had arrived.

**VESPER GATE — TRANSIT SUSPENDED UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE.**

No official word why. No estimated reopening. No alternate route listed.

In seven years of courier work, Lena had seen gates slow, gates queue, gates reroute around maintenance that everyone pretended wasn't maintenance. She had seen a gate hiccup once on the far side of the Markets and watched three administrations issue explanations within the hour, each more confident than the last, two of them contradicting the third.

This time there was only silence.

The clerk had shrugged when she showed him the notice. "Happens."

"Not like this."

"Bullshit." The clerk didn't look up from his screen. "Happens all the time. You'll live."

Lena looked at the number the system had assigned her for the supervisor call. Forty-seven. She didn't sit down. She stood at the counter with her slate open and her jaw set in the expression that made other couriers give her room.

When the supervisor finally came, she was a tired woman with a guild pin and a transit-corridor smudge on one sleeve. She didn't apologize. She looked at Lena's scan history first.

"You're not wrong," she said.

"Good."

"You're also not getting an apology. The client's manifest is what the client filed. We note the discrepancy. We don't eat it."

Lena slid the packet across. "Three kilos isn't a discrepancy. It's a clerk who wanted lunch."

The supervisor's mouth twitched—not quite a smile. "Sign here. Margin note. Next time use the witness line before you argue in front of forty people."

"I did use the witness line."

"Then use it louder." She stamped the slate. "Vesper's going to make everyone stupid for a week. Don't be stupid on my dock."

She walked back toward the transit corridor before Lena could answer. The clerk finally looked up.

"Happens," he said, like they'd never fought.

Around them, Northhook Transit continued its ordinary noise: freight crawlers reversing along the ring above, announcement chimes, a tea cart locking onto the magnetic rail that ran beneath the lake level—same corner every suspension, same crawler, different fear on the wall.

People were annoyed. Shipments delayed. Travelers grumbled in the polite way travelers grumbled when inconvenience was shared widely enough to feel impersonal.

Lena did not find it impersonal.

She found a bench near the tea cart and pulled up route maps she didn't need, because looking busy was easier than sitting still.

Vesper Gate was not the only path to Orinreach Basin, but it was the path Lena had used often enough that her muscles knew the corridor sequence—the brief pressure behind the eyes, the wrong-light flicker, the smell like hot metal and clean rain. Couriers called it the honest hangover: you felt it even when the corridor was closed.

She could go around. There was always around.

Around took eleven days and touched two jurisdictions that charged transit fees according to moods Lena had never learned to predict.

She pulled up the long-route calculator on her slate anyway—Meridian surcharge provisional, border fees ugly, Drift leg first if she wanted pilots who'd already flown a suspension week on the long route. The numbers shifted when she refreshed. They always shifted when the wall got loud.

Eleven days was not forever.

Eleven days was long enough for storms over southern mirrors to become something other than a joke.

The tea vendor passed with his cart, steam rising. She flagged him for plain tea. He handed her half-spiced anyway and moved on without comment—Northhook mercy, or habit.

She started a reply to Arin and deleted it. *Did you hear?* was too small. *I'm coming the long way* was a promise she couldn't keep until she knew what the suspension meant.

A child ran past her bench chasing a reflection in a puddle—the upside-down geography lesson every Ribbon school drilled early. The district overhead was Meridian Arc; the puddle held a piece of it; the child stamped the piece and laughed.

Lena watched the puddle flatten.

Arin had sent her a picture once—tourists wedged between mirror guides, faces tight with concentration, while locals walked past without breaking stride. She'd looked at it on a shuttle the same day she flew out. He stayed. She left. That was the bargain they'd made years ago without naming it.

---

An older woman with a festival sash—paired celebrations with Meridian Arc, from the twin colors—sat at the other end of the bench and peeled an orange without looking up.

"You waiting long?" the woman asked.

"Long enough."

"My nephew's freight is stuck because of Vesper." The woman said it the way you mentioned rain. "He'll shout about sabotage tonight. He shouts about something every night."

Lena almost smiled. "What's tonight's theory?"

"Separatists. Always separatists when the notice is clean." The woman handed her a segment of orange. "Last week it was mirror drift. Week before, bad fruit tariffs. He'll eat dinner angry and sleep fine. That's what nephews do."

Lena took it. "No reason on the bulletin?"

The woman shook her head. "Either somebody knows and isn't talking, or nobody knows."

"Which do you think?"

"I think dinner's in an hour and my nephew's still going to complain about separatists." The woman studied the peel in her lap. "My sister married a keeper. She says the corridor check came back clean. Says the bulletin left out the inspection part. She won't post that—the guild would eat her lunch." She handed Lena another piece of orange. "Eat. Fruit rots faster when gates close. People don't."

Lena chewed. The juice was sharp. "You're not a stranger."

"No." The woman stood, brushing peel into a pocket pouch—Northhook habit, nothing wasted. "You're the courier who argued about three kilos. I heard you from the tea cart." She nodded toward the transit ring. "Check your insurance before you reserve around. Vesper fear surcharges hit at noon."

She left. Lena sat with the last segment of orange and the thread wall brightening in the corner of her eye.

---

People stood around the thread wall pretending to wait for departures. Nobody was waiting. The wall wasn't schedules—it was guesswork. Travelers refreshed competing stories about why Vesper had closed, and the numbers beside each headline showed how many people were betting on that version.

Lena's slate showed **SEPARATIST SABOTAGE — forty-one percent** when she glanced at the wall. The woman beside her showed thirty-eight on the same screen. The security man showed fifty-two. Same story, different feeds.

A man in transit security livery read the sabotage thread aloud to his partner. "—deliberate corridor collapse on the separatist schedule, my cousin on Drift says freight—"

His partner snorted. "Your cousin on Drift says everything."

A woman in keeper grays passed without slowing. Two students argued over a sabotage thread as if it were an exam question.

**VESPER GATE — SUSPENDED** sat at the top in administrative white.

**SEPARATIST SABOTAGE** — thirty-eight percent and climbing on the public aggregate. Lena's feed had already nudged hers higher.

**ADMINISTRATIVE COVER-UP** — twenty-four percent.

**ROUTINE MAINTENANCE** — eleven percent, shrinking.

Nobody was liking the keeper post.

A student in transit livery jabbed a finger at the maintenance slice. "That's wrong. My uncle's a keeper. They checked the corridor yesterday."

"Then your uncle should post on the wall," his friend said.

"He did. Nobody liked it."

"Because it's boring," the first student said, and Lena felt something cold settle in her chest.

The keeper in grays had stopped at the wall's edge. She pulled a slate from her belt, entered something with two fingers, and waited while the post slot cycled.

**ROUTINE MAINTENANCE — CORRIDOR CHECK COMPLETE. TOLERANCE WITHIN SPEC.**

The sabotage slice didn't move. The cover-up slice twitched up a fraction anyway.

"Post it again," the transit security man said. "Plain language."

"I posted the inspection report."

"Nobody's reading inspection reports."

"Yeah," the keeper said. "I noticed."

"Then maybe explain what happened."

"I don't know what happened. I know the corridor passed inspection."

"That's not what people are asking."

"If I make it exciting, couriers start routing through junction lips that aren't cleared." She looked at the security man—not hostile, tired in the way of someone who'd had this argument before breakfast. "I had that argument last suspension."

Her partner muttered, "Nobody reads the log anyway."

The keeper looked at Lena then, brief as a scan. "Courier?"

Lena nodded.

"If you're routing around, find who had the watch at the junction lamps. Last handoff. Not the wall."

"The lamp's out at Northhook," Lena said.

"Then ask who was supposed to take it." She started walking. "Percentages won't tell you that." The students went back to their exam. The security man read the sabotage thread again, louder, as if volume could vote the keeper post down.

Lena moved on.

The tea vendor in the corner had watched her stare without pretending not to. His cart was tethered to the magnetic rail—locked in for the suspension week, same as always. He was old enough to have seen suspensions before, young enough to still sell cups like weather was the main event.

"Spiced or plain?" he asked when she drifted back.

"Plain."

"You look like spiced."

"I look like someone who doesn't want sugar right now."

He handed her the cup anyway—half spiced, mercy disguised as mistake. "My brother runs freight through Vesper," he said. "He's not talking to me today."

Lena wrapped her hands around the heat. "Scared or busy?"

"Yes." He wiped the counter. "Tourists buy reflection cards and throw up by the lower stair. You're still standing. That tells me enough. You waiting on someone?"

She thought about lying. "Yes."

"Then get off the wall before noon." He nodded at her slate. "Insurance surcharges refresh when fear crosses forty. You look like you're about to find out."

---

She climbed back to the upper walk at Lake Orin—the view from above, where tourists tried the lake-crossing drill: using the reflection to find stairs that only existed overhead, and failing in public.

She found the pilgrim on the low wall with her boots on the stone, watching a man in a bright jacket miss the stair twice. The pilgrim laughed quietly, not unkindly. She wore road-soft layers and the bone beads people carried on the Pilgrim Road. You usually didn't ask what they meant. One bead string had come loose; she kept tucking it back with the same irritated motion, like the wind up here was personally rude.

Everyone else on the upper walk had their displays out.

Lena stopped beside her. "You're not checking the bulletin."

"I'm watching him learn the lake."

"He's going to bruise his knee."

"Probably." The pilgrim shifted on the stone. "My knees hurt too. Lower-station stairs."

Lena waited a beat. "Vesper Gate is suspended."

"I heard."

"You don't—" Lena heard herself and slowed down. "You don't seem worried."

The pilgrim turned at last. Middle-aged. Tired in a practical way—not calm, just scheduled.

"Should I be?" She said it like she meant it. "You're worried about the gate."

"My brother's on the other side of it."

"Then go around or don't." She dug in her bag for a hard biscuit, broke it in half, held one piece out. Lena shook her head. The pilgrim ate her half without ceremony. "I'm not here for Vesper. Lower relay station. Runner died carrying the watch lamp Tuesday. I knew him—not well. Well enough to read his name right."

"Shift overlap," Lena said. She'd seen the phrase on a thread slice that morning.

"On the thread. Before the family even—" The pilgrim stopped herself. "Administration loves a verb that fits a chart."

"That's it?"

"That's the job." Crumbs on her sleeve. "Meeting's at four. Community wants the name once, correctly." She stood, brushing crumbs. "My son's picking me up at the relay ring after. If I'm late he'll talk about it for a week."

"What if they want a reason for the gate?"

"Then they can hire somebody." Already moving. "Analyst. Bureau. I don't— I've got a name to rehearse."

"If you need directions to the lower station—"

"I don't need directions." Already moving. "I need my mouth empty for twenty minutes."

She was counting stairs on the way down, lips moving.

Lena watched the tourist find the stair. He raised his arms. The reflection in the water below raised arms too.

---

She went back to Northhook at dusk because work was work even when the world narrowed.

Her berth still had six days. Her private packet still needed a signature scan she hadn't collected because the firm's representative had "stepped out" in the way firms stepped out when news was fresh and everyone pretended not to hurry.

Lena found the firm kiosk anyway.

The representative turned out to be a young woman with mirror-tech logos on both sleeves and the hunted look of someone whose employers cared about reflection more than packets. The kiosk screen behind her cycled a soft advertisement—reflection calibration, family packages, *see both cities honestly*—and cut to a blank panel whenever someone in the corridor said *Vesper* loud enough to kill the ad.

"We're not issuing statements," she said before Lena could speak.

"I'm not asking for a statement. I'm asking for your signature."

"The gate situation is fluid."

"My manifest isn't." Lena set the packet on the counter. "Sign."

The woman glanced at the blank panel, then at the thread wall visible through the kiosk glass. Sabotage had climbed again since lunch.

"Our routing desk pulled a packet yesterday," she said. "Internal. Vesper contingency. Then legal killed the request. I don't know what's in it. I know my manager stopped making eye contact."

"That's your firm's problem."

"If a mirror reads open when it isn't, my firm bills someone." She signed. Her hand shook slightly—not fear, caffeine. "I'd rather it not be you. You're not rerouting because of Vesper?"

"I'm holding until I know what holding means."

"Smart." She didn't sound sure. "Our firm doesn't take positions on separatist narratives."

"Good." Lena collected the scan. "Neither do I. I take positions on signatures."

The woman hesitated, then slid a thin card across the counter—not company issue, just a hand number. "If you end up on the long corridor, there's a keeper check on Meridian Arc side that fails if you rush. My cousin didn't post it because it sounds like maintenance."

Lena looked at the card, then at the woman. "Why give me that?"

"Because you're the kind of courier who notices when a junction lamp's out instead of asking for statements." She killed the kiosk ad entirely. "And because if you rush and break a mirror, my firm bills someone, and I'd rather it not be you."

---

At the transit desk she ran into Dael, a courier she knew from overlapping routes—older, faster mouth, compass patch newer than hers.

"Vesper?" he said, by way of hello.

"Vesper."

"You holding at Northhook or running?"

"Holding." She tapped her manifest slate. "Maybe rerouting. You?"

"Waiting to see if the story picks an ending." He jerked his chin toward the thread wall, still bright in the evening foot traffic. "Separatists are winning the hour. Markets already moved fees on the long corridor—somebody always knows before the bulletin catches up."

"I noticed junction language in the threads."

Dael looked at her sideways. "You would." He paused, then added, less joking: "My sister's on Orinreach Basin. She's fine. Annoyed. Says the local boards look like the gate's just resting."

"Resting."

"If you go long way, check the Meridian fee schedule—they changed it twice yesterday." Dael's mouth twisted. "Administrations get creative when they smell confusion. Same as always. Same bullshit, new fees."

"You rerouting?"

"Not yet." He tapped his slate. "I've got cargo that can wait. You've got a brother who can't."

"I don't know that."

"You know enough to hold." Dael's voice lost its joke edge. "That's the problem. Holding looks smart until it doesn't."

He shifted his bag, then lowered his voice. "Listen. There's a clip going around—corridor sequence through the Markets, separatist timing, the whole package. It's wrong. I flew that sequence month before last. Border's edited. If you see it, file it wrong. Don't argue with it in the thread. People pile on."

"I don't argue in public."

"You stare. Same thing." He almost smiled. "Wall eats it either way." "If you get through, tell Arin he still owes me for that bottle on Orinreach."

"He owes everybody for that bottle."

"Then tell him I'm first in line." He left. Lena stood at the desk a moment longer—fees, corridors, stories, the lamp she'd seen dark on the walk in.

A clerk on the next terminal called the long-route surcharge out loud for another courier—numbers that made the courier swear softly and pay anyway.

She filed the weights. She answered two routine queries from clients whose cargo was not going to Orinreach Basin.

On the walk to her berth she passed the junction where Northhook's running light should have been burning—courier handoff point, every shift someone supposed to take the watch lamp from the outgoing runner and carry it to the next lip.

The lamp was out.

Not dock-dark. The specific dark of a duty abandoned mid-shift—the lamp cold, the filament dead, nobody on the rail willing to say the handoff had failed.

A transit worker in transit-corridor orange was posting a staffing stub on the wall beside it, the boring kind clerks ignored.

"You see that?" he said when he noticed her staring.

"I see it."

"Supervisor says it'll relight when second shift clocks in." He didn't sound convinced. "Gate's not my gate. I still walked the corridor."

"You post that anywhere?"

He snorted. "On the wall? Nobody likes boring." He started walking again, then paused. "Tell your guild the lamp's out if you want. Just don't put my name on it. I like my job."

She didn't photograph it. She pocketed the hand-number card from the mirror-tech kiosk without deciding yet whether she'd use it.

---

At the berth hatch she finally wrote to Arin.

*I saw your message. I'm still on the Ribbon—six days on the berth. Vesper Gate suspended, no official word why. Are you all right?*

She stared at the words, then added:

*I'll find a way around if I need to. Don't do anything stupid in the mirrors.*

She sent it.

The relay accepted it. The queue showed delivered to Orinreach Basin.

No immediate reply. Arin answered when his hands were dry and his shift was done.

No reply by morning. That could mean storm window. It could mean ladder until midnight. It could mean nothing. Lena had learned not to list the other possibilities before coffee.

The berth was small—a bunk, a fold-down desk, a porthole.

She lay in her berth and listened to freight crawlers move along the ring overhead—the ordinary vibration of a habitat that pretended distance was solved.

Meridian Arc bloomed in the dark water below her porthole. The same lights climbed the curve above.

She opened her route ledger and blocked out the long way to Orinreach Basin—eleven days, two fees, one corridor she trusted—and labeled the block **reserved**, not **committed**.

Not yet.

She closed her eyes.

She slept badly and woke early.

The suspension notice was still there. Still no official word why. Sabotage up on the thread wall. Maintenance down.

She would start asking questions she didn't want to ask.
