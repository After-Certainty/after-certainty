# Chapter 02 — Competing Stories

By the second morning, fewer people stopped at the suspension screens.

She had five days left on her berth and a reserved block in her route ledger. Eleven days around. Two fees. One corridor she trusted.

Northhook Transit still smelled like recycled air and someone's breakfast. Announcement chimes still sounded cheerful. Freight crawlers hung silent from the ring overhead while ground traffic threaded beneath.

Arin hadn't answered.

The queue still showed delivered.

She ate breakfast from a cart locked to the magnetic rail near the thread wall. Her slate showed **SEPARATIST SABOTAGE — forty-two percent**; the woman beside her showed thirty-nine on the same screen. Same story, different feeds.

She watched three travelers argue in front of it.

"—obvious separatist timing, you don't suspend a major corridor without—"

"—cover-up, they're hiding casualty numbers—"

"—maintenance, my brother's a keeper, he says the guild posts are—"

"—your brother's a romantic—"

The third voice lost. The first two had volume on their side.

A fourth traveler joined—older, admin shoes, voice tired. "You're all wrong. It's resting. Gates rest."

"Gates don't rest," the separatist voice said. "They get closed."

"Gates rest when housing needs—" the admin voice started.

"When housing needs what? A press release?" The separatist voice was a young man with festival pins from two districts—Meridian and Northhook, mismatched on purpose. "My cousin flies Drift. He says fees moved before the bulletin."

The cover-up voice—a woman with a child's snack in one hand—said, "Because somebody knew."

"Because somebody always knows." The admin traveler rubbed his eyes. "That's not the same as sabotage."

The young man jabbed a thumb at the wall. "Then explain fifty percent."

"I can't. That's why I'm eating breakfast standing up like an idiot." The admin traveler took a bite of something gray and nutritious. "If I could explain it, I'd be behind the desk, not in the rope line."

Above the argument, **VESPER GATE — SUSPENDED** held steady in administrative white. Below it, **SEPARATIST SABOTAGE** had climbed past forty since yesterday. **ROUTINE MAINTENANCE** sat at the bottom like homework nobody wanted to do.

Lena finished her breakfast and went to find out what the Bureau was telling people who asked official questions.

The Bureau desk sat behind a queue rope near the public lift—Consensus gray, polite signage, a screen cycling *YOUR PATIENCE IS MEASURED*. Lena took a number. Waited. Watched travelers stand in the rope line until someone behind the desk decided for them.

The number ahead of hers belonged to a man holding a child's manifest case and a patience that had run out twenty minutes ago. He argued with a floor aide about whether redirected meant *helped* or *dismissed*. The aide smiled the Consensus smile—polite, matched, promising nothing.

"They're measuring sentiment," the man said loud enough for the rope line to hear. "My daughter's school trip is measuring whether it eats lunch in a transit corridor. Same measurement?"

The aide didn't blink. "Different department."

Lena watched two more numbers cycle. A woman in keeper grays cut through the rope—not cheating, staff lane—and dropped a slate on the aide's podium. "Tolerance logs for Vesper housing. Public copy. If anyone asks."

"That's transit," the aide said.

"That's work language. File it where you file feelings." The keeper left before the aide could answer.

Lena stayed at the podium. "What am I supposed to do with that slate?"

The aide looked at it the way you looked at someone else's laundry. "Not my job."

"It's on your desk."

"Then I don't know." He was already calling the next number. "Next."

When her number came, the desk clerk said, "Vesper inquiries are redirected to transit."

"I'm not inquiring about Vesper. I'm inquiring about what you're measuring while Vesper's closed."

The clerk blinked. "What people say on the wall."

"And if sentiment's wrong?"

"Then we publish a correction." Flat voice. Practiced. He glanced at the keeper slate still sitting on the side podium, unclaimed. "Or someone publishes a log. Whoever posts second, I guess."

"Who wins?"

"Whoever's louder by midday." He shrugged. "Sometimes whoever posts first. I don't make the rules. Next."

Lena didn't have a next. She crossed the transit ring toward the public lift.

On the way she passed a maintenance crew sealing a housing seam near the transit corridor.

"Photographers today?" the crew lead asked.

"Courier."

"Same thing." He wiped his hands on a cloth that had seen better decades. "Everyone wants a story. We're just making sure the seams stay checked."

"You think the wall's lying?" Lena asked.

The crew lead looked at her patch. "I think the wall's loud. Seams take an hour. Fear takes thirty seconds on a screen." He nodded toward the transit corridor. "Vesper's not my housing. I still don't like silence on a major gate."

"Nobody does."

"Somebody does." He picked up his kit. "From people buying the scary version. I don't know who. I know my kit's still short on brushes."

One of the younger crew members—apprentice band still bright—said, "We recorded tolerance yesterday. Keeper tried to post it. Nobody liked it. Nobody liked it yesterday either. Same log."

"Because it's boring," the crew lead said.

"I don't know why nobody reads it," the apprentice said.

The crew lead sighed. "Finish the secondary seam. Then we're on lunch."

He turned back to Lena. "Wall says sabotage. My log says tolerance. I fix seams. That's today's job."

She didn't know what to say to that except thank you, which felt insufficient and accurate.

---

The observation lip sat above Lake Orin like a balcony on the wrong axis—Northhook underfoot, Meridian Arc overhead, the thread wall bright enough to read from altitude if you didn't mind the glare.

Lena had climbed up to get away from the rope line. She told herself she was checking sightlines for a long-route corridor she might never fly. Her manifest was still half-checked in a berth locker. Her brother still hadn't answered. The percentages had climbed while she slept, and the Bureau had measured her patience like it was weather.

Students with tablets crowded the rail. A transit reporter had set up a portable mic. At the center, where the glare was worst, a man in Consensus Bureau gray recorded the thread wall on a calibrated display.

He looked like his job had taught him to stand.

Corin leaned over the display and muttered, "Your green is too bright. It looks like you're cheering for maintenance."

"I'm not cheering for maintenance."

"Then fix the legend."

Jun didn't look up. "After the reporter. Corin, fix the legend."

She edged close enough to read his chart layer. Sabotage had crossed fifty percent since she'd gone to sleep. Maintenance had stopped shrinking.

The man in gray murmured to a younger assistant, "Second wave accelerating. Good."

"Good?" Lena said before she'd decided to speak.

He glanced at her. Not rude. Not warm. The glance of someone sorting her into a category before she'd finished speaking.

"Good for measurement," he said. "Bad for everyone else. Those aren't the same sentence."

His assistant—barely more than a student, hungry eyes—made a note without looking up.

Lena pointed at the sabotage slice. "People are going to route around Vesper because they're afraid of separatists, not because separatists did anything."

"People route around the scary version." The man's voice stayed level. "They don't wait for proof. Fees move anyway."

"Mine too." She tapped her compass patch. "I'm trying to reach Orinreach Basin. My brother maintains mirrors on the southern basin. He's not a number on your chart."

The man paused half a beat—not long enough to be apology, long enough to be recalculation.

"Orinreach Basin still accepting relay delivery?"

"Yes."

"Then he's likely fine." He said it the way you said *likely* when you had charts instead of siblings. "Suspension isn't contact loss. Not yet."

"Not yet."

He turned back to his display. "Separatists—I can't—" He stopped. "I don't—look. I have odds. What people are betting. Not truth."

Lena felt heat in her face that wasn't the Lake Orin glare. "My brother isn't a bet."

"I know." Too fast, like he'd been caught. "I still don't have him on the chart unless you file him."

"Which helps who?"

"Administrators." He said it without flinching. "Pilots who price early. Couriers who read fear as cargo." His eyes flicked to her patch again. "People who move before the story settles."

"I'm not moving before I know—"

"You're already moving." He gestured at the rail, the lake, the thread wall below. "You came up here before you checked your manifest."

She had. She'd told herself it was research.

"I'm Jun," he added. "Consensus analyst. This is Corin. We're publishing a brief by midday."

Corin nodded once, still writing.

"What's the brief called?"

Jun's mouth twitched. Not quite humor. "Working title: *What Silence Buys*."

"Catchy."

"Accurate." He zoomed a timeline. "People panic before they read anything. Then they want—"

"Jun," Corin said quietly.

"—a face." Jun gestured at the sabotage slice without looking at his assistant. "Somebody to yell at. Separatists are— they're handy. Old border stuff. Vesper's not even in it if you look."

"A three-wave model," Corin muttered, barely audible.

Jun heard him. "It's not a model when you're living in it. It's Tuesday."

"You sound pleased."

"I'm not." Jun corrected gently. "I just finally got numbers that aren't garbage."

Corin made a small sound that might have been disagreement or might have been throat clearing.

Lena glanced at him. Corin looked young enough to still believe charts were moral.

"Corin thinks I'm enjoying this," Jun said, without looking at his assistant. "I'm not. Twelve gate towns. One model. Before this it was noise and prayer and guesswork."

Corin said, quietly, "Legible isn't the same as true."

Jun heard him. "No. That's why we keep measuring."

"That's worse," Lena said—to Jun, but Corin nodded once like she'd spoken to both of them.

"Probably." Jun didn't argue.

Corin shifted his tablet so she could see the keeper logs—not the chart, the raw lines. Tolerance schedules. Housing checks. Timestamps in work language that made the sabotage layer look like graffiti.

"If you publish these," Lena said, "will anyone read them?"

Corin glanced at Jun. "Jun says no."

"I say not yet," Jun corrected. "Plain language first. Then logs. If you lead with logs, people think you're hiding a villain in the footnotes."

"And if you lead with fear?"

Jun's mouth tightened. "Then people move freight. Badly." He looked at Corin. "We're testing a keeper appendix for the brief. Corin wants it on page one."

"I want it on page one because my sister flies cargo," Corin said. He wasn't performing humility. He was angry in a quiet way Lena recognized from clerks who'd been overruled by supervisors. "She's not going to read page nine."

Jun said, gently, "Your sister reads fees."

"Exactly."

Lena looked out over Lake Orin. Tourists below were doing the reflection exercise again—match your stair, don't vomit. A man in a bright jacket had graduated from bruised knees to confident wrong turns.

"My brother sent a message six days before suspension," she said. "Storms. Tourists. No gates."

"One district," Jun said. "One message. I measure twelve gate towns and the loop."

"What does that do for him?"

"Nothing." He tapped his chart. "Orinreach doesn't show here unless someone files it."

"I'm not filing my brother."

"Then he's not on my chart." Jun said it without cruelty—just the temperature of the job.

"Will separatists hold through festival?" a student asked Jun's back.

"Define separatists," Jun said, still looking at his chart.

"You're the analyst."

"Then ask me something I can count." He turned. "Festival prep makes everything loud. I don't know loud yet."

"The Bureau—reopening?" a reporter tried.

"Not my department." Jun was already turning back to the screen. "Try transit. Try keepers. Don't try me for hope."

Lena waited until the crowd shifted and Jun's shoulders dropped a fraction—exhaustion, not performance.

Corin finally looked up. "The keepers are posting again. Housing check logs. Tolerance schedules."

"Corin's been reading keeper logs since dawn," Jun said. "He thinks measurement should include work language."

"If you find keeper posts that move fees, post them plain," Corin said. He wasn't looking at Jun now. He was looking at Lena. "Not for Jun. For tea carts."

Jun's mouth tightened. "Corin."

"I'm not quitting." Corin went back to his tablet. "I'm correcting."

Jun pulled the layer. A thin green line—maintenance language—pushed against the red sabotage mass like a hand against a crowd.

"Not catching," he said.

"Because it's boring," Lena said.

Jun watched the green line plateau. "Keeper posted the same log yesterday. And the day before. I counted clicks."

"You counted clicks?"

"Somebody has to." He saved his layer. "It's not zero. It's just not winning."

"You ever try posting keeper logs in plain language?" she asked.

Jun looked at her. "Plain to who?"

"To people at tea carts."

"Then they'd argue about the plain language too." He saved his layer. "The guild posts. People scroll past."

Lena looked once more at the green line pushing against the red mass. It wasn't winning. It was still there.

---

She left the lip and rode the lift back down to the transit ring.

Down at Northhook the separatist story had picked up details overnight—a route sequence Lena knew was wrong, a clip in a Markets dialect Dael had warned her about. Someone had edited the border on a map and posted it like evidence.

At the transit desk she pulled fee schedules for the long route and stared at numbers that changed depending on which administration had woken up angry.

Meridian Arc surcharge: provisional.

Archive adjacency corridor: open, ugly, unpopular.

Vesper: closed.

The clerk watched her scroll. "You're not the first courier reserving around."

"I reserved yesterday."

"Smart." He slid a printed strip across the desk—old habit, paper when systems got nervous. "Guild advisory. Read the last line."

She read it.

**IF YOU MOVE ON FEAR ALONE, CHECK THE GROUND YOURSELF.**

No signature. No guild seal she recognized. Just sense on official-looking paper.

"You believe that?" she asked.

The clerk shrugged. "I believe fees move before bulletins. Same thing, different uniform."

"Who printed this?"

"Who do you think?" He tapped the strip. "Not the Bureau. Not transit. Somebody with a guild cousin and a printer. It showed up on three desks this morning. That means somebody with hands wants couriers to look at seams before percentages."

A courier beside her at the next terminal swore at the Meridian surcharge loud enough to turn heads. "Story tax," he said to nobody. "You see sixty percent on the wall and they charge you for breathing."

The clerk didn't look up. "Pay or don't. The corridor doesn't care about your feelings."

The courier slammed his palm on the counter—not hard enough to trigger security, hard enough to mean it. "I paid yesterday."

"Yesterday's fees are yesterday's story." The clerk slid the deduction through anyway. "You want stable pricing, wait for the third wave."

"What's the third wave?"

"Identity." The clerk said it like a weather report. "You'll know when your mother calls and asks what kind of courier you are now."

The courier paid. Lena watched the number deduct and felt her own jaw tighten in sympathy she didn't have time for.

"You're still holding?" the clerk asked her, quieter.

"Holding."

"Then update your ledger before noon. Meridian changes at lunch when the festival prep crowd wakes up." He nodded toward the thread wall. "Your ledger says tracking. Pick tracking or reserved and stop refreshing both."

---

She spent the afternoon doing courier work because clients didn't pause for civilization.

A client with wedding supplies wanted her to swear Vesper would reopen by festival.

"If I swear it, will you pay the rush fee?" Lena asked.

The client hesitated. "That's not—"

"Then I won't swear." She filed the manifest. "Check the wall yourself. It's cheaper than my guess."

The client leaned in, voice dropping. "My sister's on Orinreach Basin. If you're holding, tell me you're holding. I can tell her something besides percentages."

Lena looked at him—ordinary face, ordinary fear. "I'm holding. I don't know when Vesper opens. I'm not paying rush fees on a guess."

He nodded like she'd given him a gift he couldn't afford. "That's more than the Bureau gave me."

She collected two routine scans and left before anyone else could ask her to swear on a percentage.

---

Jun was still at the rail at dusk, tablet dimmed against evening light. Meridian Arc had begun its paired-festival prep overhead—twin colors on sashes, synchronized lighting tests that made the lake hold two sunsets at once.

Corin had left. Jun stood alone, watching the thread wall.

Lena stopped beside him at a respectful distance—close enough to talk, far enough to leave.

"You published?" she asked.

"Draft upward." Jun didn't look at her. "They'll sand the verbs before morning. They always do."

"Will it help my brother?"

"No."

"Will it help me reach him?"

Jun considered. "Maybe—maybe you'll see which stories are going to spike your fees on the long corridor. That's not nothing."

"That's not enough."

"I didn't say it was enough." He turned at last. "What are you going to do?"

Lena watched a tourist on the lower path miss a stair and catch the rail. The reflection in the lake missed the stair a second later, perfectly, insultingly precise.

She almost said *wait*.

"I'm going to reach my brother before my berth runs out."

Jun was quiet for a moment.

"There's a third part," he said, slower than he meant to. "After fear, after explanation—people pick sides. You don't need my chart for that. You're already in it."

"I understand my job."

"Good." He saved a final tag on his chart—not her name, she checked; some anonymous travel flag she didn't like seeing and couldn't prove was her. "If you find something that moves fees before my columns update, send a message. I have a colleague on Drift who insults me professionally. It keeps me honest."

"Nadi?"

His eyebrows lifted. "You know Nadi?"

"I know pilots file analysts under *later*." She stepped back from the rail. "She flies Drift?"

"She flies everything she can insult." Jun's mouth twitched. "If you end up on her route, tell her the separatist frame is at fifty-eight in the morning release. She'll argue the number and then be right about the fee anyway."

Lena hesitated at the lift mouth. "If I send what I find on the ground, you'll publish it?"

"I'll measure it." Jun said it without apology. "If it moves, it goes in the appendix. If it doesn't move, Corin will still try to put it on page one and lose."

"He might win someday."

"That's what keeps me paying him." Jun dimmed his tablet. "Ask Nadi if you want ground. She reads the dock before my columns update."

She looked at him. "You tag couriers too?"

"I tag patterns." He almost smiled. "You're not fitting mine. Good."

"I'll keep that in mind."

"Send what you find." He wasn't asking for friendship. He was asking for data. "Nadi's on Drift if you need a pilot who reads fees before bulletins."

"Send your brief. I'll read it. I won't believe it just because it's measured."

"Nobody should."

She left him to his curves and climbed back down to the transit ring.

Meridian Arc's paired-festival prep had spread to the lower decks—twin-colored sashes on kiosk staff, synchronized lighting tests flickering across the lake surface. Tourists mistook the tests for emergencies. Locals stepped around them without breaking stride.

At Northhook she opened **STORIES** and saved the clip with the route sequence that didn't match her map. She tagged it *wrong border* because wrong was something a courier could file.

She opened her ledger at the transit desk—the clerk busy with someone else's fees—and changed the reserved block's note to:

**TRACKING.**

The clerk glanced over. "You find anything on the ground yet?"

"No."

"Then tracking's honest." He went back to his screen. "Checking the ground yourself costs extra on the long corridor. Everybody pays it eventually."

Lena shouldered her bag. Arin's queue remained unchanged. She thought about the message draft—*I'm not waiting on the wall, I'm waiting on you*—and deleted it unsent.

She walked past the thread wall one last time on the way to the berth. **SEPARATIST SABOTAGE** had climbed again since dusk. **ROUTINE MAINTENANCE** hadn't moved enough to matter.

A keeper in grays posted a tolerance schedule at the bottom of the wall. Nobody looked.

On the connector she passed the junction where Northhook's running light should have been burning—courier handoff point, every shift someone supposed to take the watch lamp from the outgoing runner and carry it to the next lip. The lamp was out. Not dock-dark. The specific dark of a duty abandoned mid-shift. A transit worker in corridor orange was posting a staffing stub beside it. She didn't stop. She went to sleep badly.
