# Chapter 05 — Reserved

Two days left on the berth.

The notice had arrived without ceremony—a line item in her manifest queue while she slept.

**NORTHHOOK BERTH — TERMINAL IN 48 HOURS — RENEW OR RELEASE**

Lena read it at the fold-down desk with cold tea from yesterday and the thread wall percentages reflected in the porthole glass. **SEPARATIST SABOTAGE** had crossed seventy sometime in the night. Vesper remained closed. Arin's last message still said *don't come the long way on my account.*

The priority handoff had cleared days ago—**DELIVERED TO ORINREACH BASIN**—with no reply attached.

She ran the numbers twice.

Renewal cost four more days and another fee—enough to erase the priority handoff she'd already regretted. Commitment cost eleven days around and fees that climbed when stories climbed. Waiting cost whatever waiting cost when your brother sent pictures of marks instead of answers.

She pulled up the renewal fee on her display and compared it to the long-route block. Four more days at Northhook wouldn't open Vesper. It would only make **TRACKING** feel responsible while **COMMITTED** waited in the margin.

The math was ugly. Renewal plus priority handoff plus Drift surcharge at current wall volume would leave her broke before Orinreach. Commitment plus Drift left thin margin—pilot margin, eaten standing up.

Waiting left Arin on a ladder and the folder one photograph thicker.

She built a third column on the fold-down desk—renewal, commitment, wait—and put numbers beside each until the screen looked like someone else's problem.

The berth hummed while she did math. Six days of humming. Two days left. One decision.

She opened **STORIES** one last time. Three tracks she'd filed, not a reason: mark photos, wrong-border pamphlet, fee strips that had moved before the bulletin.

Arin had said don't come on his account.

He'd also sent a picture of a mark on a housing seam—not an explanation, just proof someone had checked the wall.

She didn't renew.

She walked the berth once more with the bag packed and counted what she was leaving—the fold-down desk, the porthole view of doubled Meridian lights, six days of humming she'd stopped hearing until it was time to go.

---

Second-to-last morning, and the transit desk was where decisions became paper.

**RESERVED — TRACKING — ORINREACH BASIN — 11 DAYS — 2 FEES**

She opened the block at the transit desk on her second-to-last morning and changed one word.

**COMMITTED.**

The clerk watched the update cross his screen. "You sure?"

"No."

"That's the usual answer." He didn't try to talk her out of it. Clerks saved arguments for fees, not for family. "Long corridor's ugly today. Meridian surcharge jumped again."

"How much?"

He turned the screen so she could see. The number was worse than yesterday. Worse than this morning. Story tax, Dael had called it. The wall didn't have to be true to be expensive.

"I saw."

He pulled up her queue and frowned at something she couldn't see from her side of the desk. "Priority handoff from two days ago cleared. Orinreach Basin delivery confirmed. No return traffic on that corridor today."

"So?"

"So your brother got the expensive message." He said it without judgment. "If you're committing around, you're not paying for speed anymore. You're paying for distance."

"I know what I'm paying for."

"Everybody thinks they do." He printed the outbound strip—paper when systems got nervous—and slid it across. "Eleven days around. Two fee tiers. Drift leg first—you know the drill?"

"I know the drill."

"Then you know Drift'll skin you if you stand still." He lowered his voice. "Markets board early when Vesper's loud. Don't miss corridor seven arguing with a percentage."

"Jun's separatists at seventy?"

"Jun's separatists at whatever makes you pay." The clerk almost smiled. "Gate'll reopen," he added, reflexive—the comfort people offered when they didn't know what else to say. "They always do. You'll look foolish running the long route for nothing."

"Everybody says that." Lena shouldered her bag. "And everybody's usually selling something."

The clerk tapped his screen. "Long corridor manifest's in your queue. Don't miss the window. Meridian gets mean about mean. And Lena—"

She paused.

"When you get to Orinreach, ask your brother what *resting* means on the basin boards. I had three couriers this morning say the same word. Nobody likes it."

"I'll ask."

"I didn't say he'd answer."

She tapped the **COMMITTED** block once more on her display. The clerk watched but didn't comment. Clerks had seen worse than couriers confirming fear with a keystroke.

"Drift window's tight," he said instead. "Boarding line's already stacking. You want paper for Markets fees?"

"Paper's for when systems get nervous."

"Systems are nervous." He printed a second strip—fee schedule, story tax highlighted—and slid it under the first. "Don't say I didn't warn you."

"I've looked foolish before," she said, and took the strip.

The transit hall was busy for second-to-last morning—families, freight, someone already arguing about separatists near the thread wall. Lena didn't stop. **COMMITTED** was in her ledger now. The rest was corridors and fees and distance.

A child asked a parent why Vesper was closed. The parent said, "Because people are scared," and Lena walked past before she could hear which people.

---

The berth looked smaller with the bag packed.

She'd lived six days in a box that hummed with the Ribbon. She'd stopped hearing it until she was leaving. The compass patch on her shoulder caught the berth light—faded at the edges, still readable. She'd meant to replace it. She'd kept not replacing it.

She filed the last manifest queries from the fold-down desk. Deleted two draft messages to Arin that said the same thing in different tones. Kept the folder—wrong borders, mark photos, Jun's brief, route clips—in the bag where it couldn't pretend to be optional.

The **COMMITTED** block sat in her ledger next to eleven days and two fees. Real now. Paper-thin until she crossed the first corridor.

Dael caught her in the hangar corridor with a rolled manifest tube and the expression of someone who'd come to say something he didn't want to be right about.

"You're leaving."

"Drift leg first. Then around."

"Not straight through."

"Not on these fees." She shifted the bag. "You were right. Reserved wasn't committed."

"Yeah." He leaned against the wall. "I wasn't trying to win an argument. I was trying to—" He stopped, embarrassed by sincerity. "My sister says hello, by the way. Orinreach Basin. Still annoyed. Still fine."

Lena felt her throat tighten. "Tell her I heard that."

"I will." Dael hesitated. "Separatists are at what, seventy now?"

"Higher."

"You believe it?"

"The pamphlet had the wrong border on the map." She adjusted the tube under her arm. "But it's enough to move freight."

"You could wait one more day. Gate might—"

"Might." Lena shifted the bag. "I've been mighting for a week."

Dael nodded once. "Send word when you hit Markets. I owe you a fee schedule that isn't lying."

"I'll collect."

He hesitated again, then pulled a scrap from his pocket—handwritten numbers, Drift leg, around leg, story tax column. "Don't let them charge you archive adjacency twice on the Markets side. They tried it on my sister last month."

She took the scrap. "You wrote this for me?"

"I wrote it because I'm tired of watching couriers get skinned for listening to a wall." He shrugged. "Go. Before you start tracking again."

"You'll still be here?"

"Somebody has to ship through fear and complain later." He almost smiled. "I'll be the one complaining."

He left. The hangar noise closed behind him—pilots, fees, stories too large to keep.

A pilot near the coffee urn called after her, "Drift corridor's boarding early—don't romanticize Northhook."

Lena didn't turn. "Wasn't planning to."

---

A message arrived before she reached the lake walk.

*Travel safe.*

Arin. Two words. No weather. No mirrors.

She sent back the old compass stamp she'd used in messages since they were teenagers.

*Following the mark,* she added, because two words from him didn't deserve a paragraph from her.

Delivered.

---

Lake Orin one last time, before Northhook let her go.

The lake held Meridian Arc in the water the way it always did—towers in the sky, towers below, the doubled city tourists never quite trusted. Festival sashes still showed twin colors on the lower path. Maintenance kites drifted east where rain was thinking about arriving.

A guide group passed her speaking in low voices about Vesper—separatists, cover-up, the usual weather. One tourist asked whether the lake was safe. The guide said, "The lake is always safe. It's the gates that argue."

Lena almost smiled.

The festival-sash woman from the first week sat on the bench with an orange, peeling it in strips. She looked up, recognized Lena—or recognized the patch—and held out a segment.

"Still waiting?" the woman asked.

"Leaving."

"Better." The woman nodded toward the transit ring below. "My nephew's still shouting separatists at dinner. Gate's still closed."

Lena took the orange. "Separatists?"

"Still separatists." The woman shrugged. "Orange is still good. Eat it before it gets brave."

"You flying around?" the woman asked.

"Around."

"My nephew says resting." The woman snorted. "Resting is what the bulletin says before the fees jump."

Lena ate the orange standing at the rail where she'd stood on the first morning—same rail, different ledger.

Below, a school group ran the reflection exercise. A child asked whether Meridian Arc was real or a trick. The teacher said, "Both."

A boy in the group pointed at the maintenance kites and asked if they were separatists. The teacher said, "They're kites." The boy looked disappointed. Stories with faces were easier homework.

The pilgrim's tourist was gone. Someone else was bruising a knee today. The lake kept teaching the same lesson to new people.

The maintenance kites were out again. Children pointed. Adults didn't.

She glanced up once, matched angles, and kept walking.

---

At a quiet kiosk away from the departure line, she wrote to Arin:

*Leaving the Ribbon tomorrow. Drift first, then around. Your mark is in my folder. I'm following it.*

She sent it.

The queue showed **DELIVERED TO ORINREACH BASIN**.

No reply. She hadn't expected one. Arin answered when his hands were dry.

She stood at the kiosk a moment longer, watching **DELIVERED** hold steady. Two words from him. Eleven days from her. The math didn't balance, but couriers learned to ship on imbalance.

---

She didn't sleep much that last night.

The berth hummed. Meridian Arc pulsed in the porthole glass. The thread wall percentages climbed in her mind even when she closed her eyes—sixty-eight, seventy, higher—numbers that moved freight faster than marks.

Once, in the dark, she pulled up the picture Arin had sent—the basin at dusk, the mark on the seam—and zoomed until the stroke filled the screen. Work. Not story.

She put the display away.

In the morning the suspension notice was still there. Still no official word why. The berth felt smaller in daylight—humming louder now that she was leaving.

She had a corridor to catch and a ledger that said **COMMITTED**.

---

Departure hour, and Northhook was ordinary until it wasn't.

Northhook Transit at departure hour was the same ordinary noise as always—freight crawlers overhead on the ring, announcement chimes, spiced tea from the magnetic rail in the corner, people grumbling about delays shared widely enough to feel impersonal.

The tea vendor nodded when she passed. No words. He'd said what he had to say days ago.

Lena moved through it with her bag and her tube.

She passed the bench where the festival-sash woman had peeled an orange. The maintenance mark was still there—dot and stroke, worn softer now.

At the thread wall she didn't stop. She'd read enough.

A child was asking a parent why the percentages were red. The parent said, "Because grown-ups are scared," and kept walking. Lena had heard worse answers.

The departure hall was full—families saying goodbye to freight, pilots arguing about weather that wasn't weather, a child asleep on a manifest case. Lena stood in line behind a woman shipping medical supplies to Drift.

"You running around Vesper?" the woman asked without turning.

"Around."

"My sister's on Orinreach." The woman shifted the case. "She says the boards look like resting. I don't know what resting means when freight stacks up."

"Nobody does." Lena adjusted her bag. "That's why they sell stories."

The woman almost laughed. "You a courier?"

"Yeah."

"Then you already know." She moved forward as the line advanced. "Drift's expensive when the wall's loud."

"I know."

Ahead, a clerk argued with a pilot about a corridor delay labeled *ADMINISTRATIVE* with no further explanation. Behind them, someone was already on a speakerphone with a client who wanted separatist timing turned into a refund.

The medical-supplies woman leaned her case against the wall when the line stalled. "You committed?" she asked.

Lena showed her the strip. **COMMITTED** in block letters, ugly and official.

"Bold." The woman whistled low. "My sister'll say you're foolish."

"My brother'll say I took too long."

"Siblings." The woman picked up her case when the line moved. "They always have opinions about corridors they aren't crossing."

The announcement chimed: **DRIFT MARKETS — BOARDING — CORRIDOR 7.**

Her name wasn't on a passenger list. Couriers boarded as cargo with opinions. She'd always liked that about the job.

Two couriers ahead of her argued in whispers about whether Drift fees would hold through the week.

"They always spike when Vesper's loud," one said.

"They spike when Markets smell blood," the other said. "Vesper's just the excuse this hour."

Lena listened because listening was free. The woman with medical supplies shifted her case and said, without turning, "You Drift leg or through?"

"Drift leg."

"Smart. Around's for people with time and bad luck."

"Or commitment."

The woman almost laughed. "Same thing, different ledger."

A family ahead of them was saying goodbye to a freight crate like it was a person—hands on the seal, whispered instructions, the soft absurdity of loving something that wasn't alive but still had to arrive on time. Lena looked away. Couriers didn't get goodbyes like that. They got strips and windows.

At the gate she paused.

Vesper's closure still sat on the wall in administrative white. Separatist sabotage still led the percentages. The green maintenance line still wasn't winning.

Lena crossed the corridor line.

The pressure behind her eyes was familiar—the wrong-light flicker, the smell like hot metal and clean rain. Her ears popped. Her stomach disagreed with the geometry. The floor under her boots went slick-then-gritty, the way corridors always pretended to be a hallway and then weren't. Her compass patch pulled warm against her shoulder—old superstition, courier habit, the body keeping score when geometry lied.

Sound thinned. Then thickened wrong—announcement chimes stretched, a child's laugh smeared across two beats, the thread wall's percentages flashing behind her eyelids even with her eyes open.

For one disorienting beat Northhook's departure hall doubled—the tea cart, the thread wall, a child asleep on a manifest case—then compressed into a bright line and spat her forward.

She didn't look back until she had to.

Northhook's departure hall shrank behind the wrong-light—freight crawlers, tea cart, thread wall still bright with fear she was leaving temporarily, not solving.

The Drift crossing spat her into a corridor that smelled less like lake and more like commerce. Signs pointed toward fees, pilots, arguments that hadn't been invented yesterday. The air was warmer here, drier, threaded with fried food and solvent and the particular ozone of corridors that saw too much traffic to pretend they were scenic.

A woman at the fee desk barely looked up. "Courier?"

"Courier."

"Story tax went up this morning." She said it like weather. "You know how it is."

"I know how it is." Lena paid.

The number matched Dael's scrap within a margin that felt insulting and accurate. She filed the receipt in the outer pouch next to Dael's handwritten scrap—paper when systems got nervous, paper when friends got honest.

A Markets runner behind her muttered about overnight spikes. "Story tax," she said to no one. "Same tax, new hour."

The fee clerk heard her. "You want a complaint form?"

"I want a corridor that doesn't read bulletins."

"Wrong window." The clerk stamped her wristband. "Boarding corridor's that way. Don't romanticize the fee desk either."

The pilot board showed three departures and one delay labeled *ADMINISTRATIVE* with no further explanation.

A pilot in a worn jacket scanned the board beside her. "You look like Drift."

"Drift leg."

"Then you're early or late. Hard to tell on this board." She snorted. "I'm Nadi if the schedule ever decides to be honest."

Lena looked at her. "Jun says hello."

Nadi's eyebrows lifted. "Jun can file himself under *later*." She tapped the board. "Drift corridor seven's still boarding. Don't stand here romanticizing Northhook. Commerce waits for nobody."

"I'm moving."

"Good." Nadi was already walking away. "Tell Jun his separatist number's wrong. It always is. The fee's still right."

A Markets vendor shouted prices at nobody in particular. A pilot swore at the administrative delay. The boarding corridor smelled like solvent and fried dough and the ozone after a crossing—commerce's version of weather.

Lena adjusted her bag, felt the folder's weight against her hip, and walked.

Somewhere behind her, Northhook kept humming.

Lena walked toward the boarding corridor with her folder of marks and her ledger that finally said **COMMITTED**.

Somewhere ahead, Drift Markets waited with its own wall and its own stories.

She had eleven days around and a folder of mark photos that weren't on the wall.

That would have to be enough until she saw the basin herself.
