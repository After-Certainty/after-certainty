# Chapter 03 — Southern Mirrors

Third morning on the berth—four days left. The deck hummed under her bunk; somewhere a transit door sealed with a pressure thump she felt through the frame.

She'd slept badly. Arin's last message had delivered. Nothing since.

Jun's brief arrived on her slate before the tea cart's magnetic crawler locked to the morning rail.

Lena read it standing at the cart window with a paper cup warming her hands.

**WHAT SILENCE BUYS — CONSENSUS BUREAU PRELIMINARY RELEASE**

*May indicate* where Jun's mouth had said *is*. *Public uncertainty* where his chart had said *fear*. Separatist story leading. Maintenance language not catching.

The annex had one section that read like work—**MARK REFRESH LOGS — NONPUBLIC — SUMMARY ONLY**—buried under bureaucratic numbering. She read it twice. Someone had checked housings. Someone had recorded tolerance schedules. The verbs were sanded, but the nouns were still hands on walls.

Corin had wanted this buried. Jun had wanted it measured.

She saved the wrong-route clip from yesterday into **STORIES** with the brief and walked down to the transit ring.

---

The thread wall was already arguing when she reached the lower loop—same percentages, louder voices. Lena took the side corridor toward the benches instead of the main flow.

Arin's reply was waiting in her queue—after two days without a word.

*I'm fine. Storm cycle, busy mirrors, tourists attempting suicide by reflection. Gate news is weird—administration says wait. Don't come the long way on my account. Eat something on that berth.*

*Don't come the long way on my account.*

*The southern path you like is closed for maintenance anyway. Not because of gate. Because I said so.*

No mention of keeper schedules. No mention of whether the basin administration had said *wait* or *resting* or nothing at all.

Lena found a bench near the thread wall—not the one with the orange woman, a different bench, colder—and typed with her thumb.

*I saw the brief. Separatists winning on the wall. Are you actually fine?*

She read it once. Sent it.

A reply arrived in four minutes—fast for basin storm window.

*Define fine.*

Lena typed: *Hands on mirrors. Food in mouth. Administration not lying to you.*

Longer pause. Then:

*Two out of three isn't bad.*

*Which two?*

*You know which two.*

She almost laughed. Arin could be funny when he wanted to be difficult. He could also be evasive when he wanted to be kind.

*Are the keepers funded?* she typed.

*Ask me when I'm not on a ladder.*

*That's not an answer.*

*It's the only one I've got right now.*

She stared at the screen. Arin had always been worse at lying than he thought. *Not on a ladder* meant he was on one, or about to be, or using ladder as a synonym for *I don't want to talk about money on a relay thread.*

*Jun buried keeper logs in the brief,* she typed. *Actual housings. Actual checks. Why won't administration say that out loud?*

Another long pause—long enough that the delivery readout flickered once and went still.

*They won't put passed inspection on the wall,* Arin wrote. *Doesn't scare anyone. Basin boards want a bad guy before they'll pay for a brush.*

*So fund the brush anyway.*

*With what? Tourist postcards?*

The heat in that line was rare. Arin didn't snap on threads. He saved snapping for tourists who touched wet mirrors.

*I'm not asking you to fix the basin,* Lena typed. *I'm asking if you're safe on the housing you're maintaining.*

*I'm safe on the housing I'm maintaining. I'm not safe on the housing administration pretends doesn't need maintaining.*

*Arin.*

*What.*

*Stop answering like a bulletin.*

A minute. Then:

*Sorry. Storm's loud. Budget meeting was louder. Eat your breakfast. Stop reading annexes meant for people with seals.*

*I don't have a seal.*

*I know. That's why you're reading annexes.*

She wanted to throw the display into the lake. She wanted to laugh. Siblings were a crossing you made whether the window was open or not.

*Are the keepers funded?* she typed again, because some questions deserved to be asked twice.

*Not enough. Never enough. Ask me when I'm not on a ladder wasn't a dodge. It was a schedule.*

*You sent me a picture instead of answering,* she typed. *That's a dodge too.*

*Picture's not a dodge. Picture's proof.*

*Proof of what?*

*That someone checked the housing before I put my harness on it.*

She read that twice. Arin didn't send proof in words. He sent a picture of a mark on a seam—and that was new.

*Then tell me about funding on a thread,* she wrote. *Not on a ladder. After shift.*

*After shift I want food and sleep.*

*After shift you want to avoid me.*

*After shift I want to not fight with my sister about administration on a relay that charges by the syllable.*

Fair. Also not an answer.

*Fine,* she typed. *Eat. Sleep. Fight tomorrow.*

*Tomorrow's storm window.*

*Of course it is.*

The queue showed **DELIVERED TO ORINREACH BASIN** on each line. Relay text was still fast when the corridors cooperated. Honesty was another matter.

She watched the delivery readout for three minutes after the last delivery, as if staring could pull another answer across storm windows and mirror work and whatever *because I said so* meant when Arin said it.

Nothing more.

---

Midmorning on the transit ring, and the bench had changed temperature with the sun.

A man sat down at the other end of the bench—courier patch, bag worn shiny at the corners.

"You waiting on Orinreach?" he asked.

"You're nosy."

"I'm bored." He watched the thread wall. "My partner's on the basin. She says the mirrors are fine and the administration is full of shit. She also says don't quote her."

"Wasn't going to."

"Everybody's waiting on someone." He stood. "Storm windows close. Or they don't. You pay your fees and you find out."

He left before she could ask his partner's name. Couriers traded routes, not intimacies.

---

The Northhook transit desk sat in the main hall where foot traffic made patience a performance.

At the desk she asked the question clerks hated.

"Alternate route advisories for Orinreach Basin when Vesper's suspended—what's the lag?"

The clerk didn't look up from her screen. "Depends which corridor."

"Archive adjacency."

"Ugly. Two jurisdictions. Sometimes six hours, sometimes two days if someone's feeling territorial about signature handoffs."

"Meridian long corridor?"

"Faster if you pay. Still not instant." The clerk finally glanced at Lena's patch. "You trying to reach family?"

"Trying to get a straight answer."

"Wrong window." Flat sympathy. "Try the relay. If it delivered once, it'll deliver again."

"It delivered. He answered. I answered back. Nothing's moving."

"Voice relay?"

Lena paused. Couriers lived in text because text left receipts. But receipts weren't replies.

"Open window for Orinreach Basin?"

The clerk tapped her screen. "Courier priority? Not for free. And not if your brother's hands are on a ladder."

"He maintains mirrors."

"Then he's on a ladder." Flat sympathy again. "Try again after storm window. Or pay for a ping."

The kiosk beside the desk wanted more than Lena liked and less than panic would pay. She paid anyway.

The speaker rang twice—a tinny ring that was supposed to sound like a corridor opening.

For a heartbeat she heard wind—not voice, not words, but the basin side of a storm window, mirrors creaking, someone breathing hard.

Then static—a granular hiss like sand poured over tin. A second voice underneath, maybe, chopped into syllables that never assembled.

*—can't—* something. *—harness—* something else.

Then nothing.

**NO ANSWER — ORINREACH BASIN — STORM WINDOW ACTIVE**

The kiosk screen added a line in smaller type: **PARTIAL CARRIER — VOICE FRAGMENT LOGGED — NOT BILLABLE**

Lena stared at *not billable* like the machine had done her a favor by failing cheaply.

A family with luggage watched her fail with the polite interest of people who'd seen worse at gates. The mother shifted her case closer, as if failed voice relay were contagious.

The clerk didn't look away. "Storm window's real. Mirror crews don't answer voice when they're on harness. Text might still—"

"I texted. He answered. Then he didn't."

"Then wait for storm window to close." The clerk's voice stayed flat. "Or pay again later and hope. Those are the options the kiosk gives me."

"Your kiosk caught a fragment."

The clerk's mouth tightened. "Fragments aren't conversations. They don't refund and they don't deliver." She glanced at the family. "Next."

Lena stood at the desk long enough to feel foolish, then long enough to feel angry. "Christ," she said, not quite under her breath, and left before she could say something that would get filed under *difficult courier*.

Behind her the kiosk chimed softly—the sound of a receipt printing for a call that had almost been a person.

A transit kid in training uniform was wiping the kiosk screen with a cloth that had seen worse days. "Fragments happen," he said, not quite to her. "Storm windows chop carrier. You want a supervisor?"

"No."

"Good. Supervisors charge more and say the same thing." He nodded at the speaker grille. "My cousin's on basin mirrors. He texts. He doesn't answer voice either. Says voice makes you sound scared."

"Does he sound scared?"

"He sounds like someone on a ladder." The kid shrugged. "That's basin for you."

---

The keeper corridor ran off the transit ring behind a painted arrow most tourists mistook for staff-only décor.

Lena had walked past it a dozen times. Today she followed the arrow.

The notice board sat in a side alcove—easy to miss if you weren't looking for work language.

**MARK SCHEDULE — NORTHHOOK APPROACH — RAIN WINDOW — HOUSINGS 4–11**

A woman in keeper grays was pinning an update when Lena arrived. The paper smelled like adhesive and rain.

"You read that?" Lena asked.

The woman glanced at her patch. "You a courier?"

"Yes."

"Then you read routes, not marks." She finished pinning and smoothed the corner with the side of her hand. "But if you're asking—someone checked. That's what it says."

"Who teaches the grammar?"

The keeper's mouth twitched. "People who don't have your job." Not unkind. Final. "If you want a story about blame, the wall's that way."

She left. Lena stayed long enough to photograph the stroke-and-dot on the board frame—the same grammar as the seam marks, not the schedule numbers beside it—and went back to the transit ring.

Another keeper passed—male, older, reading the board without stopping. Lena stepped into his path.

"Does that mean the corridor's safe?"

He looked at her patch. "It means housings four through eleven were checked before rain. Safe is a word for tourists."

"Is Vesper—"

"Vesper's not on my board." He walked on. "Ask the wall. They love Vesper."

A younger keeper—apprentice stripe on the sleeve—was copying numbers from the board into a handheld ledger in the corridor's better light.

"You're recording housings?" Lena asked.

"Someone has to." The apprentice didn't look up. "Wall logs percentages. We log seams."

"Does administration read your logs?"

"Administration reads what makes them feel in charge." The apprentice capped the stylus. "Jun's people read them. Sometimes."

"Jun's not administration."

"No." For the first time the apprentice smiled, briefly. "That's why his annex is buried."

Down the corridor, two keepers argued in low voices about brush sizes for rain windows—not philosophy, inventory. One said the guild had shorted them again. The other said the guild had shorted everyone and that was different from sabotage.

Lena listened until the argument resolved into a shared curse and a plan to borrow from housing nine.

Work language. Hands language. Nothing the thread wall would carry without calling boring.

---

Back on the upper loop, a man in transit security livery read the sabotage thread aloud in the main corridor, voice carrying. "—deliberate corridor collapse on the separatist schedule—"

Lena walked past without stopping. She'd heard the clip. The route sequence was wrong. The border on the map was wrong. The volume was right enough to move freight anyway.

---

She passed a housing post with a fresh mark—dot and stroke, cut clean into the seal paint.

A transit worker in coveralls caught her staring.

"You collecting graffiti?"

"Trying to learn what it means."

"It's not graffiti." He said it without heat, like correcting a tourist. "Keepers."

"You read it?"

"I read that someone was here." He shifted his tool bag. A wrench handle stuck out like a punctuation mark. "Don't ask me the grammar. I just like knowing the wall got checked."

He walked on. Lena touched the mark with one finger—not to decode it, to feel the edge. Fresh. Recent. Work done while the thread wall screamed sabotage.

---

Noon on the transit ring, and waiting had started to feel like a decision she hadn't made.

She filed the priority handoff at midday because the alternative was another afternoon of fragments and percentages.

The transit kiosk sat in an alcove near the transit corridor—bright screen, ugly fees, distance sold by the hour.

The kiosk let her route a message through Meridian long corridor for a fee that made her jaw tighten. **ESTIMATED DELIVERY TO ORINREACH BASIN: 18–36 HOURS.**

Eighteen hours minimum. Her last message might arrive before his next shift ended. Might.

Behind her, a courier argued with a clerk about a different corridor, different fees, same fear dressed as paperwork.

"You can't charge story tax on archive adjacency," the courier said. "It's ugly but it's not Vesper."

"It's not my policy," the clerk said. "It's my screen."

"It's bullshit policy."

"It's the only screen I've got."

The courier slapped a wristband on the counter. "Fine. Fine. Just mark it delivered before my client fires me for listening to the wall."

The clerk scanned. "Can't promise delivered. Can promise sent."

"Sent isn't delivered."

"Welcome to corridors."

The courier paid anyway. Lena watched the number deduct and felt her own jaw tighten in sympathy she didn't have time for.

She pulled up her balance. The priority fee would eat two Drift legs worth of margin. Three, if the wall climbed another point overnight.

She thought about Arin's *stop paying for corridors you don't need* and confirmed the handoff anyway.

A woman behind her in line made the same calculation out loud. "Eighteen hours for a sentence. My kid's on the basin too. Administration says wait. Kid says eat. I say pay." She tapped confirm before Lena did. "Nonrefundable's the whole habitat lately."

The fee deducted. The kiosk printed a receipt with a route sequence she didn't trust and a delivery window wide enough to hide bureaucracy.

**MERIDIAN LONG — PRIORITY COURIER — NONREFUNDABLE**

Nonrefundable sat on the receipt like a dare.

While the kiosk processed payment, her display chimed—Arin.

*Reserved doesn't mean committed. Tell me what administration actually said. Not the boards—the people.*

She typed with her back against the kiosk wall, out of the foot traffic.

*People said wait. Nobody said why. You haven't mentioned keepers or funding.*

Send.

Delivered.

Her display chimed before the receipt finished printing.

*Stop paying for priority corridors. I'm fine. Storm's ending. If you come, come because you chose, not because the wall scared you.*

She typed back:

*I'm choosing. Not the wall. You.*

Send.

No reply for twenty minutes. Then:

*Fine. Eat. Stop paying for corridors you don't need.*

She stared at the line. Arin had never used *fine* that way—not weather, not agreement. A sibling word for *stop worrying* that never meant stop worrying.

---

The tea cart on the upper loop had its own weather.

She ate lunch from the cart because Arin had told her to eat and because anger made bad fuel.

The tea vendor nodded when she passed—half spiced, mercy disguised as mistake. She didn't argue today.

"You look like someone who paid for a voice that didn't talk," he said.

"Storm window ate it."

"My brother runs freight through Vesper." He wiped the counter. "He's still not talking to me. Gate's still closed. You still waiting on someone?"

"Yes."

"Then eat." He pushed a second cup across without being asked. "Insurance refreshes at noon. Full stomach won't stop that, but it helps."

---

Late afternoon, and the picture arrived—no text, just image.

Orinreach Basin at dusk, doubled lights in the water, east housing on the basin rim. Storm light, not festival light. Mirrors catching the last clean angle before weather closed in.

She zoomed until she found the mark.

Dot and stroke on the housing seam, fresh cut catching light. Arin hadn't labeled it. He didn't need to. The grammar was the same as Northhook—the same hand, the same check, the same quiet insistence that walls could be true even when bulletins weren't.

She saved the image to **STORIES** and climbed to the upper loop to compare it to the mark on the post she'd touched earlier.

Same stroke. Same dot. Different district, same language.

She held the display beside the painted seam and matched angles the way she'd learned on Lake Orin—housing, not reflection. The dot sat left of the stroke on both. The cut depth looked the same in storm light and Northhook afternoon.

*Picture received. I see it.*

Send.

She wanted to add *what does because I said so mean* and didn't. Arin hated parsing on a thread. He answered when his hands were dry, not when she wanted a thesis.

*Good,* he wrote back, two hours later. *Now stop zooming at housings and eat dinner.*

*Bossy.*

*Older.*

She almost smiled.

She pulled up the Northhook mark photo and Arin's basin image side by side—left dot, right stroke, same cut on both seams.

A tourist leaned over her shoulder. "What's that, some kind of code?"

"Maintenance."

"Boring."

"Yes."

The tourist wandered off to find a story with a face. Lena saved both images to **STORIES** and closed the folder before she could count them again.

---

At fourteen, on his only long leave to the Ribbon, Arin had pointed at Meridian Arc overhead and said, *That's a trick.*

Lena had said, *It's both.*

They'd waved from opposite curves the next day, ridiculous and precise, and laughed when the timing lagged.

Arin had been seventeen and sure the basin was the real world and the Ribbon was a trick you visited. Lena had been fourteen and sure the opposite. They'd both been wrong in the way siblings were wrong—completely, usefully, with affection underneath.

She didn't have time for the memory long. She had a folder filling with things that weren't routes.

---

Evening on the observation lip, and the percentages had had all day to climb.

She climbed because the thread wall looked different from altitude when fear accumulated in daylight.

Sabotage was higher on the screen. Maintenance lower. Jun's green line barely visible under the red mass.

Her priority handoff cleared while she watched—**DELIVERED TO ORINREACH BASIN**—with no new message attached.

Below, tourists were still doing the reflection exercise. A child stamped a puddle and laughed. The lake didn't read bulletins.

Jun was gone. Corin was gone. The reporter's mic had been packed away.

A student remained, filming the thread wall for a project. Lena overheard her narration: "—public uncertainty as measurable—" and walked away before she could hear herself turned into a category.

The thread wall from altitude was percentages and light.

Lena rode the lift back down.

---

Night on the berth, and the long-route block was the only decision left on the desk.

Back at the berth she opened the long-route block.

**RESERVED — TRACKING — ORINREACH BASIN — 11 DAYS — 2 FEES**

In the margin she wrote: *Because I said so.*

She sent one more line to Arin:

*Got the picture. Mark's clear. Eat actual food.*

Delivered.

She lay in the bunk and listened to Meridian Arc's festival lights test against the dark water. Somewhere overhead, paired colors pulsed. Somewhere on Orinreach Basin, her brother was maintaining mirrors in a storm window she'd paid to shout into and hadn't reached.

She opened **STORIES** one more time—not to add, to check the three tracks. Wrong border. Mark photos. Jun's brief only for the annex page on housings checked.

The ledger stayed **TRACKING**.

Not **COMMITTED**.

Not yet.
