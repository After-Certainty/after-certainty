# Chapter 3 — The Slow Way

"Twenty-two days," the captain said, and did not look up from the coolant line he was scraping. "To the Tarn approach, if the pressure holds green in the secondary tank. If the seals start to weep we drop out at the three-quarter mark to let the radiators breathe. Call it twenty-six."

His name was Harl — a square-built man with the thick blunt thumbs of someone who tightened fuel-couplings by feel, bent into an open intake valve and picking lime-scale off the line with a length of sharpened wire. The boat stairs had brought her down to him: the dim iron mouth under the south concourse that couriers passed a thousand times and never took, because down was where the slow traffic crawled and a courier had never in her life had reason to follow it. The freighter tied at the bottom of them was called the *Osprey-Nine* — a bulk-hauler so old, and retrofitted so many times, that no one could have said what it first looked like, and it smelled from the gangway of pickled cabbage and damp slag and forty years of grease-traps that had never quite come clean.

She knew pilots — a courier knew everyone who moved between the worlds — and the one she'd have wanted for a run like this was Nadi, who could fly the rim routes in her sleep and charged you like she knew it. But Nadi was off working the drift markets with a full hold, the way the good ones always were the day you needed them, and the slow trade took whoever turned up. What had turned up was Harl.

"The gate takes four seconds," Lena said.

"The gate isn't there." He gave the wire another twist, still not looking up; a crust of white broke loose and rattled down into the sump. "You want to sit on the benches with the fruit-sellers till the clerks clear the wire, that's a fine plan for somebody. You want the far side of the Tarn before the winter turn, you pay for the grease I'll burn getting you round the rim. Sennen first — and I'm hearing Sennen's arch is acting up — then the Haldis leg, then a hop nobody likes into the approach. That road's older than your doorway. It'll be there after, too."

This was what the gates made everyone forget. A gate was a doorway: you stepped through on one world and out on another, and the distance between them stopped counting. For seven hundred years people had crossed as if the worlds were close. Under the doorways the older dark was still there — the long cold stretch between the actual worlds, the one everyone had spent centuries free to ignore. Days to a near world. Weeks to a far one. A gate going dark didn't cut a world off from everywhere. It only cut it off from *fast*.

"How long's the Tarn shut?" she asked, and caught the slip as it left her.

Harl finally looked up. "Tarn's not shut. Vesper's dark. Different word." He said it flatly, the way people who actually knew a thing said it to people who only thought they did. "Worlds don't close. Doors close. The world's right where it always was. It's just a long way again."

She paid him with three silver transit tokens and a twist of dry tea she'd been keeping for a return leg she now had no way to take. Harl dropped the silver in his vest, looked at the tea a moment longer than he'd looked at the silver, and pocketed that too. He did not thank her. He told her to bring her own blanket and stay clear of the crane-lines.

On the way to her berth he showed her the water gauge — a scarred glass tube with a chalk line scratched on the frame beside it. "That's the both of us, twenty-six days," he said. "I don't top up past Sennen; the rim valves lie. You wash in a cup and drink what the cup leaves." He tapped the chalk and went back to his work. A line on a glass that only went down: the first thing the slow way had given her that she could feel as a number, the way a gate never once made you feel the distance it crossed.

"You've got someone over there," he said — not a question; he'd watched her flinch at neither the fare nor the weeks.

"My brother."

"Mm." He went back to the wire. "I'll not tell you it'll be fine. I've stopped saying that this week; it started sounding like the notices." A chunk of scale rattled into the sump. "Whatever that is under your arm" — the dead panel, which she still had not put down — "carry it the whole way. People who set a thing down on my boat have generally decided to give up, and I don't care for that coming aboard."

She tightened her grip on it without deciding to.

---

She climbed back up into a Ribbon already busy again — chicory steam, voucher lines, the south concourse talking over itself.

It had taken less than a day. A dark gate was a thing that got fixed; everyone believed it the way they believed the warmth would come back into the floor — not a hope but a fact about how the world was built — and so the new notice on the board had been written to fit the belief. The first notice was gone. She stood in front of the new one with the old one under her arm and read the improvement.

> **VESPER CROSSING — TEMPORARY SUSPENSION (FAULT CODE 0). RESTORATION ESTIMATE: PENDING. THE REGIONAL TRANSIT AUTHORITY REGRETS THE DISRUPTION AND THANKS TRAVELLERS FOR THEIR PATIENCE.**

Fault Code 0. She almost admired it — somewhere upstream a person had needed a fault code badly enough to invent one that meant *no fault has been identified* and made it sound like a category instead of a confession. There was an authority named now, and a regret, and a thanks, and an estimate that was just the word *pending*. Downstairs, pending had been a shrug. Up here it read as policy. It was longer than the first notice and it said less, because it had been given time to be helped. The first one — the one under her arm, the back of it gone blank, the one she'd pulled off the board when the air went out of the room — had been honest only because nobody had had time yet to rewrite it.

The crowd liked the new notice better, and you could watch them decide to. *Temporary* did a great deal of work; so did *thanks for your patience*, which made waiting sound like a virtue instead of a fear. By evening the south concourse had the grumbling, almost cheerful air of a place that had agreed to treat a catastrophe as a delay. Vouchers were honoured through Meridian. A man sold hot chicory off a cart to the queue for the gates that still worked, and did well. The whole bright competent surface of the Ribbon had covered the hole again — loud enough that you had to look twice for the dark gate.

Corin's crate was in the south holding bays by now — the seed-stock and whatever rode under it, logged to her tally, bound for settlements past Vesper it could no longer reach, sitting in the dark with a thousand other stranded shipments. She was the courier of record on it, and a courier did not leave a consignment on the floor; that was the first rule, the one that made the rose mean anything. She was going to leave it on the floor anyway — not hand it on, not argue it up the line, just walk down the boat stairs and let it sit, hungry settlements and all, because none of it was Arin. That morning she'd made Corin sweat over nine kilos he shouldn't have moved. By nightfall she'd be worse than him: a courier who'd decided whose cargo counted. She told herself she was a sister now. It did not feel like that. It felt like a thing she'd have judged in somebody else.

She had spent four hours, that first day, holding a cable nobody came to fix. They'd come in the end — two maintenance hands and a clamp — and taken the dead weight out of her hands and Edrin's with the brisk impersonal care of people who did not want to be told how long it had been held. They fixed it the only way anything got fixed now: they kept one old thing from getting worse, and no one could call it better. They'd carried Edrin up on a board, his knee splinted with a route-marker batten, swearing in purple about the indignity. Somebody had asked her to sign that she'd been in a restricted section during a fault. The form offered unavailable, inaccessible, non-responsive, administratively withheld, or other. She checked OTHER and wrote *wall,* signed it *L. — courier, present*, and kept the pen. She'd learned something in those four hours that the chicory and the vouchers and Fault Code 0 were all working to undo, and she found she could not stand in the bright concourse and let them. So she'd gone down the boat stairs instead.

Jun had kept at the maps after they carried Edrin up. Twice before the *Osprey* cast off a runner found her with another page of references copied out in Jun's cramped hand — *auxiliary, return, legacy, do not remove* — each note more worried than the one before, the last of them saying only: *None of these go where the records say they go. Be careful what you trust.* She'd folded the pages in behind the dead panel. She did not know yet what they were for. She kept them anyway.

---

She had a berth at the transient barracks for one more night and did not sleep in it.

She sat on the edge of the cot with her bag between her boots, the gate-hum still missing from under everything, and took the candies out of the inner pocket and turned the twist of paper over, feeling the sharp square corners of the sugar crystals through the brown paper. Peppermint drops. Their mother had kept them in a tin up high, out of reach, and doled them out like medicine, one apiece, on the days that earned one — a tooth out, a fever broken, the pump thawed before the line could split, the first time Lena brought a skiff in alone against the north wind without scraping the lock wall; two on the rare day she sat all the way through a basin council and kicked only one chair on her way out. Arin had never once managed to save his. He'd pocket three with the solemn discipline of a monk and turn up ten minutes later chewing like a man being persecuted by sugar — eyes shut, ruinous — while Lena made hers last twenty minutes, and he'd watch her do it with the open envy of someone watching a thing he genuinely could not. Thirty-four now, and she'd put money he still couldn't be trusted with the tin. She'd bought these to send him, with a tag that said *next time*, because everything she ever wanted to say had never once fit on a courier-tag. *Next time* had stopped being a promise years ago. It was the arrangement they'd settled into — the door to him always open, every hour, no plan or ticket required.

She put them back. There was no route left to send them.

Not the postcard Tarn — the still black lake high in its bowl of rock, the famous southern mirrors where on a calm morning the cliffs and the sky stood in the water so cleanly that tourists came a long slow way just to be fooled by it. The other one. The canal locks Arin tended at the low end of the basin; the reed-rot smell the low water threw up under a north wind; the fog that settled thick every few years and drowned the bell-buoys, so that for a week the only people who could find the channel were the ones who'd learned it by hand. Arin had stayed for that. He'd say he stayed for the locks, the work, somebody had to — but he'd stayed for the way the lake looked different each season to whoever was patient enough to be there for all of them, and he'd long since quit pretending to her that the two reasons were different.

*Storms over the southern mirrors again*, his last message had said — the one she'd read half-asleep six days ago and never answered. *You'd laugh — tourists trying to navigate by the reflections. One man followed the lake-path twenty minutes before he worked out the stairs were above him.* She could hear him say it, see the exact tilt of his head at the tourist, the not-unkind contempt he kept for people who mistook the picture of a place for the place. He'd have watched the whole twenty minutes and said nothing, because for Arin a person who'd just walked into their own mistake didn't need him to explain it.

*You took the easy road.* He'd said it once — the only time either of them ever said any of it out loud — the morning she signed her courier oath, the two of them out on the mud-dyke behind the lower lock with grey fog dense off the low water, wet enough to bead on the shovel handle. He'd had the shovel over one shoulder and a stripe of mud up his cheek from going into the drainage cut earlier, which he was at some pains to suggest had been tactical. *You get to go where the stone's dry,* he'd said. *Where the houses have two floors and nobody's coat smells of peat-smoke. You get to leave before things freeze.* Behind him the canal lock leaked in three quiet places, because everything on the Tarn leaked eventually, and people called the leaks weather. He wasn't angry that she was leaving. He just couldn't imagine choosing a door you stepped through over a roof you stayed and mended. To Arin the gates were an unnatural sort of magic, a trick played on the distance between things; he trusted what had weight — a ditch you'd dug, a lock you'd hauled by hand, a thing that made you earn it.

*They open for everyone,* she'd said.

*No.* He'd looked at her then, really looked, the water behind him going grey under the low sky. *They open for people who can afford to have somewhere else to be.*

She'd hated him for that, mostly because it sat close enough to true to leave a bruise. *The road isn't easy,* she'd told him.

*No,* he'd said. *It's just fast.*

He'd left it there, which from Arin was a whole argument, and in all the years since neither of them had ever gone back and finished it. Each was privately sure the other had taken the lesser path. Neither had asked whether they were still fighting or only both afraid. She pitied him a little, for measuring a life in one lake's moods. She knew he pitied her back — for calling that lightness freedom.

*It's just fast.*

Now it wasn't even that.

She had been near enough to come, the day it went dark. Three slow-routes out, four if the weather pushed. Near enough, for a courier. And she hadn't, because she never did, because *next time* asked nothing of her — no risk, and it stayed open at every hour. She pressed a hand flat to her own sternum in the dark, where the cold had reached the bone that morning against the stone, and made herself feel the new fact all the way through: the door was closed. Not far. Closed. Arin would see the empty line where her arrival stamp should sit and tell himself she'd taken a paying run toward the rim — being smart, saving for spring seed. He would not know, could not be told, that the road between them hadn't narrowed or grown dear or dangerous. It had stopped existing. And if he didn't know, he'd take the skiff out into the southern mirrors in a storm to check the bell-buoys — somebody had to — with no one left to tell him the channel had moved.

She did not sleep.

---

They cast off before second sitting the next day.

Lena climbed to the upper observation tier — a narrow rusted gallery clinging to the outer skin of the cargo bay — and through the thick yellowed plexiglass watched the Ribbon get small. It had never once looked small. From the platforms it was the whole sky, the fixed and humming center of the world. From here, falling astern, it was a thin strip of lit metal against the black, docking lamps blinking one after another down its length. The *Osprey's* engines came up under her boots, a deep *thump-shudder, thump-shudder* that set a buzz in her teeth. Twenty-two days of that, instead of four seconds.

She took out the slate and wrote the message she could not send.

*Coming the long way. Don't take the skiff out. I mean it. — L.*

The wheel turned. **NO VALID RELAY PATH TO DESTINATION.**

She'd known it would. She put the slate away and took out the candies instead — and the tag she'd tied to them, *next time*, in her own hand. With the pen she'd kept she wrote beneath it, slower, the letters coming cramped and small: *Next time was too late. I know.* Paper kept the line. A slate would have swallowed it. She folded the tag back around the twist, message and peppermint together, into a packet that would reach no one, and pushed it down into her inner pocket, against her ribs. Something for the weeks ahead.

She watched the Ribbon dwindle to a grey speck in the freighter's wake, and faced the worst small piece of it — worse than twenty-two days in the dark. If Vesper stayed shut, Arin would never know she'd come at all. He would not picture her on a cold gallery aboard a stinking freighter, counting buoys toward him for a month. He would picture her where he always had: out on the dry stone where the houses had two floors and the floors didn't smell of peat-smoke, being light, being smart, taking the easy road — right up until she rattled his latch.

She drew the blanket round her shoulders and settled in to be carried the only way left, slowly, toward Arin's wooden door on the lake — weeks of dark between her and the latch.
