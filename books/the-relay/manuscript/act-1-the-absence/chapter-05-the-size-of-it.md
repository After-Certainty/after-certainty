# Chapter 5 — The Size of It

Lena had always known the network was big. She had simply never had to look at it.

That was the difference the slow way kept teaching her, leg by leg. Knowing a thing was big was a fact you carried lightly, the way you carried the fact that there were a thousand worlds — a number off a schoolroom wall, filed away beside the age of the gates and the distance to a star — true and weightless, never once converted into anything you had to feel. She'd carried that number her entire life. She'd flown a working slice of it for twenty years and called herself well-travelled. And she had never, not once, had to *see* it, because the gates had made seeing unnecessary — you stepped through a door and the distance simply didn't apply, and a courier who crossed forty worlds in a season crossed them the way you cross rooms, without ever once holding the whole of it in her mind.

Now the dark held the whole of it up in front of her, and would not let her look away.

The pilot who'd taken her off Harl's hand-off was a taciturn man named Osko who ran the long way round to the Tarn approach for cargoes too cheap or too strange to rate a gate, and who had not, before this month, expected company. His hauler was older even than the *Osprey-Nine* and slower, and he flew it with a hand always somewhere on it, listening, the way a keeper keeps a gate. The long way round meant exactly what it said. With Haldis dark, the route bent wide, out along the old slow corridors that predated the gates by no one knew how long, and Lena spent days at the one small port watching the real distance go by, and there was so much of it. That was the thing the schoolroom number had never managed to say: not that the worlds were many, but that the space *between* was vast, and had been there the whole time under the doorways, agreed by everyone for seven centuries not to be looked at.

"You're doing the thing," Osko said, on the fourth day, without turning from his board.

"What thing."

"The thing the gate people do, first long crossing. Sitting at the port like the dark's going to say something." He adjusted a trim she couldn't see the need for. "It doesn't. It's just big. You get used to it or you fly fast for a living and never have to."

"How long have you flown slow?"

"All my life. My people did. Before the gate at Sennen there was forty of us on this corridor; my grandmother could name the buoys from memory in the dark." He said it without grievance, the way Harl had, the long flat tone of someone watching a forgotten thing become suddenly, briefly, the only thing that worked. "Now it's me and two others and a lot of people about to remember why their great-grandparents were afraid of the sky."

She asked him what the slow people had that the gate people didn't, expecting a boast, the way Harl might have given her one. Osko thought about it the length of a course correction. "Patience and bad maps," he said. "And the habit of not trusting a door because it opened yesterday." He went back to his board and left it there.

She learned, over the days, what holding cost. Osko bled coolant from a line and let it hang away into the dark in a slow bright fan, gone for good, because the radiators had no gate-grid to flush them and a hot line killed you out here as surely as a cold one, only slower. He scraped the intake filters by hand, on his knees, doing the work the self-cleaners had done in a breath back when there was current to spare. When she asked once why he wouldn't cut a corner past a dead buoy and save a day, he looked at her as though she'd offered to spend a lung. "Save a day skipping a buoy," he said, "and spend three days of heat the once it turns out that was the buoy holding the line. Out here you don't spend what you can't make back. You'll learn that or you'll freeze learning it." She felt it in her own body within the week — the cup of wash-water, the rationed warmth, the dark she had stopped being able to call empty.

Bolted to the bulkhead behind his chair was a thing she'd taken at first for dead weight: a squat brass drum feeding a slow roll of charcoaled paper under a rank of fine needles. On the third day she asked what it was. A tuning-recorder, he said — in his grandmother's day every slow hull on the corridor had carried one. The needles read his crossing against its neighbours: the small constant adjustments each keeper made, passed buoy to buoy and station to station, so that a tweak at one gate could be answered all down the line and the corridor kept itself in tune. When it worked, the needles drew a braid — seven threads laid close and clean. The paper coming out of it now was blank. The needles sat parked at the margin, not broken — Osko flicked one and it swung true and settled back — only with nothing left on the far end to answer them. He kept the drum turning anyway. Habit, he said. And so he'd know the day a needle moved. It hadn't moved in years. She asked when it last had. He had to count back: a keeper at Sennen, riding out a bad alignment, before the gate there ever went dark — a needle had jumped, held, settled, and Osko had watched the braid pull itself straight again down the whole length of the paper, seven threads finding each other, and had felt, he said, the way you feel when a sick animal lifts its head. Nothing had jumped since. The paper came out white, and he kept the drum turning, the way you keep a porch light burning for someone the whole street has stopped expecting.

They put in at a waystation on the sixth day to take on fuel, and it was the waystation that did it — that turned the number on the wall into the cold in her chest, the way the cable had turned *suspended* into a thing with her hands on it.

She'd expected an outpost. It was a ruin that worked. A long spine of accreted metal and stone, hung at a junction of slow corridors, that had plainly been built and rebuilt and repurposed across so many lifetimes that no single intention survived in it anywhere — a docking ring grafted onto a fuel depot grafted onto what might once have been a chapel, the whole of it patched with the salvage of a dozen eras, signage in scripts she half-recognised and scripts she didn't, an airlock seal stamped with a maker's mark seven hundred years dead and still holding. It was filthy and it was freezing in the unused sections and it was, beyond any argument, *alive*: the lights came on, the air moved, the fuel flowed, and a single keeper came out to meet them.

His name was Pell and he was old and he was alone, and he tended the whole impossible spine by himself with the unbothered competence of a man who had inherited a job and never thought to ask its size.

"Six this month," he said, of the slow haulers putting in. "Had two a season before. Everybody's remembering the back roads." He took Osko's tally and stamped it with a stamp worn to a ghost. "Gates failing, I hear. Up the line."

She'd already seen what *six this month* looked like, coming in. The warm sections of the spine, the ones Pell kept lit, were crowded with people a week removed from being gate-folk and visibly unsure how to be anything else — a freight crew sleeping in shifts against their own stacked cargo because no one had come to take it; a woman in good city clothes boiling something on a borrowed ring, watching the corridor mouth as though a door might open in it if she only kept faith; two traders who had plainly never in their lives carried their own water, learning. They had the look she was coming to know, the look she'd worn herself stepping off the lift at Vesper: people who had walked toward a door that wasn't there and were only now finding out how far they still had to go without it. No one had told them the slow way still existed. They had simply arrived at it, the way water finds the lowest place.

"Vesper, Sennen, Haldis," Lena said. "At least."

"Mm." Pell didn't seem surprised, or afraid, which she was learning to read now and which meant only that the news hadn't yet been converted into anything he had to feel. "They do that. Less than people think, more than people like." He led them down the spine toward the fuel quarter, past a hundred meters of salvaged history, and Lena, who noticed how things worked, could not stop noticing that this one worked at all. That was the frightening part. There was no schedule on the walls. No authority's name. No office. Pell ran a station that fed the slow trade of an entire corridor and answered, as far as she could tell, to no one.

"Who do you report to?" she asked. It came out more direct than she meant.

Pell thought about it, genuinely, the way you'd think about a question you'd never been asked. "Reported to old Orla before me. She had it off a man whose name I don't have. There's a sector office, somewhere, sends a supply run twice a year and a form I sign and send back." He shrugged. "Don't know that anyone reads it. Stopped coming for a while, the supply run — couple of years, back when I was younger. Thought maybe that was the end of it. Then it started again. Different markings on the crates. Never said why it stopped or why it came back." He said this the way another man might mention a change in the weather. "You learn not to ask. The station wants running. You run it. That's the whole of the job."

She watched him work for a while — there was nothing else to do, and watching a person work had always steadied her. He moved through the fuel quarter touching valves in an order written down nowhere, reading a gauge by the sound it made. Halfway along a pump gave a wrong note and quit, and back in the warm sections the air began, very slightly, to cool; the woman in the good city clothes was on her feet at once, a hand at her throat, because she had felt a thing go cold before and knew now what it could mean. Pell didn't hurry. He crossed to an unlabelled junction, laid his palm flat on the housing the way the Sennen keeper had laid his on the stone, and knocked a seized valve over with the heel of his hand until it caught. The pump found its note; the air warmed. It had taken under a minute and lived nowhere but in his hands. "Wants doing every few days now," he said, to no one. "Used to be months."

"What happens if you stop?" she asked. "If you're sick. If you—" She didn't finish it.

Pell didn't take it hard. "Station runs down," he said. "Slow. Cold sections first, then the warm ones. Air'd hold a while. Light a while less." He tightened something that had been waiting for it. "Somebody'd come, eventually. Find it dark. Same as you find the dark gates." He said it without self-pity, as a fact about the world, and that was the part that undid her — not that the spine depended on one old man, but that the old man had long ago made his peace with the fact that no one would notice if it stopped.

Lena stood in the middle of a structure older than her civilization's memory of itself, kept alive by one old man who didn't know who'd built it or who he answered to or why the supplies once stopped and once returned, and felt the schoolroom number finally, fully, land.

A thousand worlds. She made herself do the arithmetic she'd never bothered to do, standing there, because the place forced it. A thousand worlds, near enough — and every one of them had gates, and every gate had a keeper like Pell, a person with a hand on the stone who knew their own crossing completely, locally, and not one inch past it. And the slow corridors between, the old roads, each with their waystations and their Pells. And couriers laid over the top carrying the word, and pilots carrying the weight, and offices somewhere sending forms that no one was sure anyone read. Seven centuries of it.

She had been looking for the person minding the whole since Vesper — find who touched it last, find who wouldn't sign it, find the office behind the smoked glass, find the hand. Edrin hadn't been him; he'd had a cable. Vane hadn't; he'd had a form. The dock at Sennen hadn't, so it had invented a hand to know *for* it. Every office took its sealed notice from another office. Every keeper signed his form and trusted the form went somewhere. Standing in Pell's spine, she could not find the top of the staircase.

"You all right?" Osko said. He'd come back for her; she'd stopped walking.

"How many waystations like this," she said. "On the corridors. All of them. How many."

Osko looked at her like the question was strange, and then, seeing her face, took it seriously. "Nobody knows," he said. "That's the truth. Used to be a count. The guilds kept it, my grandmother said. There's no count now." He glanced down the dim alive spine, at Pell already shuffling back toward his work. "There's just the ones you find when you need them. And the ones you don't find, that you only hear stopped answering."

---

They flew on, the long way, into the last leg before the Tarn approach, and Lena could not put the size down. It had got into her the way the missing hum had got in at Vesper — once felt, impossible to unfeel. On the second-to-last leg they passed a dead waystation, its dock cold and its bays unlit, its automated beacon still running on trickle power and broadcasting into the empty dark on a cheerful loop — *berths available, maintenance notified, thank you for your patience* — to a corridor that no longer had anyone left to thank. Osko didn't slow. He'd heard it before. Every dark port they passed now, every buoy that didn't answer Osko's hail, was no longer a fault on a route. It was a Pell who had stopped, or a corridor where the supply run had quietly never come back, a small silence in a machine so large that its small silences could go a generation unheard. She thought of Edrin saying *maybe some office moved a junction twenty years ago and updated every record but the one that matters*, and understood, finally, that he had not been listing possibilities. He had been describing the only kind of thing that *could* take down something this size: not a blow, which would be seen, but an omission, which would not — a thing nobody did, in a place nobody watched, that left no hole in any record because no one had ever written down that it was being done.

And the old roads were filling. She saw it from the port on the last legs — running lights strung along corridors where Osko swore there had never been traffic in his life, slow hulls and stranded gate-ships both, out on bad vectors, every one of them burning away from a doorway that had become a wall and remembering the back roads all at once. The dark between the worlds was crowding, and not with answers. It was filling with people who did not have one. *No one has one* — the empty thought she'd carried off Vesper, and here it was, made visible, strung clear across the sky, ship after ship of it, each captain certain the next port would hold the thing this one lacked.

Osko kept the open channel low — not off, just low, the way you leave a window cracked — and the voices on it filled the whole last leg. She sat by his board and listened to the corridor invent its reasons. A freight voice swearing the gates had been cut — *thread-cutters*, working the cargo routes, a fresh word for the old hand. Another voice, older and tired, saying it was the Core draining the rim, had to be, who else had the power. A third saying both were fools and offering nothing in their place. And under all of it, in every exchange, the thing no one would say outright: *no central statement.* No one coming on the wire to tell a thousand frightened ships what was happening. She understood, now, that there wasn't going to be one — that there was no wire that reached the whole, and no one at the end of it. The silence the stories rushed to fill went all the way down, and the voices seemed to know it, and rushed the harder for knowing.

None of it comforted her. *Three at once is a hand* let you sleep, because a hand could be caught and a hand could be stopped. What she had instead could not be: a thing this size, kept alive by a million people each minding one square of it and trusting a center that had never been there.

In her berth, in the dark, the size would not stay arithmetic. It kept turning into Arin — three legs and a hop away on a lake that told a different truth each season to whoever stayed to hear it, tending locks for a sector office that might or might not read his forms, taking the skiff out into the southern mirrors because somebody had to and the channel moved when the fog came wrong.

At some point she took the packet out — Arin's undelivered message folded around the peppermint drops, gone soft in her pocket, the message she'd been a day too late to send when sending still meant anything. She'd thought, on the *Osprey-Nine*, that she might put it in a locker and stop carrying it, because wanting to give it to him had become worse than the weight. She unfolded it instead, in the dark, and under the line she could no longer quite read she added one more: *I know how big it is now.* It wasn't comfort and it wasn't a plan. It was the only thing the slow way had left her that she fully understood. She could not send it. She could still carry it. Carrying, it was turning out, was the whole of the job — Pell's, Osko's, Edrin's hand on the cable, hers — a duty no one up the staircase had ever thought to relieve them of, because no one up there had ever known their names.

She had crossed out into the slow dark looking for the office that would explain it.

She lay in the cold and understood there was no office, and that this — not the silence at Vesper, not the fever in Sennen's arches, not even the closed country of *next time* — was the thing she should have been afraid of all along.

This was not a glitch.
