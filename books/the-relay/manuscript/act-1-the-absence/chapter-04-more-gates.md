# Chapter 4 — More Gates

Bad news travelled faster than Lena did, which should have been impossible, because she was on the fastest thing still moving.

That was the joke Harl kept making on the crossing to Sennen, and it stopped being funny somewhere in the second day. The *Osprey-Nine* was the fastest thing moving because the gates that used to be faster were dark. The slow way had quietly become the only way, and it took the time it had always taken — but the *word* did not. The word had its own routes. It went hand to hand at the resupply buoys, mouth to mouth where slow traffic crossed slow traffic in the long nowhere between worlds, and it reached Sennen before the freighter did, and was waiting on the dock to tell them what they already half feared.

Lena spent the crossing learning what the slow way felt like. It felt like time. There was no other word honest enough. A gate had taught her whole generation to treat the distance between worlds as a doorsill, a thing you stepped over without looking down; two days in the freighter's hold with the dark going past the one small port taught her that the doorsill had always been a chasm, and the gate had only ever been a way of not looking down. She didn't look much. She held the panel, and she watched the other passengers — a family rerouting around three dark gates to reach a fourth, two traders who would not say where they were from, a boy being sent to relatives the long way because the fast way had closed in his face — and she thought, more than once, *Arin would already understand this and be insufferable about it.* He'd spent his life inside one slow place. He knew what time felt like. She was learning it at thirty-six like a tourist learning the southern mirrors had stairs.

When they came up to it, Sennen was a junction world — the slow crews called it the Hinge: three gate-arches set in a row along a basalt headland that fell away on its seaward side to grey water and a tide that came in smelling of cold kelp and iron. It had been built to pass crowds through fast and was now doing nothing of the kind. Spray hung in the air off the swells below the lower tier; the waiting carts stood lashed under tarps gone stiff with salt; and the people packed against the quay rails had the dark-shouldered look of wool that has been wet a long time and stopped expecting to dry. Two of the three arches were lit and labouring. The third was dark.

Over the two that still ran, the maintenance gantries were cradled out and hooded in salt-stiff canvas — the rigs they ran only in the dead of winter, when the crews scraped the crust off the stone — and seeing them out in a working season, with the barges backed up in the harbour, told Lena more than the dark arch did. Somebody had already decided that whatever was wrong at Sennen would be called maintenance.

She knew the dark one before Harl said a word. She'd learned the look in a single morning and would apparently carry it now forever: the indicator banks gone out in sequence, the small crowd turned toward it with their bodies before their minds, the particular quality of a quiet where a hum used to be. But it was the two *lit* arches that put the cold in her. They were working the way a man works with a fever — the hum in them was there but wrong, a half-step low and unsteady, climbing and sagging, and every so often the doorway in them would thin, the far platform on the other side going faint as breath on glass, before it caught and steadied and a steward waved the held queue forward with a brightness that fooled no one. A keeper in a stained coat stood at the base of the middle arch with one hand flat on the stone, not doing anything, just feeling it, the way you'd keep a hand on a feverish child to know the instant it turned.

*Sennen's arch is acting up*, Harl had said, the way you mention weather. It had been the kind of thing you filed and didn't feel. Lena felt it now. The second gate, faltering — and her route ran through the worst of it, because of course it did; the slow way to the Tarn went on from here through a gate that was learning how to die in front of her eyes.

"Don't," Harl said, beside her, reading her face. "Not yet. It's labouring, not dark. People are still going through." A pause. "Some people are still going through."

On the dock the word that had beaten them there was already organised into a shape.

---

She heard it four times before she'd walked the length of the quay, and each time it was a little more finished than the last.

"—cut. That's what the pilots off the Haldis run are saying, it's not failing, it's being *cut*—"

"—the separatist worlds, the ones that always hated the gates, they want the old borders back—"

"—not the separatists, use your head — it's the *core*, the inner worlds drawing the power back into the middle and letting the rim go dark, same as they always have—"

"—Haldis went last night. Whole arch. So that's Vesper, and Sennen's on its way, and now Haldis—"

"—stands to reason. You don't get three at once by accident. Three at once is a *hand*."

Lena stood at a fish-stall she had no intention of buying from and listened to a story being built in real time. She didn't yet know that it was wrong. What unsettled her was how *good* it was — how cleanly it filled every gap that had frightened her at Vesper. Villains. Timing. Silence. Even the notices: *suspended pending review* read, in this mouth, as exactly the bloodless nothing a captured office would put up while the enemy did its work. At the far end of the quay the same fear had produced the opposite villain — the inner worlds hoarding the very power the separatists were supposedly cutting — and the two stories did not argue; they warmed the same hands.

Three at once is a hand. She turned the phrase over. Edrin had refused that answer in the dark of the gantry — a fistful of maybes instead: *maybe sabotage, maybe a legacy line, maybe some office moved a junction twenty years ago and updated every record but the one that matters.* He'd had no clean thing to hand upstairs, only a cable to hold. The dock had chosen. The people saying *cut* and *hand* looked less afraid than the people still saying *I don't understand*. She watched it spread along the quay — lamps coming *on* instead of going out.

And Haldis was her third leg. *Sennen, then Haldis, then a hop nobody likes into the Tarn approach.* Haldis, the dock said, had gone dark in the night. Whole arch.

She found Harl by the freighter, already in a low hard conversation with a port clerk, and waited for it to end, and didn't like Harl's face when it did.

"Haldis," Lena said.

"Haldis," Harl agreed. "Confirmed, not rumour. Arch went out around third watch. Which kills my middle leg." He rubbed his face. "There's a way round — there's always a way round, that's the slow trade, the routes are older than the gates — but it adds a week and it goes the long side of the Sennen approach, which" — he nodded at the feverish middle arch — "I'd rather not stake a hull on right now. I'll take you to the next live junction and find you a hand-off. That's the honest offer. I'm not flying into a maybe with a hold full of people because one of them's in a hurry."

"I understand."

"You say that a lot."

"I mean it a lot."

Harl almost smiled, and didn't, because the middle arch chose that moment to thin again — the hum sagging, the far platform fading to nothing — and this time it hung thin for three full seconds, four, long enough that the steward stopped his queue with a raised arm and the keeper with his hand on the stone went very still, and the whole quay held its breath with the gate. Then it caught. The platform firmed. The hum climbed back to its wrong half-step. The steward did not wave the queue on. He looked at the keeper, and the keeper looked at the steward, and without a word being shouted the message passed down the line that nobody else was going through today, and the queue began, slowly, to understand that it was now a crowd of stranded people, and to make the sounds that came with that.

---

Lena had seen a crowd start to turn at Vesper, in the upper hall, before Jun got on the baggage scale. She knew the noise of it now — the questions hardening into statements, the statements finding each other, the whole crowd looking for somewhere to point. It started here the same way. A man wanted to know who would pay for his held cargo. A woman wanted to know if the labouring arches were safe and was told *yes* by a steward in a voice that meant *I have not been told otherwise*, which she correctly heard as *no*. Someone said *separatists* and someone else said it back, harder, and the word began doing what it had been built on the dock to do.

And over the heads of all of them the port's own board told the opposite lie. Lena had passed it coming onto the quay — a tall standing slate of crossings and their statuses, and not one of them would say a plain word. Vesper *suspended pending review*; two coastal runs *weather-holding* under a clean sky; a freight lane *load-balancing*; Sennen's own arches *under seasonal maintenance*. Four bloodless phrases, and not one plain word among them, and the dock had already worked out what they were worth: a true weather-hold, posted honestly, now read as just another cover, because the office had spent its credit and could no longer be believed even on the days it happened to be telling the truth. *Review* had become the port's whole religion — no gods in it and no answers, only excellent stationery. Between a crowd inventing a hand and a board inventing a calendar, the one thing nobody on Sennen would say out loud was *we don't know.*

What Lena noticed — the way she noticed things, sideways, before she'd decided it mattered — was the one person on the quay who was not being pulled into the shape.

She was an unremarkable woman, middling years, plain travel-wool gone grey at the cuffs, no badge and no cargo and apparently no fear. While the crowd thickened toward the arch she was moving the other way, against it, and she was doing it so quietly that Lena seemed to be the only one who saw it happening: she had got hold of the rerouting family first, the one with the small children and the three dark gates behind them, and she was walking them off the open quay toward the lee of the customs sheds where it was warmer and out of the wind, and she had a hand light on the shoulder of the boy being sent to relatives, steering without seeming to push, and she was talking the whole time in a low even voice that didn't carry, so that you couldn't hear the words, only the *temperature* of them, which was the temperature of someone who had decided some time ago not to be afraid and had room left over to spend on other people.

She wasn't telling them anything. Lena drifted near enough, once, to catch a piece of it, expecting a theory — *it's the separatists, it's a fault, it'll be fixed, it won't* — and there was none. The woman was only saying *there's tea in the shed, and a wall out of the wind, and we'll know more by the morning, and your children should be warm while we wait to know it.* No story. No hand. Just a wall, and tea, and the next true small thing — and somehow three frightened families were doing what she said and the dock full of people with a clean, warm, finished theory were not doing anything at all except getting colder and surer.

Lena watched her the way Arin watched a tide arriving wrong — attention for a thing that did not fit and could not yet be named. She did not learn the woman's name. Their paths crossed for a held breath on a stranded quay; then a steward needed Lena to move and the woman was a grey shoulder among other shoulders, herding the cold and the small toward a warm wall. Lena filed her with the rest — the blue ticket, the returned crates, the held cable — and kept walking.

By dusk the story owned the dock. *Vesper, Sennen, Haldis — three at once is a hand.* It would be on the next slow boat out before Lena was, the way it had been here before her, travelling its own fast routes while she and Harl and the stranded families crawled the slow ones. Three gates now. Not one. Not a glitch at a single weak crossing — a thing that moved, that struck Vesper and bled to Sennen and took Haldis in the night, and that everyone around her had a name for and Lena, alone on the cold quay with a dead panel under her arm, did not.

She got Harl's honest offer and took it: a berth to the next live junction, a hand-off to a pilot who'd run the long way round to the Tarn approach. Another two weeks, maybe three. She stood at the rail as the *Osprey-Nine* pulled off the headland and watched Sennen's three arches fall behind — two of them lit and feverish, one of them dark. Past them the real dark opened up, the patient enormous distance the gates had taught everyone to forget. And somewhere far across it, three slow legs and a hop nobody liked away, was a brother who would by now have decided she'd taken a paying run toward the rim, who would not know to be afraid, and would take the skiff out anyway, because somebody had to.

*Three at once is a hand*, the dock had said, and been comforted.

Lena tightened her grip on the panel and was not comforted, and could not yet have told anyone why a thing that frightened everyone else into a story frightened her, instead, into needing the story to be false.
