# Chapter 04 — The Story That Fits

**SEPARATIST SABOTAGE — SIXTY-EIGHT PERCENT.**

Lena read the number over breakfast she didn't taste and checked the suspension notice. Still closed. Still clean.

Three days left on the berth. Arin hadn't written since the picture.

The tea cart had locked to the morning rail. She bought plain tea and stood where she could see the thread wall without being in the argument. Her slate showed sixty-eight; the man two meters left showed seventy-one.

**SEPARATIST SABOTAGE — SIXTY-EIGHT PERCENT.**

**ROUTINE MAINTENANCE — NINE PERCENT.**

She drank anyway.

---

Midmorning, and the Meridian Arc junction wanted more from everyone than distance usually cost.

She took the Meridian Arc junction handoff at midmorning because the client paid for speed and the thread wall paid for fear.

The junction was a glass-and-steel transit mouth where districts argued about jurisdiction in architecture—Meridian signage on one side, Northhook codes on the other, a security scan that treated everyone like a smuggler until proven boring.

Lena had flown this junction a dozen times. Today the scan took longer. Today the security officer asked her to open the outer pouch "random check" and stared at her compass patch like fabric could confess.

"Courier," she said.

"I see the patch." He scanned again. "Vesper's closed. Lots of couriers nervous. Nervous people do stupid things."

"I'm not nervous. I'm late."

"Late people do stupid things too." He tapped her bag with the scanner wand. "Secondary pouch."

"There is no secondary pouch."

"Outer flap."

She unzipped the flap. Manifest sleeve. Receipts. A folded map she'd stopped using because maps lied when pamphlets lied louder.

He pulled the map out, unfolded it, compared it to something on his screen. "Orinreach routing."

"Client routing."

"You're not flying Orinreach today."

"I'm flying a junction handoff to a client who thinks seals absorb mass."

His eyes flicked to her face. "You have family on the basin."

"Everybody has family somewhere."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you've got jurisdiction for." She kept her voice level. Clerks filed tone. Security filed patches.

He held the map a beat longer than necessary, then handed it back. "Vesper's closed. If you're building a long route in your head, we see the folder face."

"I have a folder face?"

"You have a tracking face." He waved her through. "Don't make me scan you twice. I have quotas and a wall full of percentages."

The client was already angry, which saved time.

The client met her at the counter with a manifest already half wrong.

His logo matched the firm she'd signed yesterday—the hunted optics crowd. He didn't recognize her. That was normal. Couriers were patches and manifests, not faces.

"You weighed it wet," he said when the scan disagreed.

"It's sealed."

"Seals absorb." He laughed without humor. "You think separatists are stealing mass now?"

"I think you can't read," Lena said.

His face tightened. "You couriers always—"

"Read your manifest. Slower."

Security stepped in before voices got louder. Witnesses signed. The manifest corrected. Nobody apologized.

The witness—a transit worker with steady hands—signed without looking at Lena. "Separatists," he said, half to himself.

"You were there for three kilos," Lena said.

"I was there for the scan." He capped his pen. "You want border politics, read the wall. You want weight, read your manifest."

The witness wanted to talk about border politics anyway while the scanner still flagged Lena's compass patch like patches were evidence now.

"You flying around?" he asked.

"Not yet."

"Tracking?"

"That's not your business."

"It's on your face." He shrugged. "Separatists make everybody a prophet. Don't be a prophet. Be a courier."

At the junction exit, a second security officer flagged her for a bag weigh-in that wasn't random—Vesper closure made everyone invent new rituals. He ran the scanner twice, frowned at the pamphlet sleeve inside her outer pouch, and said, "You carrying propaganda?"

"Carrying a client's packet."

"With propaganda inside."

"With a map inside that lies." She kept her hands where he could see them. "Filed for witness. Tagged for route."

He read the tag. "Wrong border."

"Yes."

"Not my jurisdiction." He waved her on. "But if you're flying around, every jurisdiction's going to want a piece of your fear. Get used to it."

By the time she left the junction her hands were steady, her coffee was cold, and the thread wall on her wrist had gained another point she didn't need to look at to feel.

She walked the connector corridor back toward Northhook with the pamphlet in her bag and the client's corrected manifest sealed. A pair of students argued ahead of her about whether separatist timing proved intent. One of them had a chart. Neither of them had a map.

---

Near the hangar corridor, a private packet needed a witness signature at a kiosk.

It arrived with a separatist pamphlet folded inside like a joke that wasn't joking.

Lena unfolded it under the kiosk light long enough to check the route schematic against the route she knew. Wrong border. Wrong jurisdiction. The map had been edited to make fear look like geography.

The border error wasn't subtle once you looked—Vesper's approach drawn two housings into separatist territory that wasn't separatist on any guild chart she'd ever flown. Someone had moved a line to make sabotage look local.

She photographed the border error, filed the pamphlet with the packet, and tagged **STORIES** again: *wrong border, junction handoff.*

A courier beside her was photographing a different pamphlet—same headline, different border error.

"Yours too?" Lena asked.

"Third one this week." The courier didn't look up. "Client wants them filed. Client doesn't want them read. Welcome to optics."

Nobody at the kiosk asked what they were photographing. Everyone was photographing something.

---

Noon in the hangar, and the noise had its own economy.

The courier hangar off Northhook filled at midday with the particular noise of people who lived between places—complaints about fees, boasts about corridors, the ritual exchange of information too small for the thread wall and too large to keep.

Lena ate standing up and listened.

Version one, from a woman with sun-cracked skin: separatists had blown a seal. She knew because her cousin's cousin had seen military traffic near a border corridor.

"My cousin's cousin sees military traffic in his coffee," someone muttered. The woman ignored him.

"He saw transports," she insisted. "Masked corridors. You don't mask a corridor for maintenance."

"You mask a corridor when you're embarrassed," the mirror-tech runner said. "Maintenance is embarrassing when the wall wants blood."

The woman looked at Lena's patch. "You flying Vesper?"

"Vesper's closed."

"Then you're routing on fear." She said it like accusation and invitation. "Long route's full of people who decided early."

"I'm not deciding early."

"Everybody decides." The woman walked away. "Some people just call it waiting."

Version two, from a man with admin shoes: cover-up. Casualty numbers. A second suspension coming.

"They always say second suspension," a pilot said. "First suspension pays better."

Version three, from a pilot who hadn't slept: archive adjacency felt wrong in his teeth. His copilot said shut up and fly it anyway.

"My teeth aren't wrong," the pilot said. "My teeth are early."

"Early isn't evidence."

"Early pays rent when you're the one flying."

Version four, from a mirror-tech runner: maintenance, obviously, but nobody wanted to hear it because maintenance didn't have a face you could hate.

"Maintenance has a brush," the runner said. "Try hating a brush."

Version five, from Dael—of course Dael—who looked tired today: "Fees moved before the story. Markets knew. Bulletin didn't."

Version six, from nobody in particular, repeated by everybody: resting. The gate was resting. Administrations rested gates the way people rested grudges—until they didn't.

A freight broker at the next table had his own version—version seven, if Lena were counting: "It's insurance. Somebody upstream bought quiet and sold it downstream as sabotage."

"That's not a version," someone said. "That's a business model."

"Same thing on the wall."

Someone at the far end shouted that Vesper would reopen by festival end. Someone else shouted back that festival end was a fiction administrations sold to children. A mirror-tech runner bet coffee on both outcomes and nobody took him seriously.

Lena set her cup down. "Which do you believe?"

Dael met her eyes. "I believe my sister wants me to stop asking and start shipping. I believe Vesper's still closed. I believe you're still here instead of on a long corridor, which means you don't believe any of us enough to leave."

"Reserved isn't committed."

"Not yet." Dael drank his coffee. "My sister says the basin boards look like the gate's resting. She also says resting is what administrations say before they say something worse. She's not a separatist. She's tired."

"Tired of what?"

"Of being told to wait politely while freight stacks up." He set the cup down. "You're not tired yet. You're still tracking. I can tell by the folder face."

"What folder face?"

"The one couriers get when they're building a route out of things that aren't routes." He almost smiled. "I had it once. Before I learned to ship through fear and complain later."

"You never learned. You just complain after."

"Fair." Dael's eyes flicked to her bag. "You still photographing marks?"

"Still filing what doesn't fit the wall."

"Then you're not ready to leave." He said it without triumph. "When you leave, you'll stop collecting and start betting. That's committed."

"Reserved isn't committed."

"Not yet."

The hangar noise rose around them—laughter at someone else's reroute disaster, someone saying Vesper would reopen by festival end, someone else saying it wouldn't reopen until someone named a body.

Lena finished her coffee. "If you hear anything that isn't a percentage—"

"I'll send it." Dael saluted with the cup. "Go look at a wall or something. You're scaring the tourists."

A mirror-tech runner at the next table was telling a new arrival about Vesper fees—how story tax hit Drift before Northhook, how around routes doubled when percentages crossed seventy. The new arrival looked sick. Dael caught Lena's eye and mouthed, *See?* without sympathy.

Lena didn't smile. She had a wall to look at.

---

Afternoon on the hangar's outer corridor, where sabotage threads didn't think to look.

She looked at a wall.

On the hangar's outer corridor a maintenance crew had finished a seam check an hour ago—she could tell by the paint sheen—and left a mark at shoulder height where nobody browsing sabotage threads would think to look.

Lena stood in front of it and pulled up Jun's brief on her display, the buried annex with mark refresh logs. The housing numbers didn't match Northhook's schedule. Different district, same grammar.

A keeper in grays passed, saw Lena looking, and slowed.

"You read that?" Lena asked.

"I wrote the schedule." The keeper's voice was flat. "I don't read gossip."

"Is it sabotage?"

"It's a check." The keeper kept walking. "Ask the wall if you want a villain."

Lena photographed the mark anyway. In the annex, Jun had buried keeper logs under bureaucratic numbering. On the wall, sabotage had a face. On the housing, work had a stroke and a dot.

She compared the mark photo to the pamphlet border in **STORIES**. Wrong map. Same grammar on the seam. The wall had a percentage for the map and none for the mark.

A hangar runner passed with a crate labeled ORINREACH—rerouted, stickered twice, proof the wall didn't need to be true to move boxes.

---

Fourteen hundred on the observation lip, and Jun was measuring what the wall had already decided.

Jun's public briefing was posted on the observation lip at fourteen hundred.

Lena climbed because she needed to know what Jun was telling the wall, and because altitude made lies easier to see.

The lip was crowded for a weekday—students, reporters, a pair of keepers in grays standing at the back as if they'd been assigned to witness measurement without participating in it.

The separatist attribution held at sixty-eight and climbing. The brief called it *likely explanatory frame for current public behavior.* Jun had learned to speak administrator without sounding human.

He was there in person, answering questions for students and a transit reporter with the calm of someone whose model had survived a second night.

The reporter asked whether the Bureau had a position on reopening. Jun said *not my department* with the calm of a man who'd practiced being disappointing. A student asked whether maintenance posts were being suppressed. Jun said *people choose stories* and Corin wrote faster.

Corin stood beside him with a tablet and the hungry look of someone still believing measurement was approval.

A student in the front row asked whether the Bureau could show the route clip again. Jun said, "We can show what was submitted. Submission isn't verification."

The student pressed: "But the timing—"

"Timing is a story people like." Jun's voice didn't change. "I measure liking. I don't issue seals."

A reporter at the edge asked whether the Bureau had reviewed the separatist pamphlets circulating at junction kiosks. Jun said, "We review submissions. Circulation isn't verification."

"So the border maps—"

"Are wrong on purpose or by mistake. Either way, they're maps, not routes." Jun gestured at the screen behind him. "People are rerouting on maps. I'm measuring that."

Corin wrote faster.

Lena waited until the small crowd thinned and Jun's shoulders dropped a fraction—exhaustion, not performance.

"You still think maintenance isn't catching," she said.

Jun glanced at her. "I think blame travels faster than work. The chart hasn't changed its mind."

"The chart doesn't have a brother."

"No." He said it without flinching. "The chart covers twelve gate towns and the Ribbon loop. Your brother is one person in one basin. I don't say that to wound you."

"I know how you say it." Lena kept her voice level. "You say it like he's small on purpose."

Jun was quiet a beat. "What would you prefer?"

"A reason. On a bulletin. From someone with a seal."

"I would prefer that too. I don't have a seal." He turned his tablet so she could see the green line—keeper posts, tolerance logs, mark schedules—pressing against the red mass. "This is what I have."

Lena looked at the chart. The green line wasn't zero. It just wasn't winning.

"If it's wrong," she said, "your model breaks."

"If it's wrong," Jun said, "I'll publish that too."

A student with a tablet asked Jun whether the Bureau recommended rerouting. Jun said, "The Bureau recommends measuring what people do, not what they should do."

Another student asked whether maintenance marks meant sabotage. Jun said, "It means someone checked a wall." Corin wrote faster.

A keeper at the back spoke up—first time Lena had heard one volunteer on the lip. "Marks aren't votes."

The crowd turned. Jun nodded. "Thank you. That's correct."

The keeper didn't look grateful. "Stop measuring us like we are."

Jun said, "Noted," and moved to the next question.

Corin caught Lena's eye on his way past—tired, impatient, still hungry. "If you find ground," he said quietly, "publish plain. Jun can't."

"I don't have a seal either."

"Then photograph seams." Corin was already walking. "Seams don't need seals."

Jun had heard that—Lena could tell by the way his shoulders tightened without turning. He said, quietly, to no one in particular, "Seams still need funding."

The keeper at the back answered, "Seams need brushes. Funding's your department."

Jun didn't argue. He wrote something on his tablet instead.

Lena left before she could ask a question she already knew he wouldn't answer.

---

Dusk on the lower path, and the festival made fear danceable.

At dusk the paired festival began—synchronized lights, shared music across the curve. People danced and argued about separatists at the same time.

Lena walked the lower path because the upper rail was full of students measuring fear. Meridian Arc bloomed overhead in twin colors. The lake held both festivals at once—lights in the water, lights in the sky, tourists trying to decide which celebration was real.

She almost believed the separatist story for half a block.

A vendor sold festival ribbons and, in the same breath, blamed separatists for Vesper. A couple argued about whether resting meant dead. A child cried because the reflection in the lake had too many lights and not enough stairs.

It fit. Sixty-eight percent had a face. The pamphlet's wrong border hadn't stopped anyone at the junction. Neither would the percentage.

Then she passed a housing seam with a fresh mark and remembered the keeper: *It's a check.*

Concrete pulled her back—a maintenance crew coiling hose at the seam, paint tin still open, apprentice wiping a brush on a rag that would never be clean again.

"You done?" the crew lead asked the apprentice.

"Done enough for rain."

"Done enough for the log."

The apprentice scratched the mark—dot, stroke—and capped the tin. No audience. No percentage. Just a seam that would take weather better tonight than yesterday.

"You need a witness?" the crew lead asked Lena, noticing her patch.

"Already got one." She tapped her bag. "Photo witness."

"Photos don't keep rain out."

"No. Brushes do."

The crew lead grunted approval and coiled hose. Lena walked on before she could turn the moment into a thesis.

A vendor at the next stall sold fried lake skewers and blamed separatists for the price of oil. A teenager in festival paint argued that resting meant the gate was dead and her friend argued resting meant the gate was napping and they were both loud enough to be wrong in stereo.

A woman in festival colors bumped Lena's shoulder and apologized without stopping, already back in an argument about separatists. A child broke away from a guide group to stamp a reflection in a shallow pool. The pool held Meridian Arc. The child held nothing but delight.

Lena checked Arin's queue at a kiosk near the lake path. Nothing new.

She stood at the railing where she'd met the pilgrim days ago. The tourist the pilgrim had watched had graduated to confident wrong turns. The lake hadn't changed. The bulletin had.

In the morning her berth would be two days from ending.

She stopped at the transit desk on the way back—not to file, to look. The long-route block still said **TRACKING**. Eleven days. Two fees. One corridor she trusted.

Not **COMMITTED**.

Not yet.

She walked back to Northhook with the festival at her back and the thread wall still climbing ahead.

At the transit ring she passed Jun and Corin heading down from the lip—Jun with his tablet, Corin with his hungry tired face. Corin nodded to Lena like they'd become allies without agreeing on anything.

"Morning release will be worse," Corin said quietly as he passed.

"For who?"

"For people who want a villain." He didn't stop walking. "Publish plain if you find ground."

Jun didn't look back. Lena didn't call after him. She had enough sanded verbs in **STORIES** already.

She slept badly and dreamed in percentages.
