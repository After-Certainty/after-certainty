# Chapter 2 — No One Has One

The upper offices of the south concourse had been built by people who believed a crisis would arrive in an orderly line. That was Lena's first thought when the lift doors opened on a hall that was narrow, low-ceilinged, and aggressively calm — blue tile, smoked glass down the left wall, public benches bolted so hard to the stone they might once have tried to leave.

Behind the glass the desks of Vesper South sorted the small griefs of travel into windows by category: missed parcels, overweight freight, berth disputes, family-board corrections, voucher transfers. None of the windows said **GATE HAS BECOME WALL.** That struck her as an oversight.

A woman with a sleeping infant was already arguing at Window Three. A line had formed under a sign that read **ROUTE CHANGES / REFUNDS / EXCEPTIONS,** and was busy arguing over which of the three words covered the end of the world. Lena went to Window One.

The clerk behind it had a narrow face, a badge worn nearly blank — **P. VANE,** the P almost gone — and the exhausted dignity of a man who had said the same sentence too many times and was beginning to hate everyone for needing to hear it again. Lena set the stolen notice panel flat on the counter. He looked at it, then at her courier rose, then — through the open door at the end of the hall — at the empty clip on the board, and made the small, wise decision not to ask.

"Courier," he said. "The bulletin is clear."

"No," Lena said. "The bulletin is short."

Someone two places back made a sound that might have been a laugh in a kinder room.

"Vesper crossing is suspended pending review," Vane said. "Non-active until the review flag clears."

"Who set the flag?"

"The regional authority."

"Which one?"

"The appropriate regional authority."

"That sentence didn't survive downstairs either."

His jaw tightened. He squared the stack of pink slips at his elbow — tap, tap, tap, a rhythm too even to be calm. "Courier, I understand you're concerned."

"I'm not concerned. I'm holding the first notice."

"Then you're holding station property."

"Then tell me who owns it."

He looked at the panel as if paper might bite: grey board, black type, a sector stamp heat-pressed into one corner. No signature. No office code past the broad sector mark. "It came through sealed," he said.

"From where?"

"The posting block. Which receives from the sector office. The Vesper sector office — not in this building, not in this station."

"Can you call it?"

"No."

"Can anyone?"

He hesitated, which was the first useful thing he'd done. "The line's down," he said, low. "To the sector office, the supervisor's desk, the gatehouse maintenance room, the corridor steward station. At least from here."

Lena leaned toward the speaker mesh; it smelled of metal polish and sour peppermint. "Who knows more than you?"

Vane glanced back at the office, where the clerks sat behind the smoked glass — one listening to a dead headset as if patience might restore the tone, an older woman with a silver loop through one ear murmuring to a station runner. "No one here."

"Then who?"

"The supervisor went down to the gantry terminal. Forty minutes ago. He hasn't reported back."

"Did anyone go after him?"

Vane's mouth opened and closed. "There's protocol. We don't send a second person into an unconfirmed gate fault until maintenance clears the first route."

"Maintenance is down."

"Yes."

"So protocol says wait for the man who may be trapped in the route you won't check — because protocol says wait."

Vane's face went blank in the way of a clerk who has reached the end of the form and found open air. "It says hold position."

Lena picked up the panel. "Where's his office?"

"Courier—"

"Vane."

He looked at her rose, then through the glass to the older clerk, who had stopped speaking to the runner and was watching him with an expression that said, very clearly, *do not make this mine.* Vane reached under the counter and pressed a latch. The side gate clicked open.

"End of the hall. Blue door. If anyone asks, you forced it."

"I didn't."

"I know," he said. "That's why I'm asking."

She liked him for that — not much, but enough — and stepped through. Behind her the room surged at the mesh — children on a Vesper routing, a paid-through voucher, *is this safety or audit* — and Vane raised both hands and said, "One at a time," which had never once worked and remained the first thing every clerk tried.

The administrative corridor smelled of old paper, warm wiring, and stone that had not seen rain in seven centuries. Message tubes and manual courier slots ran the walls in careful black script: **Vesper North, Tarn Run, Meridian Spur, Family Board, Cargo Exceptions, Review Holds.** The Review Holds slot had a brass mouth polished by generations of hands, and it was empty. Of course it was. Empty things had become popular today.

Set into the wall beside the slots was a manual override station — **GATE OPERATIONS** stencilled across a steel box, a pane of glass to break, a keyed handle behind it for someone to take a failing gate by hand. The glass was whole. The keyway behind it had been painted over in some smooth, prosperous year, a roller run flat across the lock and the warning placard alike, the same institutional blue as the wall, sealing the one hand that might have reached in as neatly as it sealed a scuff. No one had ever broken the glass. No one, in living memory, had believed there was anything behind it worth the breaking.

The blue door wore a nameplate — **SUPERVISOR EDRIN HALO · SOUTH CONCOURSE GATE OPERATIONS** — and was not locked, which was somehow worse. Inside, the office looked as though its owner had stepped out meaning to be back before his tea cooled, then forgotten what *back* meant. A skin had formed on the dark chicory in the cup. A jacket hung on the chair with one sleeve pulled inside out.

Lena shut the door. Without the public hall around it the missing gate-hum felt naked here too, under the tick of a wall clock and the soft thump of pneumatic tubes carrying other people's smaller troubles around the building. She set the panel on the desk and opened the logbook.

Edrin Halo had excellent handwriting: purple ink, block letters, a man who believed legibility was a public good. The morning's entries were boring enough to be beautiful.

> **09:23 — TARN RUNNER CHECKED THROUGH EMPTY, NO CARGO.**

She stopped. *Empty, no cargo.* It didn't mean no passengers, didn't mean safe, didn't mean Beska's aunt hadn't been aboard — but it was the first concrete Tarn thing she'd seen since the arch went dark, and concrete things had weight. She read on.

> **10:02 — SOUTH CONCOURSE LAMP FAULT. SENT MAINTENANCE, NO RESPONSE.**

The stuttering lamp — the two kids by the candy cart who'd watched it and not moved. *Not their shift,* she'd thought that morning. The log said otherwise.

> **10:41 — RETURNED CARGO, VESPER MARK, BAY FOUR. NO FORM ATTACHED. REQUESTED CLARIFICATION.**

Beska's crates.

> **10:53 — NO CLARIFICATION. HOLDING.**
> **11:07 — FAMILY BOARD DELAY, TARN ENTRIES NOT SYNCING. SENT QUERY.**
> **11:11 — REVIEW FLAG RECEIVED. VSP-SOUTH. SOURCE SEAL VALID.**

Below it the ink changed — not another entry, a line, dragged hard across the page and tearing through at the end, as if the pen had been pulled from his hand. No sign-off. No fault code. No note that he'd gone to the gantry. The clock on the wall had stopped at 11:12. She didn't like that either.

Under the desk sat the old manual relay box, its cracked safety cover broken and the red handle down — someone had snapped the copper safety wire pulling it, the bright threads splayed fresh against the dust. The relay was meant to wake the far terminal and shout across the line: *state your condition, answer if you're alive.* Lena pressed her scanner to the casing. Grey. The pull had gone nowhere. No one had answered because no one had heard — or because there was no longer anyone left to hear.

She looked back at the log. Returned cargo at 10:41. Tarn entries failing at 11:07. The flag at 11:11. The gate dark at 11:12. Not one thing — several, arriving close enough together that the mind wanted to braid them into a single cause, because a single cause would be merciful. But the page showed no hand, only gaps: requests, no clarification, no sync, a valid seal, an old relay that answered nothing.

The people downstairs would hate this more than they'd hate a villain. A cause can be cursed, a culprit named, a delay endured; this was only a chain of offices and seals, each passing along just enough absence to make the next person responsible for waiting. *No one has one,* she thought — and the system had gone on anyway, posting the notice, cutting the power, telling the room to wait, while every person inside it stared at their own square of blankness.

Someone knocked once and came in before she answered. Not Vane. A young man, twenty-five or so, thin enough that his station jacket looked assigned by a clerk who only knew the category *human.* Ink stained three fingers of his right hand; a receiver clipped to his collar blinked a green pattern that looked busy and meant nothing. He had a logbook under one arm and a paper cup in the other, and he stopped when he saw her crouched by the desk.

"Oh," he said. "You're not Edrin."

"Neither are you."

"No. I'm very consistently Jun."

"That a job?"

"Not officially."

"Then pick one and leave."

He looked at the broken relay, the open logbook, the stolen panel — and shut the door with his heel instead, crossing to the desk with the bright, hungry attention of someone who'd found a rare insect under a cup.

"You took the first posting," he said. "I wanted that."

"You were too slow."

"I was documenting spread." He opened his own logbook on the corner of the desk. The pages branched — names, arrows, phrases circled and crossed out: **TAX HOLD. SAFETY REVIEW. VSP SABOTAGE. MERCHANT STRIKE. PILOT LOCKOUT. TARN LOST.** Several rewritten with question marks.

"You're tracking rumors," Lena said.

"Variations. Sixteen people have said *tariff* in the last twenty minutes. Eleven said *safety review.* Four said *sabotage* — but sabotage is growing fastest, because it has the best teeth."

"This is what you do while the gate is dark."

"It's the only thing moving." That stopped her, and he saw it. "The lines are dead, the tickers are stale, the family board is lying by omission because it has no new truth to tell. But the stories are moving. Platform to concourse to market deck to freight berths. No working gate required."

He turned a page and tapped an entry circled twice. An hour ago, he said, he'd told a steward who asked that the family board was *not syncing — probably an indexing lag, provisional.* By the time it came back to him off the market deck every soft word was gone: a man was telling a crowd *Tarn entries not syncing, confirmed,* as a thing he plainly knew. Same observation. The *probably* filed off, the *provisional* gone, Jun's name nowhere in it. "That's the part I can't get used to," he said, and for a second the brightness went out of him. "They don't lie, mostly. They just can't hold a *maybe.* You hand them something unfinished and it comes back finished and hard — and then people act on the hard version." He said it like a man describing weather he was going to have to live in.

"Stories don't open gates," Lena said.

"No. But they decide who gets blamed while the gates stay shut." He said it plainly, no drama, and that was the first thing about him she liked. He'd already heard about Beska's aunt, he added — three versions: she'd missed the runner; the family board lost her; she'd come through and been taken upstairs, which wasn't true but would be believed by evening unless someone found a better story.

"What's a better story?" Lena asked.

"I don't know yet." He almost smiled. "I try honesty occasionally. It startles people."

The receiver clicked twice. They both looked at it; the green light blinked, steadied, blinked, and died. Something in his face went inward. "Local sub-hub," he said. "It didn't come back. It stopped pretending."

Down the corridor, someone shouted. Then another voice. Then several.

The waiting room had changed while she was in the office. It had been crowded before; now it had density — people pressed too close under the route boards and the feed-screens, the mild irritation of delayed travelers curdled into something thicker. A man in a green coat stood on a bench.

"It's the merchants!" he was shouting. "They locked Vesper to force the grain levy!"

"No — it's a safety seal, my cousin says the far platform cracked—"

"The family board dropped the Tarn names!"

That one went through the room like thrown metal.

Jun came up at her shoulder, logbook open. "Too fast," he said. "*Tarn lost* was a small branch. It shouldn't have reached the public hall yet."

"Unless someone pushed it."

"Or everyone was already afraid of it."

Behind the glass Vane had both hands up, the older clerk beside him, and no one was listening. People had stopped asking for information and started handing it to one another. That was worse — a room asking questions could still be answered.

"Can you stop it?" Lena asked.

"No."

"Slow it?"

"Maybe. Give them something more interesting than panic." He pushed past her into the room.

"Terrible idea," she said, and followed.

He climbed onto the edge of the baggage scale, which chimed in protest, put two fingers in his mouth, and whistled. That worked; the room turned. "Who heard Tarn is lost?" he called, and the room erupted — *my cousin, the board dropped, station crew said, names are gone.* Jun lifted both hands. "Good. Excellent. Very specific panic. Thank you." The confusion that followed wasn't calm, but it had some of the same furniture.

He pointed at a woman near the feed-screen. "You. Did you see names vanish, or did you see the board stop refreshing?"

"It said pending."

"Pending isn't lost. Pending is rude." A few people laughed despite themselves. He pointed at the man in green. "You said merchants. Which merchant? Which member of the board?"

"I don't—"

"Then it isn't a merchant yet. It's a feeling in a coat." His pen kept moving as he spoke, still collecting. "Here's what we know. Vesper is dark. The notice is useless. The family board isn't updating. The sub-hub just stopped pretending to know things. That's bad enough without improving on it."

"Who are you?" someone shouted.

"Jun."

"That means nothing."

"Most names do at first."

"Are we safe?"

He glanced at Lena — and she saw the temptation to answer past what he knew, and saw him refuse it. "I don't know," he said. The room hated that, then, slowly, respected it more than she expected. "No one in this room has an answer yet. Anyone selling you one is charging too much."

The woman with the infant had come up from downstairs. She held the child against her chest and looked at Lena. "What do we do, then?"

The room turned again — toward Lena this time, which she hated. She'd wanted an office, a room number, a person who'd signed a thing and could be made to explain it. Instead she had a dead relay, a missing supervisor, a rumor-collector on a baggage scale, and a roomful of people who'd just been told no one knew anything useful.

She set the panel on the counter where they could all see it. "This is the first posting. It says Vesper is suspended pending review. It doesn't say why, or who, or when. If anyone tells you more than that, ask where they got it." The room listened — not because she was inspiring, but because she had an object, and objects help. "Family board entries are delayed, and delayed is not lost. *No valid route* means the message can't find a path from here — not that there's nothing on the other side."

"That's supposed to make us feel better?" said the man in green.

"No. But worse lies are lining up to help you feel worse." She picked the panel back up. "The supervisor went to check the gantry and hasn't come back. I'm going to find out why."

"You?" said a cargo hand. "Why you?"

*Because my brother is behind the wall,* she thought. *Because the slate refused his name. Because if I stand still much longer I'll break something that doesn't deserve it.* "Because I'm already moving," she said. That satisfied some of them, not all.

Vane opened the side gate before she reached it, paler than before. "You can't go to the gantry alone. The access is sealed during a fault, and the key's with maintenance, in the lower spine office."

"Are they answering?"

"No."

"Then I need another way."

The older clerk with the silver loop stepped up beside him. "Talla," Vane said, half a warning. She ignored it, looking at Lena, then at Jun, then at the room pretending not to listen. "There's an old stair," she said. "Behind Records, down two levels. Comes out near the gantry throat — if the lower latch still opens. It sticks."

"How badly?"

"Historically."

"I'm coming," Jun said.

"You collect rumors."

"Variations. The 11:11 line, the returned crates, the Tarn board, the dead sub-hub — all of it was moving before the public bulletin. If we find the supervisor, he may know one of them. If he doesn't, what he doesn't know matters too."

She didn't want him to be right, which was becoming an unhelpful category. The woman with the infant said, "Take him. He made everyone admit what they didn't know." Jun looked pleased. "Don't," Lena told him, and he rearranged his face into something less pleased.

Vane passed her a ring of access tabs through the slot, his fingers unsteady. "These won't open the gantry. They'll get you to Records." She asked what the supervisor looked like. "Tall. Grey beard. Purple ink on his hands. Bad knee." He looked down. "He's my uncle."

That changed the shape of him. She took the tabs more carefully. "What do you call him?"

"Edd."

"All right," Lena said. "We'll look for Edd."

His mouth tightened hard, and for a second she thought he might cry, which would have been inconvenient for everyone. He didn't. "Courier — if he's—"

"I'll bring back what I know." It wasn't comfort. It was evidence. That would have to do.

Records sat behind a door that claimed to be locked and changed its mind at the third tab — drawers, dead posting panels, rolled maps, dust enough to make the air visible. The old stair was behind a rack of voucher forms sixty years dead, and Talla hadn't exaggerated: Lena put her shoulder to the latch twice before it gave with a shriek that brought grey dust sifting from the ceiling.

The air below was cold, not cool. A stone stair dropped into the dark, its rail worn smooth by emergencies no one upstairs remembered, the smell coming up it damp and old oil and something electric that had burned and gone quiet. Jun clipped a lamp to his collar and they went down. After the first turn the waiting room faded; after the second, even the office sounds were gone, and without the gate-hum the station felt old — not the romantic ancient of the commemorative prints, but old like a body that had carried too much for too long. Halfway down, Lena stopped: purple ink smeared the rail at hand height, where a hand had gripped hard enough to leave it, and on the next step lay a broken pen. She pocketed it.

At the bottom the latch opened onto a service passage behind the south gantry, emergency strips glowing dull green along the floor, the pipes overhead bundled thick as roots. The passage bent toward the local terminal, and something scraped. Then a voice, thin in the dark: "Don't pull it."

The terminal room was barely larger than a freight lift, built around a signal column that rose through floor and ceiling, its indicator glass black, the manual call-box hanging open with its wires bared. On the floor beside it sat Supervisor Edrin Halo — grey beard, purple fingers, one knee swollen against his trousers, one leg twisted under him — both hands wrapped around a cable bundle as thick as his wrist. He looked up with the irritated relief of a man whose bad day had finally produced witnesses.

"Don't pull it," he said again.

Lena crouched. "We won't."

"That's Edd?" Jun whispered.

Edrin's eyes moved to him. "Who are you?"

"Jun."

"That means nothing."

"I'm getting that a lot today."

"What happened?" Lena asked.

"Terminal went blank. I came down to check local response. Relay was dead. The manual line had a pulse, then it didn't, so I opened the box to test the old copper — and the line pulled back." He swallowed; his voice had gone dry. "It's under tension. From the far side. Or somewhere between."

"Can you fix it?"

"No." He said it without dignity, which made it worse. "I can keep it from retracting into the column until someone who knows this section gets here. That's what I'm doing."

"How long have you been holding it?"

"Don't know. Clock died."

"Since 11:12," Jun said, and Edrin closed his eyes.

"That explains my leg." The cable wasn't unlabeled, he added when she asked. It was labeled **Auxiliary Return / Legacy / Do Not Remove.** "Helpful," said Jun. "Very," said Edrin. "We have whole rooms full of helpful." No one knew where the other end went. Records might.

"Here's what I know," he said. "The review flag was valid. The seal checked. The system obeyed it. Then the gate dropped, the relay failed, and the auxiliary return started behaving like it had been cut loose from a place that shouldn't move."

"Sabotage?" Jun asked, and Lena looked at him sharply.

Edrin gave him the tired contempt of a man in pain. "Maybe. Maybe maintenance. Maybe a legacy line no one's mapped since our grandparents were children. Maybe some office moved a junction twenty years ago and updated every record but the one that matters." He looked at Lena. "Pick whichever answer helps you sleep. I don't have one."

*No one has one,* she thought again — but this time it didn't feel like emptiness. Edrin had no answer, but he had a cable in his hands. Jun had none, but he had a map of the stories. Vane had opened the gate; Talla had remembered the stair. And Lena had a stolen panel under her arm, a broken purple pen in her pocket, a brother behind silence, and a free hand. She wrapped it around the cable beside Edrin's.

"What are you doing?" he asked.

"Introducing reasons."

He laughed, which became a cough and then a grimace. "Good. Pull gently. — Jun. Go up, find Talla. Tell her we need every map in Records with the words *auxiliary, return, legacy,* or *do not remove* on it."

"That's a lot of maps."

"Then bring help," Lena said. "The kind with hands."

He stood, and at the door he stopped. "Should I tell them we found the answer?"

Lena looked at Edrin holding the cable, at the dead column, at the line that hadn't vanished only because one tired supervisor refused to let it go. "No," she said. "Tell them we found a thing to hold."

He nodded once and ran.

Lena shifted her grip. The cable was colder than she'd expected. Above them, through stone and office glass and a room full of people waiting for an answer, the Ribbon stayed quiet.

"You're the courier with the stolen panel," Edrin said.

"Yes."

"Good. If this gets worse, keep it."

"It already got worse."

"Then keep it harder."

She almost smiled. The cable twitched in her hand, barely, and on the other side of the wall — somewhere in the old dark of the system — something had moved, or failed, or tried to let go. She tightened her grip. She couldn't reach Arin. She couldn't open Vesper, or make the notice explain itself, or send a single word across the gap. But she could hold this line until another hand came.

For now, that was the work.
