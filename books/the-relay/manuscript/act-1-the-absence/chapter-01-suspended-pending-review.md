# Chapter 1 — Suspended Pending Review

A manifest never lies.

That was the first thing the Depot taught her, printed at the front of the manual they hand you on your first day. It was also the first thing the job taught her to stop believing. Manifests lied all day long — by leaving things out, by a wrong number, by some freight-hand a few worlds up the line swapping the cargo and counting on the distance to hide the switch. Everything she cleared went out through the Vesper gate to worlds that would take weeks to reach any other way, and anything you could send that far on nothing but a stamp was worth lying on a form to move. Lena had quit reading the logs for the truth years ago.

She read the seams instead — the welds, the seals, the way a crate was actually put together. Cargo couldn't lie the way paper could.

The freight deck was already loud with the morning: sled-wheels grinding over the grating, a route-call bouncing flat off the high roof, the smell of yeast and brake-dust and damp stone that hung over the Ribbon — the long transit spine of Vesper, built to be passed through and never lived in.

"You're pinching the corner," she said, not looking up from the loading sled's tally.

On the other side of the loading rail, Corin flinched and took his hand off the crate. She'd cleared his shipments before. He'd dressed down as a freight-hand, in the heavy oiled canvas the dock crews wore, but his hands were too soft for the work; whatever grease he'd rubbed under his nails, he'd never hauled a shift in his life. The cargo was his own, and he'd come to walk it through himself rather than hand it off to anyone. He'd been pressing the third crate's seal down with one thumb, as if he could hold shut what the weld hadn't.

"Adhesive's rubbish this season," he said. "The seal won't hold in this damp — you know how the Ribbon gets."

"The damp on the Ribbon hasn't changed since I was apprenticed." She crouched and ran a thumb along the seam — a three-millimeter lip where the polymer had overshot the weld. "And depot seal-tape doesn't do that. You tucked a second lining inside the shell after the customs stamp. Recent. The lip's still soft."

He didn't answer. She let the quiet do the work. His fingers kept twitching against the crate. His eyes kept going to the departure board. And tucked into his left cuff was the blue edge of a high-fare ticket — the kind that bought a fast crossing instead of a steerage berth, the kind a freight-hand didn't pay for out of a freight-hand's wage.

"It's seed-stock," he said, lower now. He smelled of chicory and the stale sweat of a man who'd been scared a while. "For the lower settlements out past Vesper. They've had no relief vessel from the inner worlds in three months — the regional office is sitting on the paperwork till the quarter closes, everyone covering their own backs. I'm just trying to get it through before the rotation locks the platforms. People have to plant something."

It was probably even half true. Good lies were built on a real grievance. But the crate ran nine kilos heavy for seed, and a man moving seed for hungry strangers did not also buy himself a blue ticket out of the sector.

"Lena, please." He'd read her rose — the courier's badge at her shoulder — or just her face. "The arch locks for the maintenance cycle in two hours, and the next courier through to the lower settlements is a week out. Just sign the stub. It's a routine hop." He meant the part about the settlements.

"The seed's real," Lena said. "And so's whatever's under it paying for your seat." She watched that land — not denial, just a man going still. "I don't need to know which. Get it on the sled."

He blinked. "You'll take it?"

"I'll take it." She thumbed a line into the tally. "Ten percent on the weight variance, logged as bulk adjustment. If customs opens it on the other side and finds what's under the seed, you've never met me."

He let out a breath he seemed to have been holding a long time. "Bless your eyes, courier."

"Don't." She closed the tally. "Get it down before the loaders dump it."

She watched him hump the crate to the chute, and she didn't feel good about it, and she didn't feel bad. In seven hundred years nobody had built a network this size that anyone could actually keep track of; it survived because everybody knew which bolts were loose and agreed, quietly, not to kick them. A skipped fee here, a second lining there, a number rounded toward whatever kept the line moving. The system was too big to notice nine kilos. She was small enough to care about them.

She set the blue ticket aside with the rest of the day's loose ends and went to do the one thing she came near Vesper to do and never quite managed: stand at the arch that pointed toward her brother, and decide, one more time, not to step through it.

---

The Vesper arch was a doorway of plain grey stone, and you walked through it onto another world. That was all it did, and it did it without drama — no flash, no lurch, no sense of falling. One step and you were on the south concourse at Vesper; the next, your foot came down on a floor a thousand worlds away. People braced for thunder their first time and got nothing but the quiet step and the strange new air; the children cried because their bodies had readied for something huge that never came. Everyone else stopped noticing by the second crossing. You had to; no one could get anything done standing stunned in front of a door that turned the distance between worlds into a single step.

The whole town had grown up around that one arch — freight decks, offices, berths, the queues and the route-calls and the noise, all of it the Ribbon.

Lena had stopped seeing the strangeness of it years ago. She still came and stood near it anyway.

A woman near the head of the queue was working herself up to bolt — one hand white on a travel case, the other gripping the sleeve of a man murmuring "it's nothing, it's nothing" in the exact voice people used when it was something. First crossing. Lena could spot one across a concourse.

"It won't feel like anything," she said, stepping in beside her. "That's the whole trick. Your body braces for a fall that never comes. Don't stop walking in the arch — that's the only part that matters."

"That sounds worse."

"It's worse the first time." The steward waved the next group on. "Go on. People worse than you have done it all morning."

"That isn't comfort."

"No," Lena said. "But it's true. Watch the next ones go through — they all come out the other side."

The woman went, flinching, and was gone — out the far side onto another world, safe, the way ten thousand people crossed before second sitting without once looking up. Lena watched the empty arch a moment, the way nobody else did anymore, and turned back to her own reasons for standing there.

She was not going to cross today. She never was. She was going to stand at the arch a minute, think about the Tarn, decide not to, and buy the peppermint drops their mother had rationed like medicine when they were small — Arin still couldn't be trusted around them, thirty-four years old and he'd eat the whole twist if no one stopped him. She'd send them on with whoever was actually making the Tarn run, with a tag that said almost nothing, because everything she wanted to say had never once fit on a courier-tag. *Saw these. Thought of you.* Or *next time.* She'd written *next time* so many times it had stopped being a promise and turned into the thing they said instead of meeting.

She bought the candies first. The vendor twisted the paper shut. Near his cart two maintenance kids sat on an upturned crate, sharing a strip of candied peel and watching an overhead lamp stutter out of rhythm, doing nothing about it. Not their lamp, then, or not their shift. Down at Bay Four, Beska — a dockhand she knew a little — was restacking the same three crates in the same wrong order, the way people do when they've had bad news and can't keep still.

"Your crates giving you trouble?" Lena said.

Beska looked up too slowly. "My aunt's on the Tarn run. Was due through last night. Didn't come." She said it to the crates. "No delay log. No keeper's name. Nothing in the corridor notes. My mother keeps asking did I check the family board." She nodded at the three crates under her hands, and Lena saw they wore the Vesper mark — not delayed, not rerouted. *Returned.* Cargo that came back before it went anywhere had forms, had a person swearing about forms. These had neither. "Before first sitting," Beska said. "No runner. They were just there."

Lena filed it with the morning's other wrong things — the soft seam, the stuttering lamp — but this one wouldn't leave her alone.

That was when the hum stopped.

She had never once heard it, which was how she knew it the instant it was gone — the low constant note the gate made, the sound everyone on the Ribbon had been hearing since the day they were born and had long since stopped hearing as a sound at all. It cut off all at once. And in the quiet after it, the doorway in the arch — that faint sense of another place just past the stone, a platform you could almost see — closed. Not slammed — it slid shut, smooth and complete, the way an eyelid does. The grey arch showed her its own back wall, plain damp stone with an old repair scar down one side, as if there had never been anywhere on the other side of it at all.

No siren. No red alert. A red alert meant an ordinary emergency — a fault in the gate's machinery, a breach, somebody sprinting down a gantry swearing into a headset. Red was *loud*, and loud meant someone was already fixing it. This had no sound at all. The indicator lamps over the arch went dark, one bank after another, the darkness traveling down the concourse as if something were moving along the line and putting them out.

For a moment nobody did anything. The station kept up its ordinary noise — shoes, route announcements for the gates that still worked, a child somewhere — over the hole where a sound used to be. The people nearest the arch turned toward it slowly, before they quite knew why.

Down the platform a ticker that had been clattering arrival times all morning ran down to nothing, its paper tape gone still. On a cart nearby a kettle kept boiling, absurdly, while its owner stood with the empty cup forgotten in his hand. The warmth in the floor began, very slightly, to go.

A steward came at a half-run with a printed panel in his hands. Young, Meridian-trained — you could see it in the careful way he moved — and frightened, and working hard not to show it. He slotted the panel into the board beside the dark arch and stepped back from it fast, as if it might be hot.

People drifted up to read it with the mild irritation of travelers whose train was late. That was the part Lena would remember — how badly the place wanted this to be ordinary, how hungry everyone was for it to be the kind of trouble you could complain about and wait out.

"Always something," a man said, comfortably, to no one.

"They'll sort it," said a woman with an infant asleep against her chest, and she and the man traded the small reassuring nod strangers give each other when they want to believe everything's under control. But the lights were out, and the hum was gone, and the nod was harder to give than it should have been.

The panel held one line, in the flat unspaced type of the regional office:

> **VESPER CROSSING — SUSPENDED PENDING REVIEW**

Lena read it twice, then a third time, because anger sometimes hid things. It hadn't. There was nothing to find. No who: it didn't say which authority, or whose hand. No why: it didn't say what had failed, or whether anything had — transit, signaling, the crossing itself, the right to ask. No when. No fault code, no posting office, no estimated restoration — not even the limp printed apology you always got, the one where some office too big to mean it was sorry for the inconvenience.

Three words and a stamp. A shrug with formatting.

It was a real object, at least. She could give it that. Someone had cut it, printed it, carried it down, and a frightened boy had pushed it into a slot. She reached up and worked the panel out of the board's clip and turned it over. The back was blank.

"Courier—" the steward started.

"Log that I took the first posting," she said. "My name's on the rose if anyone wants it." He looked relieved to be handed something to do.

She kept it. Notices like this got replaced inside the hour, once the offices upstream caught up and decided what story to tell — there'd be a fault code by evening, an apology, an estimated time, an office's name signed at the bottom. She wanted the first one. The honest one, honest only because no one had yet had time to improve it. It seemed suddenly important that one true thing survive the morning before anyone upstream decided what the truth would be.

Then she opened her slate. She had to know if she could still reach Arin, and there was only one way to find out.

Arin's last message sat where she'd left it six days ago, read half-asleep on the Hadrian berth and never answered, because answering properly took time and answering badly felt worse.

*Storms over the southern mirrors again. You'd laugh — tourists trying to navigate by the reflections. One man followed the lake-path twenty minutes before he worked out the stairs were above him.*

She typed under it. *Vesper is down. Are you all right?*

The slate searched for a route. A little wheel spun beside the message, the way it always did while it worked. It spun for a while.

**DELIVERY QUEUED — ROUTE UNAVAILABLE**

She tried again, routing the long way through Meridian. The slate refused before the wheel finished a single spin.

**NO VALID RELAY PATH TO DESTINATION**

She stood with the dead panel in one hand and the dead slate in the other. The Tarn was three slow-routes past Vesper if you were sensible, four if the weather pushed, five if the pilots got careful about risk — reachable, still, the long way, by ship and patience. But not from here, not now, not with a word. Arin would be at the canal lock at this hour, watching the water turn grey and rough under the north wind, his ledger open on his knee where he kept the lock's daily log — the water level, the weather, every boat through. He'd see the empty line where her arrival stamp went and think she'd taken a paying run down toward the rim. He'd think she was being smart, saving for the spring seed. He would not know the road between them hadn't closed. It had stopped existing.

She put her free hand flat against the stone where the doorway had been. Cool. Slightly gritty. Entirely a wall. No hum under it, no faint shimmer, no sense of a far platform waiting on the other side. No place for the message to go, no way to tell him not to take the skiff out if the mirrors were storming, no way to send the candies in her pocket, no way to say — stupidly, too late — *I was near enough to come, and didn't.*

All the things she'd ever put off until *next time* were suddenly just gone. She had always believed there would be a next time — Arin on the other side, the way to him always open, no plan or ticket required. Now there wasn't. Someone had suspended it pending review.

She kept her hand there until the cold reached the bone. Then she took it away.

"Some of us are just trying to get home." The man was broad, sweating at the temples, two children behind him, angry the way frightened people get when no one will give them someone to blame.

"So am I," Lena said.

"Then take Meridian around like the rest of us."

"Vesper goes to the Tarn."

"Nothing goes to the Tarn today." He heard it as he said it, and looked away. The words had landed harder than he meant — exactly where it hurt.

Around her the concourse was settling in to wait — someone asking whether vouchers carried through Meridian, someone asking if *review* meant safety or audit, the woman with the infant beginning to hum without knowing it. *They'll sort it*, the place kept telling itself, in a dozen small voices, and Lena watched that faith wear thinner each time someone said it. Nobody had said who *they* were. Everyone here had spent a lifetime trusting that someone, somewhere, was in charge of all this — a *they* nobody had ever had to name. The first time that someone failed to answer, no one knew which door to knock on. The young steward stood at the edge of it with nothing left to hand anyone.

Lena stepped into his path. "Who posted it?"

He looked at the courier rose on her shoulder, then at the board, hoping one of them would let him off. "Regional authority."

"Which one?"

"The appropriate one."

"That sentence has never survived a specific question." When he only swallowed, she softened it a degree. "Where'd it come from? Which office?"

"It came down sealed," he said, low, almost honest. "I just put it up. I don't know who sealed it." His eyes went, without his meaning them to, up toward the smoked glass of the upper offices.

That was enough to begin.

She put the candies back in the inner pocket of her bag, beside the slate that couldn't reach her brother, and slid the printed panel under her arm where it wouldn't bend. Up behind the smoked glass was an office that had taken a sealed notice from another office and handed it down without opening it. Somewhere above that was the one who had decided three words would do — or who had passed the decision along rather than make it. She wasn't going to get a name easily, and she was beginning to see why. No single villain had shut the door. Just a long line of hands — a last one that had passed it down without asking, and a first one, somewhere up the line, that wouldn't put its name to what it had done.

She had cargo she shouldn't have taken, a returned crate nobody logged, a stuttering lamp, a dockhand whose aunt was due through last night and never came, a frightened boy with a stack of panels, and a brother behind stone. Every piece of it should have gotten where it was going. None of it had.

Lena turned away from the dark arch and started up toward the offices. She didn't know what had happened to Vesper. She didn't know whether Arin was safe.

But she knew how to begin. Find who touched it last. Find who wouldn't sign it. Find who decided three words and a stamp were enough to hold a door shut.

Find the person who had written the notice.
