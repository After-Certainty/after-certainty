# Chapter 19 — Housing 7-C

Cai had been on the scaffold forty meters from the corridor housing when the administrator called him back for the third time that week.

Not because something failed.

Because nothing failed loudly enough to count.

"The junction reads green," Cai said into his comm, not looking down at the woman on the screen. Green was a lie you told when the numbers you were allowed to publish were the numbers someone wanted to hear.

"The junction is green," the administrator agreed. "We need visibility. Public confidence."

"Public confidence is not a housing seal."

"Cai."

He sighed without sound—a habit keepers learned so clients wouldn't think you were difficult. Difficult keepers didn't get recalled. Difficult keepers got replaced by vendors who sold confidence in quarterly slides.

He clipped his harness and rode the scaffold down toward the approach infrastructure—the unglamorous bones: pressure manifolds, alignment guides, the places tourists never photographed because they didn't arc dramatically against the sky.

The First Gate itself occupied the space where a moon ought to have been. Ships crossed its aperture like insects through a cathedral. Every surface gleamed with evidence of continuous repair.

Cai worked the parts that made gleam possible.

Before first light he'd already walked intake two—gauge, dot, photograph—because night shift ended at shift change, not at convenience. The corridor smelled of solvent and warmed metal and the faint ozone tang that meant freight had passed recently. Tourist decks above him sold reflection cards; below his boots the approach bones carried weight nobody priced in tickets.

---

His official title was maintenance keeper, which sounded medieval and fit.

His unofficial title, from pilots who still remembered names, was *the dot man*—for the marks he left at checked sites.

Dot and stroke. Simple. Not graffiti—language.

Keepers had used variants for longer than Cai had been alive. Longer than most administrators admitted, because admitting meant admitting someone had to learn the language.

He found yesterday's mark on the approach manifold scratched by a vendor's tool slip—not malice, stupidity—and refreshed it with his stylus: dot, stroke, date code in a shorthand only keepers and a few pilots read.

Photograph. Log. Upload to an archive almost nobody opened.

The courier had asked him yesterday why nobody taught the grammar publicly. He hadn't had a good answer except *politics*, which wasn't an answer, only a weather report.

---

Housing 7-C sat in the shadow of a freight guide rail where the approach corridor bent toward crew intake.

Green board on the wall: **WITHIN TOLERANCE**.

Cai trusted the hum more than the board and the gauge more than the hum.

Lio was already at the housing when Cai arrived—twenty-two, fast hands, still young enough to want villains in the news ribbon. He'd clipped in wrong on the first try and corrected without embarrassment, which was why Cai kept him.

"Vendor patch from last month," Lio said, pointing at the left flange. "Thick."

"Thick," Cai agreed.

"Green says—"

"Green says what it says." Cai ran the gauge. The seam read inside published tolerance by two microns that meant nothing until thermal load shifted. "Keeper says rework."

Lio leaned closer. "That's not vendor error on the board."

"That's keeper catch before error becomes headline." Cai photographed the seam, tagged the flag, opened the maintenance log. "You write the note. Full sentence. No acronyms. If a supervisor reads it, they can't pretend they didn't."

Lio typed, tongue at the corner of his mouth. "They'll still pretend."

"Archive won't." Cai meant it as comfort and heard how thin it sounded.

He cut the vendor patch himself because rework requests sat in queues while green boards smiled. The seam underneath was clean—vendor competence wasn't the issue; box-checking was. You could meet published tolerance and still build a lie proud enough to catch load wrong.

Lio held the light. "That's not in the vendor contract."

"That's in the keeper contract." Cai measured the replacement patch thickness twice. "Write it down. Photograph the before. Photograph the after. If they recall me for slowing intake, the archive will have sentences."

---

Another mark on the housing exterior stopped him—a dot with a stroke angled five degrees off his family's usual pitch. Same code family. Different hand. Night shift, maybe. Or the keeper on corridor four Cai had never met but had been reading for months.

He added his own mark beside it, not overwriting.

*I see you seeing.*

The grammar of the profession. Evidence that attention was distributed, not heroic.

Lio touched the older mark with a gloved fingertip. "That's not yours."

"That's the job." Cai made his fresh dot. "If you're the only one checking, you're already losing."

The boy—man, almost—looked toward the aperture where another hauler slid through with obscene ease. "Courier asked me if Vesper was sabotage."

"What'd you say?"

"I said you said signature asymmetry would show in adjacent corridors."

"And?"

"She said the thread wall doesn't read adjacent corridors." Lio paused. "I said I trust you. She didn't laugh."

Cai logged the housing, flagged the patch, closed the panel. "Couriers trust routes. Keepers trust seals. Analysts trust clusters." He started toward the next housing on the list. "Nobody trusts the whole thing. That's why marks exist."

---

The Toma query chain had grown legs overnight.

Cai's display held three new forwards—archive moon to relay guild remnant to supervisor inbox to *marked read, no reply*. The original request sat at the bottom like a seed nobody wanted to water:

**HISTORICAL KEEPER ROSTERS — RELAY GUILDS — URGENT QUERY FROM STATION ARCHIVIST TOMA.**

The courier had delivered page one in person. Toma had delivered attrition in ink. Cai delivered seals in person and logs into voids.

He forwarded the chain again with a note: **Roster names match unfilled shifts on approach corridor. Historical may explain current. Recommend crosswalk before next budget cycle.**

Supervisor would ignore it.

Archive might not.

That was the whole faith left in the system—that somewhere a patient person with citation habits would outlast a frightened quarter.

His supervisor pinged before lunch: **KEEP STATEMENTS OFF PUBLIC THREADS**.

Cai typed back: **THEN PUBLISH TOLERANCES**.

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. No further message.

Politics was his blind spot. He knew that. He kept checking manifolds anyway.

---

At lunch—protein bar, scaffold lip, view of ships threading the aperture—Lio pointed at the news ribbon.

**VESPER GATE SUSPENDED — DAY 47 — NO REASON CODE**

**SEPARATIST SABOTAGE — EIGHTY-ONE PERCENT**

"People saying sabotage," Lio said.

"People say things."

"You don't think—"

Cai chewed, watched a freight hauler slide through the aperture with the ease of a system that still worked on the surface. "If it were sabotage, we'd see signature asymmetry in adjacent corridors. We don't."

Lio leaned forward. "So what is it?"

Cai wiped his gloves. "It's what happens when everyone assumes someone else is checking."

Heavy for lunch.

Lio was young enough to still want villains. Cai had been young once too. Villains were easier than erosion.

He showed Lio the housing 7-C photograph instead—the seal pattern, the wear line, the vendor patch applied too thick.

"See that?"

"Vendor error."

"Keeper catch," Cai said. "Before error becomes headline."

Lio touched the image of the fresh mark on screen. "So Vesper's not…"

"Vesper's a corridor. Throats have keepers. Keepers have rosters. Rosters have budgets." Cai stood. "Ask me again when the budget meeting cares about seals."

---

Afternoon was scaffold three—alignment guides, unglamorous, essential.

Cai clipped in and rode the lift with the hum in his teeth. Forty meters from the corridor housing the air changed pressure—not failure, presence. The aperture worked. That was the terror and the job.

Lio met him on the platform with a relay from intake four: vendor team requesting expedited clearance on a patch Cai had flagged.

"Expedited clearance," Cai read.

"They say green board—"

"Green board." Cai hooked his gauge. "Write denial. Full sentences. Copy archive."

"They'll complain to the administrator."

"Good." He ran the alignment guide seam. Clean. Dot anyway. "Complaint is louder than logs. Maybe louder helps."

Below them a tourist deck erupted in flash photography—proof of visitation, not proof of maintenance. Cai had learned not to resent tourists. They funded the structure that let him climb. He resented the quarterly slides that pretended funding was the same as checking.

---

The viewport deck at shift end was where the cast of the civilization showed itself without introduction.

Cai stood where tourists took pictures and keepers took measurements. The First Gate filled the frame—ancient, maintained, stranger than ruins because ruins didn't hum.

Ships passed.

Worlds passed through.

Somewhere Vesper Gate stayed silent—not broken on the surface, quiet in the way a corridor got quiet when coordination failed upstream and nobody published the no official word because no official word required someone to own the sentence.

Somewhere a mirror crew saved knees on Orinreach Basin.

Somewhere a pilot named Nadi flew fringe corridors because fees demanded it and margins required nerve.

Somewhere a pilgrim taught a community to see checking—not gates, junctions, the small honest places.

Somewhere a courier reserved a long route for a brother who'd stopped answering through the short corridor.

Somewhere an archivist struck names through on page one and called it slope.

Cai touched the viewport glass, mark-stained glove leaving a faint smudge he wiped away.

Fourteen marks today. Two vendor flags. One argument with an administrator. Zero catastrophes.

Zero catastrophes was the job.

Until it wasn't.

---

The administrator's final message of the day was not a call but a memo—**VISIBILITY REQUEST DENIED — KEEPER STATEMENT NOT APPROVED FOR PUBLIC DISTRIBUTION**.

Cai read it on the descent lift and felt the quiet anger keepers got when they could name exactly which seal nobody wanted to hear about.

The hum followed him down—steady in the deck plates, steady in his teeth.

He trusted it.

He did not trust what trust meant anymore.

Hum was not proof. Hum was habit. Habits were what institutions shed when nobody was looking.

Tomorrow he would climb again. Tomorrow budgets would still be politics. Tomorrow someone would assume someone else was handling it.

Tonight the gate hummed in a key he knew by bone, and knowing frightened him more than a bad reading would have.

He went home angry in a quiet way, logged fourteen marks, forwarded Toma's query one more time, and slept thin.

In the morning the hum would still be there.

He wasn't sure that was enough anymore.

---

Before sleep he opened the archive upload queue one more time—fourteen photographs, two flags, one crosswalk thank-you from a moon he'd never visited.

The hum sat in the walls of his apartment the way it sat in his teeth.

Habit.

Not proof.

He set the alarm anyway.

---

Morning brought the administrator's weekly digest—**VISIBILITY METRICS UP**—and a freight delay on intake four that made the vendor complaint real.

Cai read both, clipped in, and rode the scaffold toward housing 7-C.

Green board. Gauge. Keeper.

The job continued whether or not the quarter had learned grammar.

Lio met him at the platform with coffee and a question. "Courier's briefing today. Jun. You going?"

"No."

"You going to be there anyway when they ask about seals?"

Cai took the coffee. "I'll be on scaffold three when they ask. That's the same answer."

Lio nodded like someone storing sentences for later. "Housing 7-C first?"

"Housing 7-C first." Cai hooked his gauge. "Always housing 7-C first."

Below them the aperture swallowed another hauler with obscene ease.

Zero catastrophes was the job.

Until it wasn't.

Cai logged the morning's first dot before the safety AI reminded him breakfast was a roster requirement. Lio laughed once—surprised, young—and wrote the note in full sentences.

Archive might open someday.

Supervisor might read someday.

The hum held today.

That was the bargain.

Somewhere on the approach corridor another keeper added a mark at a different angle—*I see you seeing*—without knowing Cai had taught an apprentice the same grammar before breakfast.

The ecosystem persisted.

Cai climbed.
