# Chapter 02 — Southern Mirrors

Arin had stopped trusting tourists on the third day of the storm cycle, which was unfair because the storm wasn't their fault.

It was his.

Not the weather itself—that belonged to Kestrel Span's southern basin, the way weather belonged to any place that had learned to be specific. But the mirrors were his shift, his responsibility, his name on the roster when the coordinator signed the weekly sheet nobody outside the department read.

The mirrors were supposed to make reflection navigation honest.

Tourists treated them like a game.

Arin stood on the lower catwalk with rain needling the back of his neck and watched a couple from the inner districts argue about which reflection was "the real path" while their children ran ahead toward a slick patch he'd flagged twice.

"You flagged it," his assistant Miko said. "They don't read flags."

"I know they don't read flags."

"Do you want the speech or the ladder?"

"The ladder."

Miko went for the ladder. Arin went for the children, which was the job in the way the roster never quite captured—the part that wasn't maintenance and wasn't policy and wasn't the elegant theory of navigation his instructors had drawn on boards.

The part that was grabbing a sleeve before a knee hit stone.

The girl looked up at him, annoyed and alive. "We saw it in the water."

"I know."

"The water said go."

"The water says a lot of things." He guided them back toward their parents, who had finally noticed absence. "Storms lie. That's why we have mirrors."

The father opened his mouth to argue. Arin had heard the argument before: *if the mirrors are honest, why do locals use them differently?* Because locals had learned the basin the way you learned a sibling's moods—not by rules, by repetition.

He didn't say that. He said, "The upper path closes in twenty minutes. Take the east stair. It's uglier. It's dry."

They went, offended and safe.

Miko returned with the ladder and a look that meant *you're doing the speech in your head again*.

"I'm not doing the speech."

"You always do the speech after you save knees."

Arin took the ladder. Above them, the southern mirrors caught a break in the cloud and threw Kestrel Span's lower towers into doubled light—real stone and reflected stone, the city's particular trick of making you feel both grounded and duplicated.

He loved this place in the way you loved something you'd never left long enough to mythologize.

Lena had left. Lena always left. That wasn't cruelty; it was her breathing.

She moved between worlds the way he moved between catwalk levels—naturally, without thinking, as if flat maps were a moral choice.

He moved because the mirrors needed tending and the basin needed honesty and someone had to remember that storms were not metaphors.

---

At shift end he sat in the break room with wet cuffs and a cup of tea that tasted like the building.

His display held two messages.

The first was from Lena, delivered hours ago while he was on the catwalk.

*I saw your message. I'm still on the Ribbon—six days on the berth. Kestrel Gate suspended, no reason given. Are you all right?*

*I'll find a way around if I need to. Don't do anything stupid in the mirrors.*

He read it twice.

Gate suspension was already on the local boards—not Kestrel Gate, he thought at first, heart wrong for a second—but the notice was there, clean and reasonless, the same shape of silence Lena hated.

He checked the route maps.

Kestrel Gate: suspended.

The long throats still open. The fees still predatory. The world still reachable if you had time and money and patience.

Lena had two of those on any given week.

He typed back with hands still smelling like rain.

*I'm fine. Storm cycle, busy mirrors, tourists attempting suicide by reflection. Gate news is weird—administration says wait. Don't come the long way on my account. Eat something on that berth.*

He paused, thumb hovering.

Lena would hear *don't come* as challenge.

He added: *The southern path you like is closed for maintenance anyway. Not because of gate. Because I said so.*

That was almost a joke. It was also true.

He sent it.

The second message was from his coordinator, marked low priority and therefore urgent in the way low priority messages always were.

**Budget review — mirror crew — respond by end of week.**

Arin closed it without responding.

Budget reviews were the weather of institutions—predictable, impersonal, capable of flooding basins if you ignored them long enough.

Miko leaned in the doorway. "Gate thing's got people talking."

"I noticed."

"Separatists."

"Of course."

"You don't believe that."

Arin dried his hands on a towel that had seen better decades. "I believe people want a villain because villains can be argued with. Silence can't."

Miko considered this with the seriousness of someone young enough to still be surprised by adulthood. "So what do you believe?"

"I believe the mirrors are honest and tourists are not." He stood. "And I believe my sister is going to do something inconvenient because she loves me."

"That's a belief system."

"It's a family."

---

He walked home the long way along the basin rim because the short way passed the administration screens, and the screens tonight would be full of clean notices and invented dirt.

Kestrel Span at evening was a place of depths.

Not cosmic depths—Arin left that language to people who'd never scraped algae off a mirror housing. Human depths: the baker who saved the heel of the loaf for Miko because she'd helped his grandson with a stuck kite; the old woman on the east stair who taught upside-down geography to grandchildren who would grow up thinking other worlds were abstract.

Other worlds were not abstract.

They were fees and throats and siblings who sent pictures of lakes that existed twice.

His apartment overlooked the basin's quiet side, where reflection navigation was not a sport but a commute. He cooked rice badly, answered a neighbor's question about storm shutters, and sat by the window with the bowl and the view.

In the water below, the city's lights began their doubled life.

He thought about Lena on the Ribbon, six days on a berth, blocking routes in her ledger.

He knew that habit. *Reserved* meant she was already moving toward him in her mind.

He should tell her not to.

He should tell her to stay safe and let the gate reopen and trust silence to resolve into competence.

He didn't believe that tonight.

He believed storms would continue. He believed budget reviews would continue. He believed tourists would continue needing knees saved and mirrors would continue needing hands.

He believed the gate suspension was a shape he didn't recognize yet—not sabotage, not drama, something flatter and worse: the world adjusting to an absence nobody had language for.

On the catwalk earlier he'd noticed a maintenance mark on the east housing—dot and stroke, keeper code he'd learned years ago and rarely saw anymore because keepers were thin on Kestrel Span and thinner everywhere else.

He'd refreshed the mark himself with a tool from his belt, not because it was his job officially, but because the symbol meant *someone checked*.

Someone checked.

That had to be enough until the checking became a story people told instead of a person they paid.

His display chimed.

Lena again, already.

*Reserved doesn't mean committed. Tell me what administration actually said. Not the boards—the people.*

Arin smiled into the rice.

There she was—breadth, pattern, the question under the question.

He typed slowly so it would sound like him and not a bulletin.

*People said wait. People said it's happened before. People said don't scare tourists. Nobody said why. Storm's lifting tomorrow. Mirrors will lie differently. I'm here.*

He almost added *I miss you* and didn't, because siblings on Kestrel Span and the Ribbon had languages they'd built out of weather and routes and stubbornness, and the sentence would land wrong without her shoulder in the same room.

Instead he sent a picture—the basin at dusk, doubled lights, the east housing with its fresh mark visible if you knew where to look.

She would know where to look.

She always did eventually.

That was her breadth. His depth. The conversation they'd been having since they were children, one of them always in motion, one of them always in place, both of them trying to explain a world too large to see whole.

Outside, the storm eased. The mirrors held.

Arin washed the bowl and went to bed early because tomorrow the tourists would return and the budget review would still be waiting and the gate would still be suspended and his sister would still be moving toward him in her mind.

He would keep the basin honest.

It was not saving civilization.

It was participation.

It was enough to build a life on until the larger silence found its words—or didn't, and people had to act anyway.
