# Chapter 05 — First Approach

Cai had been on the scaffold forty meters from the throat housing when the administrator called him back for the third time that week.

Not because something failed.

Because nothing failed loudly enough to count.

"The junction reads green," Cai said into his comm, not looking down at the woman on the screen. Green was a lie you told when the numbers you were allowed to publish were the numbers someone wanted to hear.

"The junction is green," the administrator agreed. "We need visibility. Public confidence."

"Public confidence is not a housing seal."

"Cai."

He sighed without sound—a habit keepers learned so clients wouldn't think you were difficult. Difficult keepers didn't get recalled. Difficult keepers got replaced by vendors who sold confidence in quarterly slides.

He clipped his harness and rode the scaffold down toward the First Gate's approach infrastructure—the unglamorous bones: pressure manifolds, alignment guides, the places tourists never photographed because they didn't arc dramatically against the sky.

The First Gate itself occupied the space where a moon ought to have been. Ships crossed its aperture like insects through a cathedral. Every surface gleamed with evidence of continuous repair.

Cai worked the parts that made gleam possible.

---

His official title was maintenance keeper, which sounded medieval and fit.

His unofficial title, from pilots who still remembered names, was *the dot man*—for the marks he left at checked sites.

Dot and stroke. Simple. Not graffiti—language.

Keepers had used variants for longer than Cai had been alive. Longer than most administrators admitted, because admitting meant admitting someone had to learn the language.

He found yesterday's mark on the approach manifold scratched by a vendor's tool slip—not malice, stupidity—and refreshed it with his stylus: dot, stroke, date code in a shorthand only keepers and a few pilots read.

Photograph. Log. Upload to an archive almost nobody opened.

Participation.

---

The First Gate hummed at a frequency you felt in teeth more than ears.

Cai trusted the hum.

He did not trust the funding sheet.

Three positions cut from the keeper roster last quarter. Two vendor contracts added. Vendors checked boxes. Keepers checked truth.

He'd tried to explain the difference once at a coordination meeting.

The room had nodded and funded vendors again because vendors came with slides.

Politics was his blind spot. He knew that. He kept checking manifolds anyway.

---

At lunch—protein bar, scaffold lip, view of ships threading the aperture—his apprentice Lio pointed at the news ribbon.

**KESTREL GATE SUSPENDED — DAY 9 — NO REASON CODE**

"People saying sabotage," Lio said.

"People say things."

"You don't think—"

Cai chewed, watched a freight hauler slide through the aperture with the ease of a system that still worked on the surface. "If it were sabotage, we'd see signature asymmetry in adjacent throats. We don't."

Lio leaned forward. "So what is it?"

Cai wiped his gloves. "It's what happens when everyone assumes someone else is checking."

Heavy for lunch.

Lio was young enough to still want villains. Cai had been young once too. Villains were easier than erosion.

He showed Lio the manifold housing instead—the seal pattern, the wear line, the place last year's vendor patch had been applied too thick.

"See that?"

"Vendor error."

"Keeper catch," Cai said. "Before error becomes headline."

Lio touched the fresh mark Cai had cut. "So Kestrel's not…"

"Kestrel's a throat. Throats have keepers. Keepers have rosters. Rosters have budgets." Cai stood. "Ask me again when the budget meeting cares about seals."

---

The administrator called again at shift end.

"We need a statement."

"No."

"We need—"

"The gate approach reads within tolerance. The throat reads within tolerance. I refreshed fourteen marks today. I flagged two vendor patches for rework. That's my statement."

Silence on the comm, the administrative kind that meant *you are inconvenient*.

"Cai, people are frightened."

"People are frightened when silence replaces checking." He paused, surprised at his own sentence. "Publish the tolerances. Publish the mark schedule. Tell them someone is here."

"That's not reassuring language."

"It's true language."

He ended the call and immediately felt tired.

Lio handed him water. "That was almost politics."

"Don't spread rumors."

---

Walking the approach corridor at dusk, Cai counted marks—his, others', some old enough to wear soft at the edges.

Keepers leaving evidence.

An ecosystem of attention so easy to forget because it worked quietly.

At housing 7-C he found a mark he hadn't cut—same code family, different pressure angle.

Another keeper, still working.

His chest loosened a fraction he didn't show.

Not alone then. Not yet.

He added his own mark beside the other, not overwriting—*I see you seeing*—the grammar of the profession.

His display held a message from the archive moon he'd never visited, auto-forwarded through a chain he didn't fully understand: **REQUEST — HISTORICAL KEEPER ROSTERS — RELAY GUILDS — URGENT QUERY FROM STATION ARCHIVIST TOMA.**

Someone looking backward.

Cai looked forward—at seals, at budgets, at the aperture's steady hum.

He forwarded the query to his supervisor with a note: **Historical rosters may show names we've stopped paying.**

Supervisor would ignore it.

Archive might not.

Cai logged fourteen marks, two vendor flags, one argument with an administrator, zero catastrophes.

Zero catastrophes was the job.

Until it wasn't.

---

He stood at the viewport where tourists took pictures and keepers took measurements.

The First Gate filled the frame—ancient, maintained, stranger than ruins because ruins didn't hum.

Ships passed.

Worlds passed through.

Somewhere Kestrel Gate stayed silent.

Somewhere a mirror crew saved knees.

Somewhere a pilot flew fringe corridors because fees demanded it.

Somewhere a pilgrim taught a community to see checking.

Somewhere a courier reserved a long route for a brother who stayed.

Cai touched the viewport glass, mark-stained glove leaving a faint smudge he wiped away.

Participation, he thought—not certainty.

Tomorrow he would climb again.

Tomorrow budgets would still be politics.

Tomorrow someone would assume someone else was handling it.

He would handle what was in front of him until the handling became visible enough to matter—or until the silence grew loud enough that marks alone couldn't hold it.

Both outcomes were possible.

Only one involved his continuing to show up.

He chose show up.

The gate hummed.

Cai went home to sleep the thin sleep of people who know where the seals are—and know, too, how few people want that knowledge until the hum changes key.
