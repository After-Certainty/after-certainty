# Chapter 01 — Lake Orin

Lena had learned to read the Ribbon the way other people read weather.

Not the forecasts—the actual sky, or what passed for sky inside a structure that curved back on itself. She watched how light moved across Lake Orin at different hours, how the reflected district overhead sharpened or went vague, how tourists stood on the lakeshore and turned in slow circles trying to decide which city was real.

The locals didn't turn. They glanced up once, matched the angle of the towers in the water below to the towers in the water above, and kept walking.

Lena was not quite a local. She had a berth at Northhook Transit for another six days, a manifest half-checked, and a habit of standing on this particular railing when she needed to think without being asked what she was thinking about.

The far shore of Lake Orin wasn't a shore at all. It was Meridian Arc, forty kilometers away by the map and considerably less by the curve, suspended overhead so cleanly that on clear mornings you could count windows in the reflection and windows in the sky and still not be sure which set belonged to the world you were standing in.

Today the air smelled like rain that hadn't decided where to fall. A line of maintenance kites drifted between the habitat mirrors to the east, silver wings catching light from a star thirty light-years away. Lena didn't know the engineering. She knew they appeared before storm season and vanished after, and that children on the lakeshore pointed at them the way children everywhere pointed at things adults had stopped seeing.

She'd been paid to see things adults had stopped seeing.

That was the job, or the part of the job she was good at.

Her courier bag hung against her hip, heavier than it needed to be because she'd taken a private packet for a mirror-tech firm that paid well and asked few questions. The patch on her shoulder—the white compass rose—had faded at the edges. She kept meaning to replace it. She kept not replacing it, the way she kept not settling anywhere long enough for the patch to matter.

A message from Arin sat in her queue, six days old, marked read but not answered.

She'd meant to answer. She'd opened it at a gate throat on Kestrel Span two weeks ago, laughed once, and told herself she'd write from the Ribbon where she had time and decent relay bandwidth.

*Storms over the southern mirrors again. You'd laugh if you saw the tourists trying to navigate by reflection.*

That was Arin. One world, known completely. He sent weather and tourists and the small sharp pleasures of a place held in the hand.

Lena sent distances.

She pulled up the message again on her wrist display, not to read it—she knew it by heart—but to check the timestamp against the transit bulletin she'd been trying not to think about.

Six days before suspension.

Arin had written from Kestrel Span as if the world were only weather and bad navigation jokes. No mention of gates. No mention of the southern route she usually took when she needed to reach him without detouring through the Markets.

She closed the message and opened the bulletin instead.

---

The suspension notice had arrived forty minutes ago, while she was arguing with a dock clerk about manifest weights.

**KESTREL GATE — TRANSIT SUSPENDED UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE.**

No reason code. No estimated reopening. No secondary contact.

In seven years of courier work, Lena had seen gates slow, gates queue, gates reroute around maintenance that everyone pretended wasn't maintenance. She had seen a gate hiccup once on the far side of the Markets and watched three administrations issue explanations within the hour, each more confident than the last, two of them contradicting the third.

Explanations arrived faster than solutions. That was one of the rules.

This time there was only silence.

The clerk had shrugged when she showed him the notice. "Happens."

"Not like this."

"Sure it does." He'd gone back to his screen. Around them, Northhook Transit continued its ordinary noise: freight crawlers, announcement chimes, someone selling spiced tea from a cart that had been in the same corner since before Lena's first run through the Ribbon.

People were annoyed. Shipments delayed. Travelers grumbled in the polite way travelers grumbled when inconvenience was shared widely enough to feel impersonal.

Lena did not find it impersonal.

She found a bench near the tea cart and pulled up route maps she didn't need, because looking busy was easier than sitting still.

Kestrel Gate was not the only path to Kestrel Span, but it was the path Arin assumed when he wrote *southern mirrors* and meant home. It was the path Lena had used often enough that her muscles knew the throat sequence—the brief pressure behind the eyes, the wrong-light flicker, the smell like hot metal and clean rain.

She could go around. There was always around.

Around took eleven days and touched two jurisdictions that charged transit fees according to moods Lena had never learned to predict.

Eleven days was not forever.

Eleven days was long enough for storms over southern mirrors to become something other than a joke.

She started a reply to Arin and deleted it. *Did you hear?* was too small. *I'm coming the long way* was a promise she couldn't keep until she knew what the suspension meant.

A child ran past her bench chasing a reflection in a puddle—the upside-down geography lesson every Ribbon school drilled early. The district overhead was Meridian Arc; the puddle held a piece of it; the child stamped the piece and laughed.

Lena watched the puddle flatten and thought: real people live inside the pattern.

Arin had told her that once, not in those words. He'd told her by sending a picture of tourists wedged between mirror guides, faces tight with concentration, while locals walked past without breaking stride.

She'd been flying out the same day. She'd looked at the picture on a shuttle and felt the old familiar pull—breadth, distance, the next place.

She loved her brother. She did not always know how to stay in the same sentence as him.

---

The maintenance mark was on the bench arm, not obvious unless you were looking for scratches that weren't scratches.

A short vertical stroke with a dot beneath it, worn by weather and palms. Lena's thumb found it without meaning to. She'd seen the same mark on a dock post yesterday and on a mirror housing last month in a different district. She'd filed it as local graffiti until the repetition started to bother her.

Nobody else seemed bothered.

An older woman with a festival sash—paired celebrations with Meridian Arc, Lena guessed, from the twin colors—sat at the other end of the bench and peeled an orange without looking at the mark.

"You waiting long?" the woman asked.

"Long enough."

"My nephew's freight is stuck because of Kestrel." The woman said it the way you mentioned rain. "He'll shout about sabotage tonight. He shouts about something every night."

Lena almost smiled. "What's tonight's theory?"

"Separatists. Always separatists when the notice is clean." The woman handed her a segment of orange. "Clean notices make people invent dirt."

Lena took it. "You don't think it's sabotage?"

"I think the universe is large and administrators are lazy." The woman stood, brushing peel into a bin that swallowed it with a soft click. "If they wanted us scared, they'd give us a reason. Silence is either competence or panic. Hard to tell which."

She left before Lena could ask which she believed.

Lena ate the orange segment and thought about clean notices and invented dirt. Separatist sabotage was already spreading—she'd heard it in the tea line, seen it in two public threads before she'd given up scrolling. A story that explained fear without requiring anyone to be competent.

Eighty percent of what she knew fit inside it.

The other twenty percent was silence.

---

She found the pilgrim at the upper walk by Lake Orin, where reflection navigation was a spectator sport.

The pilgrim sat on the low wall with her boots on the stone, watching tourists attempt the crossing exercise—match your reflection, find the stair that exists in both waters, don't vomit. A standard Ribbon afternoon.

Lena had seen pilgrims before. You saw them everywhere if you traveled, the way you saw couriers and dock clerks and people who made their living explaining things that used to be obvious. This one wore the road-soft layers and the small bone beads that didn't mean anything specific unless you asked, and you usually didn't ask.

What caught Lena's attention was the lack of attention.

Everyone else on the upper walk had their displays out. Kestrel suspension trending. Route advisories. A clip of someone shouting in a Markets dialect about trade reroutes.

The pilgrim watched a man in a bright jacket miss the stair twice and laughed quietly, not unkindly.

Lena stopped beside her. "You're not checking the bulletin."

The pilgrim didn't look up. "I'm watching him learn the lake."

"He's going to bruise his knee."

"Probably. He'll remember the lake longer than the bulletin."

Lena waited a beat. "Kestrel Gate is suspended."

"Yes."

"You don't—" Lena heard herself and slowed down. "You don't seem worried."

The pilgrim turned at last. Middle-aged, calm face, eyes that did the uncomfortable thing of actually landing on you instead of through you.

"I'm not paid to be worried on your behalf," she said. "I'm on my way to the lower stations. A community lost a relay maintainer and wants a story about what maintenance means when the name is gone."

She stood, adjusting her bag. "Suspension happens. Communities adapt. People still need oranges and stairs and arguments about separatists." A small nod toward the lake. "Worried is a full-time job. I already have one."

She walked on before Lena could ask her name.

Lena stood at the wall and watched the tourist finally find the stair. He raised his arms like he'd won a race. The reflected version in the water below raised arms too, and for a second the two gestures matched perfectly—above and below, real and mirror—and Lena felt the old dizziness the guidebooks warned about.

Not because she couldn't tell which city was real.

Because both were.

---

She went back to Northhook at dusk because work was work even when the world narrowed.

The manifest argument had resolved itself in her absence. Her berth still had six days. Her private packet still needed a signature scan she hadn't collected because the firm's representative had "stepped out" in the way firms stepped out when news was fresh and everyone pretended not to hurry.

Lena collected the scan. She filed the weights. She answered two routine queries from clients who did not care about Kestrel Gate because their cargo was not going to Kestrel Span.

On the walk to her berth she passed three more maintenance marks.

She photographed them this time, not sure why, and uploaded them to a personal folder she labeled with dates instead of theories.

At the berth hatch she finally wrote to Arin.

*I saw your message. I'm still on the Ribbon—six days on the berth. Kestrel Gate suspended, no reason given. Are you all right?*

She stared at the words, then added:

*I'll find a way around if I need to. Don't do anything stupid in the mirrors.*

It wasn't poetry. It was the kind of sentence siblings sent when love was real and distance was the habit you were always trying to break.

She sent it.

The relay accepted it. The queue showed delivered to Kestrel Span node.

No immediate reply. That meant nothing. Arin answered when his hands were dry and his shift was done and he wasn't dealing with tourists who thought reflection was a metaphor.

Lena told herself that.

She lay in her berth and listened to the Ribbon move—the faint structural hum that never quite stopped, the life of a place that was also a machine and also a city and also, if you stood at Lake Orin at the right hour, a lesson in how many versions of the same thing could be true at once.

Outside, the overhead district had begun its evening lights. Meridian Arc bloomed in the dark water below her porthole. The same lights climbed the curve and became stars she couldn't reach by walking.

She thought about silence that wasn't panic and panic that hadn't found its words yet.

She thought about marks no one else seemed to see.

She thought about a pilgrim who had looked at a bruised knee instead of a bulletin and somehow sounded less foolish than everyone scrolling for reasons.

In the morning she would check the suspension notice again. She would listen for explanations that might arrive late. She would start asking questions she didn't want to ask because questions were how couriers turned inconvenience into information.

Tonight she made one decision that felt smaller than it was.

She opened her route ledger and blocked out the long way to Kestrel Span—eleven days, two fees, one throat she trusted—and labeled the block **reserved**, not **committed**.

Not yet.

But no longer pretending the suspension was someone else's annoyance.

The Ribbon hummed. The kites beyond the mirrors folded their wings against an wind she couldn't feel. Somewhere overhead, people met for appointments on opposite curves, waving across a gap that was only distance if you insisted on flat maps.

Lena closed her eyes and held the image of Arin's tourists in the southern mirrors, lost and laughing.

Real people lived inside the pattern.

She was going to reach him or learn why she couldn't.

That was participation, she thought—not certainty, not rescue, just the next act of staying in the same story as someone who stayed.

She slept badly and woke early, which was also the job.
