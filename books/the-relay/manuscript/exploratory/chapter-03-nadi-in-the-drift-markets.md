# Chapter 03 — Quick Margin

Nadi knew something was wrong before the analysts did because she had fuel in her tanks and bills on her desk.

The Drift Markets didn't orbit a star. They orbited opportunity—the slow gravity of price differentials, rumor gradients, the way freight moved when administrators spoke in clean notices and pilots spoke in fuel burn.

Nadi spoke in fuel burn.

Her ship, *Quick Margin*, was not pretty. Pretty ships cost money. She had a hull patched where a dock collision had kissed the port side three years ago, a crew of one plus a semi-autonomous loader that worked when it felt like it, and a reputation for taking routes other pilots called "creative" and accountants called "why."

Creative paid when the Markets shifted.

This week the Markets were shifting.

She stood in the auction bay with a cup of bitter station coffee and watched the wall.

Every wall in the Drift Markets carried arrivals, departures, exchange rates, freight auctions. Entire fortunes rose and fell before breakfast. Nobody asked where you came from. The answer changed too often to matter.

What mattered was the next line.

The next line was Kestrel Gate — suspended — reroute density up eleven percent — long-throat fees spiking.

"That's not eleven," the pilot beside her said. "That's someone lying about eleven."

Nadi didn't look at him. "Someone's always lying about eleven."

"This is different."

Everything was different until it wasn't. Nadi had flown through three gate scares, two real slowdowns, one actual closure that lasted a week and turned out to be a certification backlog everyone pretended was sabotage so they could sell consulting.

She'd made money on two of those.

She sipped coffee and did math in her head.

Her contracted run to Meridian Arc was still good—Ribbon freight paid reliably, and reliable was a word you didn't sneeze at when your loader had moods. But the return leg had been Kestrel throat, Kestrel fees, Kestrel fuel economics that only worked if you didn't think too hard about why they were cheap.

Cheap meant enough traffic to hide margin.

No Kestrel meant long way home or sit in the Markets burning berth fees she couldn't afford.

"Sabotage?" the pilot tried.

Nadi rolled her eyes at the wall. "Sure."

"You don't believe it?"

"I believe my fuel gauge." She finished the coffee, crushed the cup. "I believe reroutes. I believe fees. I believe people will pay to move cargo before they pay to be right."

The pilot laughed like she'd said something cynical instead of something she'd learned by scraping hull plates with her own hands.

---

In the ship she spread her trade maps across the nav table—not the public ones, her maps, layered with three years of memory.

Red lines where routes had thinned.

Green where prices had jumped.

Yellow where rumors lived.

Rumor was not data. Rumor was weather. You flew through it anyway if the cargo was time-sensitive and the client was desperate.

Kestrel suspension had turned yellow into red on two corridors she'd depended on.

She tapped the long throat option. Eleven days. Two fees. A jurisdiction that charged extra if your manifest included words like "mirror" or "reflective surface" because some clerk had been scammed once and wrote policy like revenge.

She tapped the Markets hold option. Berth fees. Opportunity cost. Loader sulk.

She tapped a third line she'd been ignoring—a fringe corridor through archive adjacency space, longer than Kestrel, shorter than the full long way, ugly and unpopular because pilots said it felt "wrong" in a way they couldn't price.

Wrong was often underbid.

Nadi marked it.

Her comm chimed. Client ping—mirror-tech firm from the Ribbon, the kind that paid well and asked few questions.

**Need priority slot Meridian Arc — revised delivery window — paying premium for certainty.**

Certainty.

Nadi almost laughed.

She typed back: **Premium accepted. Certainty not included. Delivery yes.**

She didn't wait for the frown emoji that wouldn't come because clients like this didn't use emojis. They used lawyers.

---

By afternoon the bay smelled like hot metal and hurry.

Reroutes stacked up. Dock crews shouted in four dialects. A consensus analyst she'd seen on public threads once—Jun somebody, belief flows, the kind of profession Nadi filed under "later"—stood at the edge of the auction wall taking notes with the calm of a person who thought stories were cargo too.

Maybe they were.

Nadi didn't have time to think about it.

She had a loader to bribe, a fringe corridor to file, and a premium contract that would cover half her quarter if she didn't miss the window.

She found a maintenance mark on her assigned dock clamp—dot and stroke, same as she'd seen on a throat housing last month and ignored because ignoring was free.

Today she photographed it and sent it to her own archive with a note: **third time**.

Not a theory. A habit.

Habits were how pilots survived when theories were cheap.

---

She lifted on schedule with Meridian Arc freight humming in the belly and the fringe corridor loaded like a promise she might regret.

The Markets fell away behind her—stations bright as thrown dice, ships crawling between them, the wall's numbers still updating in her peripheral display because pilots who looked away from numbers too long ended up working for numbers instead of the other way around.

Kestrel Gate suspension scrolled past on the news ribbon.

Separatist theories scrolled past after it.

Nadi set her jaw and flew.

Wrong corridors felt wrong for a reason. Usually the reason was loneliness—too few ships, too much silence, too many places where your echo came back unfamiliar.

This corridor had all of that.

It also had fees she could afford.

Halfway through she got a ping from a pilot she knew only by callsign—**BRICK**—running the long way because BRICK didn't take fringe bets.

*You seeing prices on the back leg?*

Nadi checked. Prices were ugly-beautiful, the kind that meant someone somewhere was scared enough to pay.

*Seeing them,* she sent.

*Gate's not coming back Tuesday,* BRICK wrote. *My cousin in admin says wait until they admit they don't know.*

Nadi didn't have cousins in admin. She had fuel and a premium contract and a map that remembered what fear did to freight.

*Tuesday's a theory,* she sent. *I'm flying today.*

BRICK sent back a string of characters that meant respect in Markets dialect.

---

Meridian Arc throat opened like a clean knife.

Ribbon freight delivered. Client satisfied. Premium paid.

Nadi stood in Northhook Transit for one night on the client's dime and watched Lake Orin throw Meridian Arc overhead in doubled light. Tourists failed the stair exercise. Locals didn't.

A courier with a faded compass patch stood at the railing longer than necessary, watching the lake the way pilots watched walls.

Nadi recognized the look.

Not worry about cargo.

Worry about a person.

She almost spoke—pilots and couriers shared routes, shared rumors, shared the same tired math about throats and fees—but the courier's face had the private set of someone holding a conversation in her head with someone far away.

Nadi had been that person once, about a ship, before *Quick Margin* taught her that ships responded better to maintenance than longing.

She took her berth assignment for the return fringe run and left the courier to the lake.

---

Back in the Markets a week later—because that's how fringe corridors measured time, in weeks you hadn't planned for—Nadi updated her maps.

Kestrel still suspended.

Reroute density up twenty-two percent now, not eleven; someone had stopped lying.

Separatist theories thicker.

Her business alive.

That was the thing people like Jun wanted to quantify, maybe: fear moved cargo before facts did. Belief was a freight market.

Nadi didn't dislike belief.

She disliked not getting paid.

She filed the fringe corridor as **viable** and the long throat as **emergency only** and Kestrel as **wait**, which was the closest thing to prayer she allowed herself.

On the dock clamp someone had added a fresh maintenance mark beside hers from the week before.

Same dot. Same stroke.

Different hand, steadier.

Nadi touched it with her glove and didn't photograph it this time.

Some habits became trust when you saw them repeat.

She lifted again because lifting was the job—move goods, read prices, adapt before the analysts wrote papers about adaptation.

The Markets fell away.

Somewhere a gate stayed silent.

Somewhere a courier reserved a long route.

Somewhere a mirror crew kept tourists from breaking knees.

Nadi kept flying.

It wasn't solving civilization.

It was keeping *Quick Margin* in the sky long enough to matter to the people who paid her—and to herself, which was a motivation she didn't apologize for.

The wall would update.

She would read it.

That was participation enough for today.
