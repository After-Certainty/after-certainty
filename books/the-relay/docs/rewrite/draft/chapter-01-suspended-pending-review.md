# Chapter One — Suspended Pending Review

> *Calibration draft from the rewrite handbook (Ch 1 of the [chapter outline](../chapter-outline.md)). POV: Lena. Job: open the book; establish Lena's gift = attention (instinct, not omniscience), the world through worn detail, the suspension-bulletin motif; the Vesper silence lands clean (protected humor-free zone). Withholds: the cause; whether Arin is safe. Hybrid structure: manifest attitude → live morally-gray manifest scene → gate fails live → physical bulletin → omissions as narration → Arin unreachable → action.*

---

A manifest never lies.

That was the first thing the Depot taught her, printed in the front of the manual in letters meant to look carved. It was also the first thing the job taught her to stop believing. Manifests lied all day long — by omission, by typo, by some freight-hand six crossings up the line swapping the cargo and trusting the distance to launder the difference. Lena had quit reading the logs for the truth years ago.

She read the seams.

"You're pinching the corner," she said, not looking up from the sled's tally.

The man on the other side of the loading rail — Corin, she'd cleared his consignments before — flinched and took his hand off the crate. He wore the heavy oiled canvas of a freight-hand, but his hands were too soft for the grease under his nails, and he had been holding the third crate's seal down with one thumb as if warmth might fuse what the heat-weld hadn't.

"Adhesive's rubbish this season," he said. "You know how the damp gets on the Ribbon."

"The damp on the Ribbon hasn't changed since I was apprenticed." She crouched and ran a thumb along the seam — a three-millimeter lip where the polymer had overshot the weld. "And depot seal-tape doesn't do that. You tucked a second lining inside the shell after the customs stamp. Recent. The lip's still soft."

He didn't answer. She let the quiet do the work. His fingers kept twitching against the crate. His eyes kept going to the departure board. And tucked into his left cuff was the blue edge of a high-fare ticket — the kind that bought a fast crossing instead of a steerage berth, the kind a freight-hand didn't pay for out of a freight-hand's wage.

"It's seed-stock," he said, lower now. He smelled of chicory and old fear. "For the lower settlements out past Vesper. They've had no relief vessel from the inner worlds in three months — the regional office is sitting on the paperwork till the quarter closes, everyone covering their own backs. I'm just trying to get it through before the rotation locks the platforms. People have to plant something."

It was probably even half true. That was the thing about the good lies; they were built on a real grievance. But the crate ran nine kilos heavy for seed, and a man moving seed for hungry strangers did not also buy himself a blue ticket out of the sector.

"Lena, please." He'd read her rose, or her face. "The arch locks for the maintenance cycle in two hours, and the next courier through to the lower settlements is a week out. Just sign the stub. It's a routine hop." He meant the part about the settlements. People always believed the true half loudest; it was how they got the false half to ride along under it.

"The seed's real," Lena said. "And so's whatever's under it paying for your seat." She watched that land — not denial, just a man going still. "I don't need to know which. Get it on the sled."

He blinked. "You'll take it?"

"I'll take it." She thumbed a line into the tally. "Ten percent on the weight variance, logged as bulk adjustment. If the customs hands on the far side crack it and find wire, you've never met me."

He breathed out like something had let go of his throat. "Bless your eyes, courier."

"Don't." She closed the tally. "Get it down before the loaders dump it."

She watched him hump the crate to the chute, and she didn't feel good about it, and she didn't feel bad. In seven hundred years nobody had built a network this size that anyone could actually hold; it survived because everybody knew which bolts were loose and agreed, quietly, not to kick them. A skipped fee here, a second lining there, a number rounded toward whatever kept the line moving. The system was too big to care about nine kilos. She was small enough to.

She filed the blue ticket away on the same inner shelf where a courier kept the things that hadn't earned a place yet, and went to do the one thing she came near Vesper to do and never quite did.

---

The Vesper arch sat at the low end of the south concourse, and it was working the way it always worked, which was to say it did not look like anything at all. People walked up to a doorway in the old grey stone and stepped through and were simply elsewhere — out the far side on another world, a thousand of them stitched into the length of an afternoon's walk. No flash, no falling. One step here and the next step changed its mind about where your shoe belonged, and that was all. Children cried their first crossing because their bodies braced for thunder and got accounting. Everyone else stopped seeing it by the second time. You had to, or you'd never get anything done, walking around stunned at a doorway that ate the distance between stars.

Lena had stopped seeing it years ago. She still came and stood near it.

A woman near the head of the queue was working herself toward bolting — one hand white on a travel case, the other on the sleeve of a man murmuring "it's nothing, it's nothing" in the exact voice people used when it was something. First crossing. Lena could spot one across a concourse.

"It won't feel like anything," she said, stepping in beside her. "That's the whole trick. Your body braces for a fall that never comes. Don't stop walking in the arch — that's the only part that matters."

"That sounds worse."

"It's worse the first time." The steward waved the next group on. "Go on. People worse than you have done it all morning."

"That isn't comfort."

"No. But it's evidence."

The woman went, flinching, and was gone — out the far side onto some other world, whole, the way ten thousand people stepped through before second sitting without once looking up. Lena watched the empty arch a moment, the ordinary miracle of it, and turned back to her own reasons for standing there.

She was not going to cross today. She never was. She was going to stand at the arch a minute, think about the Tarn, decide not to, and buy the pepper-mint drops their mother had rationed like medicine when they were small — Arin still couldn't be trusted around them, thirty-four years old and he'd eat the whole twist if no one stopped him. She'd send them on with whoever was actually making the Tarn run, with a tag that said almost nothing, because everything she wanted to say had never once fit on a courier-tag. *Saw these. Thought of you.* Or *next time.* She'd written *next time* so many times it had stopped being a promise and become a place they both lived.

She bought the candies first. The vendor twisted the paper shut. Near his cart two maintenance kids sat on an upturned crate, sharing a strip of candied peel and watching an overhead lamp stutter out of rhythm, doing nothing about it. Not their lamp, then, or not their shift. She filed it. Down at Bay Four a dockhand she knew a little, Beska, was restacking the same three crates in the same wrong order, the busy hands of someone who has had bad news and can't be still.

"Your crates giving you trouble?" Lena said.

Beska looked up too slowly. "My aunt's on the Tarn run. Was due through last night. Didn't come." She said it to the crates. "No delay log. No keeper's name. Nothing in the corridor notes. My mother keeps asking did I check the family board." She nodded at the three crates under her hands, and Lena saw they wore the Vesper mark — not delayed, not rerouted. *Returned.* Cargo that came back before it went anywhere had forms, had a person swearing about forms. These had neither. "Before first sitting," Beska said. "No runner. They were just there."

The shelf inside Lena took it and couldn't find anywhere to set it down.

That was when the hum stopped.

She had never once heard it, which was how she knew it the instant it was gone — the low constant note the gate made, the sound everyone on the Ribbon had been hearing since the day they were born and had filed under *world*. It went out the way a held breath goes out. And in the quiet after it, the doorway in the arch — the faint optical almost-nothing, the suggestion of a far platform breathing on the other side of the stone — closed. Not slammed. Closed, the way an eye closes. The grey arch showed her its own back wall, plain damp stone with an old repair scar down one side, as if there had never been anywhere on the other side of it at all.

No klaxon. No red. Red was a guide fault, a breach, somebody running down a gantry swearing into a headset — red was *loud*, and loud meant someone was already fixing it. This had no sound at all. Up and down the concourse the indicator lamps over the arch simply went dark, one bank after another, like candles taken by a draft moving down a long hall.

For a moment nobody did anything. The station kept up its ordinary noise — shoes, route announcements for the gates that still worked, a child somewhere — over a hole where a sound used to be, and the people nearest the arch turned to it slowly, the way you turn toward a thing your body understood before you did.

Down the platform a ticker that had been clattering arrival times all morning ran down to nothing, its paper tongue gone still. On a cart nearby a kettle kept boiling, absurdly, while its owner stood with the empty cup forgotten in his hand. The warmth in the floor began, very slightly, to go.

A steward came at a half-run with a printed panel in his hands. Young, Meridian-trained by the walk, terrified of being seen to be. He slotted the panel into the board beside the dark arch and stepped back from it fast, as if it might be hot.

People drifted up to read it with the mild irritation of travelers whose train was late. That was the part Lena would remember — how badly the place wanted this to be ordinary, how hungry everyone was for it to be the kind of trouble you could complain about and wait out.

"Always something," a man said, comfortably, to no one.

"They'll sort it," said a woman with an infant asleep against her chest, and the two of them shared the small warm nod of strangers agreeing the world was in hand. But the lights were out, and the hum was gone, and the nod cost them more than it should have.

The panel held one line, in the flat unspaced type of the regional office:

> **VESPER CROSSING — SUSPENDED PENDING REVIEW**

Lena read it twice, then a third time, because anger sometimes hid things. It hadn't. There was nothing to find. No who: it didn't say which authority, or whose hand. No why: it didn't say what had failed, or whether anything had — transit, signaling, the crossing itself, the right to ask. No when. No fault code, no posting office, no estimated restoration, not even the little printed line pretending some department large enough to regret in shifts regretted the inconvenience.

Three words and a stamp. A shrug with formatting.

It was a real object, at least. She could give it that. Someone had cut it, printed it, carried it down, and a frightened boy had pushed it into a slot — the way you draw a sheet over something you can't fix. She reached up and worked the panel out of the board's clip and turned it over. The back was blank.

"Courier—" the steward started.

"Log that I took the first posting," she said. "My name's on the rose if anyone wants it." He looked relieved to be given a verb.

She kept it. Notices like this got replaced inside the hour, once the offices upstream caught up and decided what story to tell — there'd be a fault code by evening, an apology, an estimated time, an office's name signed at the bottom. She wanted the first one. The honest one, which was honest only because no one had yet had time to improve it. It seemed suddenly important that there be one object that remembered the exact shape of the moment the air went out of the room.

Then she opened her slate, because she had to know the other thing, and there was only one way to know it.

Arin's last message sat where she'd left it six days ago, read half-asleep on the Hadrian berth and never answered, because answering properly took time and answering badly felt worse.

*Storms over the southern mirrors again. You'd laugh — tourists trying to navigate by the reflections. One man followed the lake-path twenty minutes before he worked out the stairs were above him.*

She typed under it. *Vesper is down. Are you all right?*

The slate searched for a route. A small wheel turned beside the message. It turned for a while.

**DELIVERY QUEUED — ROUTE UNAVAILABLE**

She tried again, routing the long way through Meridian. The slate refused before the wheel finished a turn.

**NO VALID RELAY PATH TO DESTINATION**

She stood with the dead panel in one hand and the dead slate in the other. The Tarn was three slow-routes past Vesper if you were sensible, four if the weather pushed, five if the pilots got careful about risk — reachable, still, the long way, by ship and patience. But not from here, not now, not with a word. Arin would be at the canal lock at this hour, watching the water go to iron under the north wind, his ledger open on his knee. He'd see the empty line where her arrival stamp went and think she'd taken a paying run down toward the rim. He'd think she was being smart, saving for the spring seed. He would not know the road between them hadn't closed. It had stopped existing.

She put her free hand flat against the stone where the doorway had been. Cool. Slightly gritty. Entirely a wall. No hum under it, no wrong-light flicker, no far platform breathing on the other side. No place for the message to go, no way to tell him not to take the skiff out if the mirrors were storming, no way to send the candies in her pocket, no way to say — stupidly, too late — *I was near enough to come, and didn't.*

All the things she'd ever filed under *next time* were suddenly just unsent. *Next time* had been a place the two of them lived, a country with no border guards, open at every hour. Someone had suspended it pending review.

She kept her hand there until the cold reached the bone. Then she took it away.

"Some of us are just trying to get home." The man was broad, sweating at the temples, two children behind him, angry the way frightened people get when nobody's offered them a cleaner feeling.

"So am I," Lena said.

"Then take Meridian around like the rest of us."

"Vesper goes to the Tarn."

"Nothing goes to the Tarn today." He heard it as he said it, and looked away. The words had landed harder than he meant. They'd landed exactly where she lived.

Around her the concourse was settling into the shapes of waiting — someone asking whether vouchers carried through Meridian, someone asking if *review* meant safety or audit, the woman with the infant beginning to hum without knowing it. *They'll sort it*, the place kept telling itself, in a dozen small voices, and Lena watched the faith in it thin with each repetition. Nobody had said who *they* were. Everyone here had spent a lifetime trusting a *they* that never needed a face, and the first time it failed to answer, no one knew which door to knock on. The young steward stood at the edge of it with nothing left to hand anyone.

Lena stepped into his path. "Who posted it?"

He looked at the courier rose on her shoulder, then at the board, hoping one of them would let him off. "Regional authority."

"Which one?"

"The appropriate one."

"That sentence has never survived a specific question." When he only swallowed, she softened it a degree. "Where'd it come from? Which office?"

"It came down sealed," he said, low, almost honest. "I just put it up. I don't know who sealed it." His eyes went, without his meaning them to, up toward the smoked glass of the upper offices.

That was enough to begin.

She put the candies back in the inner pocket of her bag, beside the slate that couldn't reach her brother, and the printed panel under her arm where it wouldn't bend. Behind the smoked glass was an office that had received a sealed notice from somewhere else. Behind that was an office that had sent it on without opening it. Behind that, somewhere up the line, was a person who had decided three words would do, or a person who had decided not to be the one to decide. Behind all of it was a silence wide enough to turn a door into a wall — and silence, she was already beginning to understand, had no author you could name. Only a last hand that touched it, and a first hand that wouldn't.

She had cargo she shouldn't have taken, a returned crate nobody logged, a stuttering lamp, a dockhand whose aunt was due through last night and never came, a frightened boy with a stack of panels, and a brother behind stone. All of it belonged somewhere. None of it had been carried there.

Lena turned away from the dark arch and started up toward the offices. She didn't know what had happened to Vesper. She didn't know whether Arin was safe.

But she knew how to begin. Find who touched it last. Find who wouldn't sign it. Find who decided three words and a stamp were enough to hold a door shut.

Find the person who had written the notice.
