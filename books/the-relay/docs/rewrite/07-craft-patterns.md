# Guide 7 — Craft Patterns

*The Relay Design Handbook · the living catalog of techniques the novel discovers about itself. Assumes all prior guides. Owns recurring craft techniques; owns nothing that any other guide owns. Written last, and **deliberately empty at the start.***

> **Status:** v1 — **first entries promoted from the Ch 1 / Ch 9 voice calibration.** The catalog began deliberately empty and fills only *from* drafted prose; two patterns have now earned promotion, with several candidates logged (with first-seen instances) on the Watchlist.

Every other guide is written *before* the prose, to decide how the novel works. This one is different. It is written *from* the prose. A craft pattern is a technique the novel keeps using — proven by recurrence, not predicted in advance. Inventing patterns before the book exists would be guessing; this guide refuses to guess.

So it begins empty, on purpose. Its value is that it stays honest: it records only what the writing has actually shown to work, so that a technique discovered in chapter three can be applied deliberately in chapter twenty.

---

## What belongs here (and what does not)

- **Belongs here:** repeatable *prose and scene-construction techniques* — moves at the level of how a scene is built or a sentence lands, that recur across the book and benefit from being named so they can be reused on purpose.
- **Does not belong here:**
  - philosophy, themes, commitments → **Constitution**
  - plot beats, integration events → **Plot**
  - world rules, cultures → **World Design**
  - artifact conventions → **Artifacts**
  - people → **Character Guide**
  - voice, tone, dialogue, humor *rules* → **Writing Style**

The boundary with Style is the one to watch: **Style owns the rules; Craft Patterns owns the recurring techniques that execute them.** If it's a principle, it's Style's. If it's a concrete, repeatable move discovered in the writing, it's a pattern.

The cleanest illustration is the **observational voice**, which lives in two dimensions:

- **Prose dimension → Writing Style.** *How* Lena notices things, sentence by sentence.
- **Structure dimension → Craft Patterns.** *When* a particular kind of observation recurs and becomes a deliberate, repeated literary device across the book.

A single vivid noticing is Style. A noticing that becomes a recurring engine of the novel is a candidate pattern here (promoted only once it proves it recurs).

---

## Promotion threshold

A technique is **not** a pattern until it earns the name. Promote an entry only when **either**:

1. **it has appeared at least twice** in the drafted prose (recurrence is the proof), **or**
2. **it has been adopted as a deliberate organizing principle** in revision (an agreed, intentional device the book will use repeatedly going forward).

Until one of those is true, an idea lives in the **Watchlist** below, not the catalog. This threshold is the guard against the guide filling up with untested theories.

---

## Entry template

When a pattern qualifies, add it using this template:

```
### [Pattern name]

- **What it is:** one sentence — the technique itself.
- **What it's for:** the effect it produces / the rule it serves (link the owning guide).
- **First seen:** chapter/scene where it appeared (and the second instance, for promotion).
- **How to do it:** the repeatable move, concretely.
- **Example:** a short illustrative line or beat from the manuscript.
- **Failure mode:** how it goes wrong; when *not* to use it.
- **Related:** guides/patterns it touches.
```

## Catalog

*(First entries promoted from the two voice-calibration chapters, Ch 1 and Ch 9. A pattern appears here only once it has met the promotion threshold — twice in prose, or deliberately adopted in revision.)*

### Failure-in-miniature

- **What it is:** the chapter's small, concrete, human problem is a scale model of the book's macro failure — *uncommunicated local corrections compounding* — so the theme is carried by plot texture and never stated.
- **What it's for:** dramatizes the Constitution's failure mechanism without explaining it; each chapter quietly trains the reader to read the large collapse by reading a small one. (Constitution / Plot; Style's implication-over-explanation.)
- **First seen:** **Ch 1** — Corin's false manifest: a partial-truth workaround around a slow official channel (*"everybody knew which bolts were loose and agreed, quietly, not to kick them"*). **Second:** **Ch 9** — the sealed Sumn relief: a pile of individually valid reasons that never meet (*"a pile of perfectly good reasons that had never been introduced"*).
- **How to do it:** give the scene a tangible problem (a crate, a sealed pallet) whose cause is *distributed* — no villain, only reasons that don't connect; let the POV character feel the rhyme without naming it; resolve it locally and leave the macro unresolved.
- **Example:** *"The system was too big to care about nine kilos. She was small enough to."*
- **Failure mode:** the rhyme gets *stated* ("and that was how the whole network was failing too") — forbidden; or the local problem is given a single culprit, which breaks the no-villain architecture.
- **Related:** Constitution (failure mechanism), Plot, Writing Style.

### Open on an undercut stance

- **What it is:** begin a chapter with a short, confident *opinion* in the POV voice, then immediately puncture or complicate it — grip before scenery.
- **What it's for:** the engagement test — attitude, friction, and a reason to read on land in the first lines, before any world-admiring. (Writing Style: "openings grip before they breathe.")
- **First seen:** **Ch 1** — *"A manifest never lies"* → *"the first thing the job taught her to stop believing."* **Second:** **Ch 9** — *"A hungry crowd did not turn dangerous when the food ran out. It turned dangerous when the food was right there…"*
- **How to do it:** state a rule or claim the character holds; undercut it within a sentence or two with lived experience; drop straight into the scene the claim sets up.
- **Failure mode:** the opinion is the *narrator's* thesis rather than the character's working knowledge (tips into lecture); or the undercut never comes and the line just sits there as an epigraph.
- **Related:** Writing Style (openings; observational voice).

## Watchlist — candidate patterns (not yet promoted)

*Techniques anticipated by the design guides or noted in the parking lot. They are recorded here so they can be **watched for** during drafting — but they are **not** patterns until they meet the threshold above. Do not cite these as established technique.*

- **Position-argued / interest-inferred** *(from Style/Character/parking lot)* — characters voice positions; the reader infers interests. Candidate because it may recur as a concrete scene-build move, not just a rule.
- **The bulletin-omission** *(from Style/Artifacts)* — revealing rot through what an official notice leaves out. **First strong instance: Ch 1** (the `SUSPENDED PENDING REVIEW` panel read by its omissions — "No who… No why… No when"). Watch for a second.
- **Right-and-wrong-in-one-scene** *(from Character)* — a character (or a whole group) shown visibly correct and visibly insufficient within a single beat. **First instance: Ch 9** (the chorus-culture *right but too slow*; Varik feeds the child and over-answers in the same breath — *"Both were true"*). Watch for a second.
- **The faceless "they," punctured** *(from Constitution/Style)* — institutional faith named and quietly thinned: a *they* that has never needed a face, failing to answer for the first time. **First instance: Ch 1** ("*They'll sort it*… nobody had said who *they* were"); lighter echo in Ch 9 (his name said "like a handhold"). Watch for a deliberate third use.
- **No-author / last-hand–first-hand** *(from Constitution/Plot)* — the no-villain architecture rendered concrete: blame resolves not to a culprit but to "a last hand that touched it, and a first hand that wouldn't." **First instance: Ch 1.** Should recur whenever the book is tempted to supply a villain; watch for it.
- **Artifact-against-artifact** *(from Artifacts/Plot)* — staging two conflicting documents so the reader assembles the truth.
- **The aha-belongs-to-arrangement** *(from Plot)* — locating realization in the configuration of people, never in a narrated thought.
- **Observational-reveal (no interpretation)** *(from Style)* — Lena's noticing carries the system; the interpretation is withheld. *(Now also covered as a Style rule, "never name the gift; spend the tells"; keep here as a candidate for the structural/recurring dimension.)*
- **Meaning-migration recurrence** *(from Artifacts)* — reusing an object (the manifest, the bulletin) only when its meaning has changed. *(Seeded Ch 1; the bulletin is meant to return, answered, in Ch 28.)*

When any of these appears twice in prose — or is consciously adopted in revision — move it into the **Catalog** with a full entry and delete it here.

## Failure Modes

*Ways this guide can betray its own purpose.*

- **Patterns declared before they recur.** The catalog fills with predicted techniques. *Tell:* an entry exists that has appeared zero or one times in prose. *Instead:* it stays on the Watchlist until it earns promotion.
- **Watchlist items cited as law.** A candidate is treated as an established technique. *Tell:* a draft justifies a choice by an unpromoted pattern. *Instead:* candidates are watched for, not invoked.
- **Style rules smuggled in as patterns.** A principle that belongs to Writing Style is renamed a "pattern." *Tell:* the entry is a rule, not a repeatable concrete move. *Instead:* rules → Style; recurring techniques → here.
- **The catalog becomes prescriptive.** Patterns turn into a checklist the prose must hit. *Tell:* scenes are built to exhibit patterns. *Instead:* patterns describe what the writing already does; the story stays primary (Law 1).
