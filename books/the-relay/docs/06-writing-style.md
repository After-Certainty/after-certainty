# Guide 6 — Writing Style

*The Relay Design Handbook · how the novel sounds and feels on the page. Assumes all prior guides (Constitution and Plot frozen; World, Artifacts, Character drafted). Owns prose, voice, dialogue, humor, and POV. Owns nothing about what is true — only how it is rendered.*

> **Status:** Draft v1, written to be argued with. Items marked **[DECISION]** are author-level calls.

Every other guide decides *what* the book is. This one decides *how it reads*. Its single governing instinct comes straight from the Constitution: **implication over explanation.** The book trusts the reader. It shows a civilization through worn objects and offhand competence, dramatizes ideas through people at work, and lets meaning arrive by inference. The prose is the last line of defense against the book's greatest temptation — to *explain itself*.

---

## The master rule: characters argue positions; readers infer interests

The most important style rule in the handbook, shared with Plot and Character and owned here for execution:

- **Positions are spoken.** Characters say what they want and why, in their own terms ("We cannot abandon what generations entrusted to us"). Positions are loud, partial, and sincere.
- **Interests are inferred.** The need beneath the position — Cai's fear of failing on his watch, Nadi's fear of obsolescence, Varik's need to be needed — is **never stated by the character and never narrated.** It is dramatized: shown in what they protect, what they refuse, what they flinch at.

**The test:** if a character names their own underlying need, or the narrator names it for them, rewrite. The reader does the integrating — which is the book's whole subject enacted at sentence level.

---

## Tone and voice

- **Overall register:** grounded, warm, dry. Competent people doing real work in a vast old system. Not grimdark, not whimsical — *lived-in.*
- **The narrator is not a lecturer.** Third person, but never omniscient-explanatory. The narration knows what the POV character notices, not what the civilization "means." When you feel the prose reaching to summarize a theme, cut the sentence; the scene already carried it.
- **Awe through specificity, not adjectives.** The scale of the Relay lands through one worn, concrete detail, not through the words *vast*, *ancient*, *immense*.
- **The thesis is never on the page as a thesis.** No character and no paragraph may state the book's argument outright. (Constitution: the ending *increases* mystery; the prose must not collapse it.)

---

## Point of view

- **LOCKED: third person limited, a tiered POV system of exactly three.** **Lena is the primary anchor** — the clear majority of scenes, the spine and emotional center. **Arin is the secondary POV** (the Stay leg). **Jun is a limited third POV** — a handful of scenes — because his interior, watching his own hedged work stripped into Varik's certainty, is the one thing that genuinely cannot be carried from outside. **Everyone else stays external:** Cai, Nadi, Toma, Sera, Veya are seen only through these three. No single mind can hold this book, and neither should the narration — but three is enough to make distributed knowing structural without diluting the protagonist.
- **Varik never gets POV (principle, not default).** Giving the embodiment of premature certainty an inhabitable interior either flattens him into a strawman or makes him sympathetic from the inside — and, worst, invites the reader to *adopt* his certainty. He is most dangerous and most tragic seen from outside. No exceptions.
- **Arin's POV is emergent, not expository.** It opens in the middle and renders *staying as texture* — deep local attention, small quiet connections — **without Arin conceptualizing it as building a "web."** The depth-network coheres before he (or the reader) names it, so his POV never spoils the "who is building the lists?" recognition; it mirrors the book's thesis that institutions emerge unplanned. Both siblings do something whose significance they grasp only at convergence.
- **Keep Lena dominant.** She is the protagonist even though she is never the solver; if the ensemble ever crowds her out, cut back. As a rough guard, the great majority of scenes are hers, and Acts I and V are Lena-dominant (Arin and Jun cluster in the middle, where the parallel thread and the cascade live).
- **Don't map POV one-to-one onto fragments.** A POV exists to *withhold* and to give an interior that matters — never to deliver that character's "piece." If a POV scene's only job is to hand the reader a fragment, that is the puzzle-piece trap; fold it into another vantage.
- **One POV per scene.** No head-hopping. The reader learns the same way the characters do: partially, from one vantage at a time.
- **POV is an epistemic instrument.** Each POV character sees truly and sees partially; the *gaps between* POVs are where the reader assembles what no character knows. Choose POV per scene by asking *whose partial knowledge best withholds the rest.*
- **Lena's ceiling (see Humor):** as the observational anchor she carries the dry wit, but her POV must never become the voice that *understands the civilization* — she notices, she does not explain.

## Dialogue

- **Anti-thesis rule.** No character speaks the book's argument. Dialogue carries *positions and goals*, not authorial conclusions. The moment a line could be lifted out as the book's "message," rewrite it.
- **People talk past each other.** Because everyone is right-and-wrong-and-dependent, conversations are real arguments between partial truths, not debates with a winner. No one gets the last word that settles it.
- **Subtext over statement.** Characters pursue goals through talk; the need underneath leaks out sideways. Let people be indirect, defensive, funny, evasive — human.
- **Distinct mouths.** Each character's one-line voice (Character Guide) is executed here. The reader should know who is speaking before the dialogue tag.
- **No exposition ventriloquism.** A character must never explain world mechanics for the reader's benefit that they would never explain to the person they're talking to. If it's for the reader, find another way (an artifact, an action, an inference).

## Humor

Humor is structural, not decorative — it is how the book stays warm and human while handling large ideas. (Constitution: the book is allowed to be funny.)

- **Sources of humor:**
  - the gap between official notices and ground truth (bureaucratic self-importance vs. reality);
  - competence comedy — experts being deadpan about absurd situations;
  - character friction — the same event read three incompatible ways;
  - gallows humor in the face of the slow crisis (Nadi);
  - dry archival and historical irony (Toma, Veya).
- **Who carries what** (from Character fingerprints): **Nadi** — salt and gallows wit; **Cai** — rare deadpan that lands harder for its rarity; **Veya/Toma** — dry irony; **Lio** — youthful irreverence; **Sera** — gentle, including humor; **Jun** — self-deprecation; **Varik** — genuinely charming (his humor *works*, which is the point).
- **Lena's observational ceiling.** Lena holds the book's running dry wit, but it stays *observational* — she's funny about what she sees, never a stand-up narrator. Her humor reveals how information moves; it never editorializes the theme.
- **[DECISION] Humor-free zones.** Certain beats are protected from wit so they land clean — proposed: the Vesper silence, the Jun–Varik recognition, the sibling reunion, and the final image. Mark these in the outline; comedy near them is a defect, not relief.

### Every recurring gag must evolve

A running joke is not allowed to merely *repeat* — each return must **grow**, accumulating meaning until the joke quietly becomes true. A repeated gag is a tic; an evolving one is structure. The model case is the **courier reputation** running joke:

| Roughly | The joke |
|---|---|
| early | "Couriers? They know *everything*." |
| later | "They know *everyone*." |
| later | "They accidentally become diplomats." |
| later | "They get blamed for the rumors they carry." |
| climax | They are carrying the Open Manifest — **and the joke has become literally true.** |

The laugh at the start and the weight at the end are the same observation, ripened. When you plant a recurring joke, plan its ladder; if it cannot climb, cut it.

### Character humor exposes an assumption

The right question for a character's comedy is not "what *kind* of humor do they have?" but: **what assumption does this character accidentally expose when they're funny?** Humor then does characterization, not decoration — each person's wit reveals the world they unconsciously live inside.

| Character | The absurdity their humor exposes |
|---|---|
| Lena | institutional absurdity (notices vs. reality) |
| Jun | mathematical / model absurdity |
| Nadi | economic absurdity |
| Cai | maintenance absurdity |
| Toma / Veya | historical absurdity |
| Sera | social absurdity |
| Varik | **almost none — his certainty suppresses humor.** He is charming, but he rarely finds things *absurd*, because absurdity requires holding a question open. His near-humorlessness about the world is itself characterization. |

## The observational voice *(owned here)*

The novel's signature mode: a character (chiefly Lena) reading the civilization through small concrete observation. This is the engine of "show the system, don't explain it."

- **What it is:** noticing *how things actually work* — who isn't heard, what a notice omits, where a story has a seam — and letting the reader infer the system from the noticing.
- **When to use it:** to reveal world, theme, or character through perception rather than exposition; especially when you're tempted to explain.
- **When *not* to use it:** when it would tip into the character *understanding* what they should only *notice*. The line is: **observation reveals; interpretation explains.** Stay on the observation side.
- **Never name the gift; spend the tells.** A character's skill or instinct (Lena's attention) is rendered *only* as behavior the reader can watch — the twitching fingers, the blue ticket-edge in a cuff, the seam still soft to the thumb — never as a narrated trait. *(Calibration: an explicit "a courier's gift was not a magic eye…" was cut from Ch 1 precisely because the tells had already proven it; naming it was the prose taking credit for what the scene had earned.)*
- **Systemic events land in the body before the bulletin.** A failure in the vast machine is felt first as *sensation* — the hum you only notice once it is gone, the warmth leaving the floor — and only afterward as *information* (the notice). The infrastructure is most visible the instant it disappears. *(Calibration: Ch 1's gate fails as a missing sound before any notice exists.)*
- **On the page (example, illustrative):**
  - *Yes:* "The bulletin said *suspended pending review*. It didn't say by whom, or what review, or pending what. Three words doing the work of a shrug." *(notices the omission; reader infers the rot)*
  - *No:* "The bulletin's vagueness was a symptom of a civilization that had stopped relaying what it knew." *(narrator explains the theme — forbidden)*

**Two dimensions — and only one is owned here.** The observational voice lives in two places, and the boundary matters: **this guide owns the *prose* dimension — *how* Lena notices things, sentence by sentence.** When a particular observation *recurs* and hardens into a deliberate, repeatable literary device (a structural motif used on purpose across the book), that is the **structure** dimension, and it belongs to **Craft Patterns** (promoted only once it has proven it recurs). Rule of thumb: a single vivid noticing is Style; a noticing that becomes a recurring engine of the book is a Craft Pattern.

## Cultural and species voice

Implements World Design's cultures without essentialism.

- **Culture flavors speech; it does not script the person.** A character's species/culture shapes idiom, rhythm, reference, and humor — never their morality, intelligence, or choices.
- **No caricature, no monoliths.** Two characters of the same culture should sound as different from each other as any two humans. Counter-examples are deliberate (Character Guide's anti-essentialism guardrail).
- **Idiom does the work of exposition.** Loan-words, sayings, and rhythm signal a culture's values (the road-culture's allusive speed; the place-culture's slow, context-heavy talk) so the narrator never has to describe "what their kind is like."
- **[DECISION] Restraint on invented language.** Future Language is the parking-lot reduction of invented jargon; on the page, invented terms are rare, legible in context, and never explained outright.

## Register and profanity (per character)

Register is part of the fingerprint and is executed consistently.

- **[DECISION] Proposed profanity policy:** profanity is a *characterization tool*, used sparingly and unevenly so it stays meaningful.
  - **Nadi** — high; she carries most of the book's profanity (blunt, salty, concrete).
  - **Lio** — occasional; youthful.
  - **Lena** — rare and dry; lands hard when it comes.
  - **Sera** — almost never (warmth, not piety).
  - **Cai, Toma, Veya, Jun** — sparing to none; register does their work instead.
  - **Varik** — never coarse; his power is fluency and warmth, not edge.
- **Register as fingerprint:** Jun qualifies and hedges; Cai is terse; Toma is antiquarian; Veya is incisive; Sera is plain and rooted. The reader should place a speaker by register alone.

## Locked register lexicon *(from calibration)*

Small, concrete word- and texture-choices the calibration chapters settled — recorded so the book stays one consistent place rather than drifting chapter to chapter:

- **Time / meal markers:** *"second sitting,"* not *"second bell."* Stations keep time by *sittings.*
- **Transit-hub palette:** yeast off the platform carts, brake-dust, damp stone, chicory, travel-wool — the smell of a place *built to be passed through,* not of a village. *(Guard against bleed from adjacent projects: no "bread and rope.")*
- **A false manifest is grease, not conspiracy.** The everyday lie is a local workaround that keeps the line moving ("everybody knew which bolts were loose and agreed not to kick them"), never a sinister plot. When such an object must mean more later, let the *meaning* migrate; the object stays mundane.
- **Avoid *"throat"*** as an invented term (per the Future-Language reduction); a single voice carrying a room is *"one voice,"* not *"one throat."*

## Line-level commitments

- **Implication over explanation** (the Constitution rule, enforced sentence by sentence): one vivid unexplained detail beats a paragraph of mechanism.
- **Concrete over abstract:** prefer the worn object, the specific gesture, the named route to the general statement.
- **Openings grip before they breathe; endings compress.** Lead a chapter on a *stance, a pressure, or a person in conflict* — not the narrator admiring the world. Orient fast (the reader is never lost about *what is happening*), and earn any lyricism by placing it *after* the first friction, never before it. Scene and chapter endings still cut early and trust the reader. *(Calibration: Ch 1 and Ch 9 each open on an undercut opinion — "A manifest never lies" / "A hungry crowd did not turn dangerous when the food ran out" — not on a vista. This supersedes any earlier "openings may breathe to orient.")*
- **No narrated epiphanies.** When a character realizes something, show the realization's *effect*, not a paragraph naming the insight. (Especially: the climax's "aha" belongs to the *arrangement of people*, never to a narrated thought — Plot.)
- **Earn the abstract word.** The book may occasionally name an idea — but only after the scene has made it felt, and never as the scene's point.
- **Ration the aphorism hard (the "thesis-shaped line").** The voice *will* throw off the occasional crystalline, quotable sentence ("a shrug with formatting"; "a pile of perfectly good reasons that had never been introduced"; "he had fed them twice"). Treat these as expensive: aim for **zero, allow at most one per chapter**, never two in a paragraph, never the line a scene is built to deliver. The default is to *cut* the polished sentence and let behavior, object, and gesture carry the meaning; keep one only when nothing concrete can do the job and the thought is load-bearing. *(Calibration tell: if you can lift the sentence onto a poster, you have probably already spent more than the chapter can afford — keep the single best, rough the rest back into action.)*

## Accessibility

From the Constitution: this is a humane, readable book, not a puzzle box.

- **Future tech reads *easier* than present-day tech**, never harder. No technobabble; future tools are legible at a glance (Artifacts).
- **The reader is never lost about what is happening** — only, deliberately, about what it *means*. Withhold significance, never basic orientation.
- **Difficulty is reserved for ideas, not for decoding sentences.** If a reader has to reread to parse the grammar, that's a defect; if they reread to sit with a thought, that's the design.

## Failure Modes

*Ways prose can follow the rules and still go wrong. (Localizes "do not mistake explanation for understanding.")*

- **Everyone sounds witty.** The whole cast shares one (the author's) sense of humor. *Tell:* you can't tell who's speaking from the joke. *Instead:* each character's humor exposes *their* assumption; some characters are barely funny at all.
- **The narrator explains the theme.** Observational voice tips into interpretation. *Tell:* a sentence states what a detail was a symptom *of*. *Instead:* observe; let the reader infer. (See the yes/no examples above.)
- **Interests get spoken.** A character or the narrator names the need beneath a position. *Tell:* "What he really wanted was to be needed." *Instead:* dramatize it; the reader integrates.
- **Exposition ventriloquism.** A character explains world mechanics they'd never explain to their listener, for the reader's benefit. *Tell:* on-the-nose lore in dialogue. *Instead:* artifact, action, or inference.
- **Recurring jokes repeat.** A gag returns unchanged. *Tell:* the third instance lands exactly like the first. *Instead:* it must climb its ladder or be cut.
- **Future tech as technobabble.** The world reads harder than the present. *Tell:* a reader rereads to parse a device. *Instead:* future tools legible at a glance; difficulty is reserved for ideas.

## Dramatization

*How style ideas become scenes — `idea → shown through… / not explained by…`.*

- **Implication over explanation** → *shown through* a three-word bulletin doing the work of a shrug; *not explained by* a sentence naming the symptom.
- **Position vs. interest** → *shown through* what a character defends when cornered; *not explained by* the character or narrator naming the need.
- **Distributed knowing** → *shown through* the gaps between one-POV-per-scene; *not explained by* an omniscient summary.
- **Humor as warmth** → *shown through* deadpan competence under strain; *not explained by* telling the reader the mood is light.
