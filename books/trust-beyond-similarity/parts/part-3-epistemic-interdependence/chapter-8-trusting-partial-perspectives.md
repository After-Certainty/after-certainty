# **Trusting Partial Perspectives**

> *What if trust is necessary because none of us can see enough alone?*

The fence kept returning.

No matter where the project began—a budget meeting, a design sketch, a youth session, a council question, a safety review, a conversation about hours—the fence eventually reappeared. Sometimes literally, in photographs, maps, renderings, and site walks. Sometimes indirectly, in words that sounded like other topics until everyone realized they were still talking about the boundary around the lot.

Access.

Safety.

Belonging.

Liability.

Supervision.

Welcome.

Control.

Responsibility.

Each word had its own way of finding the fence.

David saw the fence as a failure of design. It was a boundary without coherence, a line that neither welcomed nor protected well. Its patched sections testified to unresolved use. Its bent wire revealed a building problem before the building existed: people were already moving through space in ways the official plan had not understood.

Grace saw the fence as responsibility made visible. The city owned the property. The city would be accountable if something happened there. A broken fence was not only rust and wire. It was exposure. It was a question the city might one day have to answer in a meeting, in a claim, in a budget review, or in a newspaper article.

Priya saw the fence as a test of community trust. The project had promised participation, but the fence kept asking what participation meant. Were people being invited to decorate a process that remained unchanged, or were they being trusted enough to alter the proposal itself? The fence revealed whether engagement had become a ritual or a relationship.

Jade saw the fence as exclusion and access at the same time. It kept people out. It let people in. It marked the difference between being officially welcomed and finding a way through anyway. For her, the hole in the fence was not a design feature, a liability category, or a facilitation challenge. It was the place where the official world had failed to notice belonging already happening.

The same object.

Four partial truths.

For a while, the project had tried to decide which truth mattered most. Was the fence mainly unsafe? Mainly exclusionary? Mainly irresponsible? Mainly evidence of use? Mainly a symbol? Mainly a problem?

The more carefully they looked, the harder it became to answer.

The fence was not one thing.

That was why it had followed them from the beginning.

### The Problem With a Single View

At the next working session, Priya taped a large photograph of the fence to the wall.

It was not the clean aerial photo from city hall or the polished rendering David had prepared for council. It was a plain photograph taken on an ordinary afternoon. The chain-link leaned slightly. One patch was newer than the rest. Grass had grown unevenly along the base. The bent section near the worn path was visible if you knew where to look. In the background, beyond the lot, the apartment buildings stood in pale light.

Priya did not begin with an agenda.

Instead, she handed out index cards.

"Write what you see," she said.

David looked at her. "Literally?"

"Literally first," Priya said. "Then otherwise."

Grace smiled faintly at that.

For a few minutes, the room was quiet.

David wrote: *patched fence, weak point, informal passage, poor edge condition, unclear circulation.*

Grace wrote: *property boundary, liability exposure, uncontrolled access, maintenance failure, public responsibility.*

Jade wrote: *the way in, bad patch, adults pretending not to see, the spot where Marcus cut his hand last winter, where people already go.*

Priya wrote: *trust problem, participation test, place where the project keeps telling the truth.*

They taped the cards beneath the photograph.

No card was complete.

No card was false.

That was the unsettling part.

If David's card had been the only card, the project would have treated the fence as a design failure. It might have produced something cleaner, safer, and more coherent while still missing the meaning of the path.

If Grace's card had been the only card, the project would have treated the fence as a risk problem. It might have produced something defensible while still confusing responsibility with control.

If Jade's card had been the only card, the project would have treated the fence as an access problem. It might have protected belonging while underestimating the labor required to make belonging safe, durable, and public.

If Priya's card had been the only card, the project would have treated the fence as a process problem. It might have preserved conversation while delaying the harder decisions that conversation had to serve.

Each card saw something the others needed.

Each card missed something the others protected.

The photograph did not resolve the disagreement. It made the incompleteness visible.

David stood with his arms folded, reading the cards.

"I don't think I disagree with any of these," he said.

Jade glanced over. "That doesn't mean you agree with them."

"No," David said. "It doesn't."

Grace looked at the photograph. "Maybe that's the point."

Priya waited.

Grace continued, slower than usual. "We keep acting like one description should eventually win. But maybe the fence is exactly the kind of thing that has to remain described more than one way."

Jade looked skeptical. "That sounds like something adults say right before doing whatever they were already going to do."

"It can be," Grace said. "But I don't think it has to be."

David looked at the cards again.

"The design still has to become specific," he said.

"Yes," Grace said.

"The budget still has limits."

"Yes."

"The entrance still has to be somewhere."

"Yes."

Jade crossed her arms. "And if it's in the wrong place, all the descriptions in the world won't fix that."

"No," Priya said. "They won't."

For a moment no one spoke.

That was the new difficulty.

They were no longer pretending that understanding was enough. They knew better now. The project still had to make decisions. A building could not be constructed out of plural interpretations. A budget could not fund every meaning equally. A fence could not remain both open and closed in every sense.

But neither could the project return to the comfort of one summary.

The work was not to avoid judgment.

The work was to judge while remembering that judgment remained partial.

### Trusting What We Cannot See

Trust is often imagined as confidence in another person's character.

That matters.

We trust people when we believe they are honest, reliable, careful, and unlikely to betray us. We trust people when they keep promises, tell the truth, show up when they say they will, and do not use our vulnerability against us.

But there is another kind of trust that becomes necessary in complex work.

Trust in what another person can see.

Not because they see everything.

Because they see something.

This kind of trust is harder. It does not rest on similarity. It does not require the other person to speak in our preferred language or share our instincts. It does not even require them to be right in every conclusion. It requires the humility to recognize that our own position has limits and that another person's partial view may contain information our own view cannot generate.

David had to trust that Jade knew something about the lot his model could not know.

Jade had to trust that David saw constraints that were not simply excuses.

Priya had to trust that conflict could reveal information her summaries would have hidden.

Grace had to trust that public accountability was not weakened by including more realities, even when those realities made the project harder to defend in the short term.

None of them trusted perfectly.

That would have made the story false.

They trusted unevenly, reluctantly, with questions, with irritation, with old habits still close at hand. David still bristled when someone dismissed design as aesthetics. Grace still tensed when people treated budget limits as moral failure. Priya still wanted to turn conflict into language everyone could live with. Jade still watched for signs that the adults were translating her out of the main decision.

But trust had begun to change shape.

It no longer meant believing that another person had the answer.

It meant believing that the answer would be worse without what that person could see.

### Saturation Diving

Few forms of work make human dependence as visible as saturation diving.

Saturation divers descend to extreme depths to repair, inspect, weld, or maintain underwater infrastructure. The environment is hostile to ordinary human life. Pressure changes what the body can survive. Breathing gas must be carefully managed. Decompression can take days. A mistake in communication, timing, equipment, or procedure can become catastrophic. No single diver, supervisor, technician, medic, engineer, or vessel operator sees enough to keep the operation safe alone.

The diver in the water sees what no one else can see directly: the condition of the structure, the feel of the current, the visibility, the difficulty of moving hands and tools in the actual environment. The supervisor on the surface sees a different reality: time, depth, breathing mix, procedural sequence, communications, risk thresholds, and the whole operation unfolding across instruments, voices, and plans. Technicians monitor life-support systems. Engineers understand the structure being repaired. Medical staff understand the body under pressure. The team depends on each perspective because the environment is too complex, too dangerous, and too distributed for one viewpoint to be sufficient.[^c8-saturation-diving]

Trust in that setting cannot mean casual warmth or equal confidence about everything. It cannot mean the diver ignores the supervisor, or the supervisor ignores the diver, or the engineer ignores what the diver reports because the drawing says something else should be true.

The work requires disciplined trust in partial perspectives. The diver's report from the water must matter. The supervisor's view of timing and gas must matter. The engineer's warning about forces not visible in the moment must matter.

No perspective is complete. That is why the team exists.

Saturation diving depends on procedures, checklists, communication protocols, redundancy, training, and role clarity. Those structures do not eliminate trust—they make trust usable when no one can pretend that one person sees enough.[^c8-high-reliability]

The pressures are different at a community center.

But the pattern is recognizable.

A person inside one reality reports what others cannot see from the surface of their own.

The question is whether the report is trusted enough to matter.

### The Swiss Cheese Lesson

Safety researcher James Reason's Swiss Cheese Model offers another way to think about incompleteness.

In the model, complex systems are protected by multiple layers of defense. Each layer is imperfect. Each has holes. A bad outcome occurs when the holes in multiple layers line up, allowing a hazard to pass through every defense.[^c8-swiss-cheese]

The metaphor is often used in safety-critical fields to explain why failures rarely arise from a single mistake. They emerge when multiple partial weaknesses align.

The model is usually applied to systems, procedures, and defenses.

But it also helps explain why perspective matters.

Every perspective has holes.

David's caught problems others missed—incoherent circulation, poor durability, unclear entry sequences—but had holes around lived use and informal belonging. Grace's caught funding risk, operating obligations, and liability exposure—but had holes around how defensibility could become exclusion. Priya's caught social trust, participation, and coalition fragility—but had holes around consensus hiding unresolved meaning. Jade's caught the actual path, the social meaning of entrance, and the difference between inclusion and appendix—but had holes around cost, staffing, and political accountability.

Each perspective protected against some failures and allowed others through. The danger was arranging the project as if one perspective did not.

Trust across difference does not remove the holes. It helps prevent them from lining up unnoticed. The community center needed layered sight, not agreement alone.

### The Limits of Each Protector

What followed had revealed what each perspective protected.

By now, the project had begun to see the limits of those protections.

David's protection of design integrity mattered. Without it, the center could become a shapeless response to every request, a building that looked inclusive in meeting notes and failed in daily use. But design integrity alone could become distance. It could make actual use seem like interference. It could mistake a clean sequence for a humane one.

Grace's protection of accountability mattered. Without it, the project could become a promise nobody knew how to sustain. Programs could be announced without staffing. Entrances could be opened without responsibility. Public money could be spent on hopes that collapsed after the first difficult year. But accountability alone could become risk avoidance. It could make the safest project the one least capable of belonging to anyone.

Priya's protection of community trust mattered. Without it, the project could fracture into accusation and withdrawal. People would stop showing up. The process would lose the relationships it needed before it reached a decision. But trust-holding alone could become smoothing. It could keep the room together by hiding the truths the room needed to face.

Jade's protection of belonging mattered. Without it, the project could become a place designed for people who had been kept outside the design of it. It could replace the hole in the fence with a beautiful entrance that felt like a permission slip. But belonging alone could underestimate the cost of making welcome durable. It could treat every boundary as exclusion even when some boundaries made shared use possible.

Each protection was real.

Each could become distorted when isolated.

That was the central discovery.

The problem was not that one perspective had been wrong and another had been right.

The problem was that each perspective became less trustworthy when it stood alone.

### The Meeting With Two Plans

Grace asked for two access models.

She did not call them Plan A and Plan B. She knew that would make the meeting feel like a vote before it became a judgment. Instead, she labeled them by the problem each tried hardest to solve.

*Model One: Clear Public Entry.*

*Model Two: Dual Approach.*

David prepared both.

Model One preserved the street-side entrance as the primary public face, with improved sidewalks, lighting, and a formal route from the apartments around the perimeter. It was cleaner, less expensive, easier to staff, easier to explain, and closer to the original budget assumptions.

Model Two kept the street entrance but added a secondary public approach from the worn path into a shared courtyard visible from the lobby. It cost more. It required clearer hours, lighting choices, staff protocols, and maintenance planning. It also treated the path as part of the building's public reality rather than a problem at the edge.

Grace prepared cost ranges for both models.

Priya prepared a meeting format that did not ask people to choose too quickly.

Jade prepared questions.

That mattered.

She did not arrive only with objections. She came with a folded sheet of paper and a pen.

The meeting began with David explaining the two models. He did not hide his preference entirely, but he did something Jade noticed: he described the strengths of each before describing the risks. He did not call Model One the responsible choice and Model Two the responsive choice. He did not call one clean and the other complicated, though everyone could see the temptation.

Grace followed with numbers.

"Model One has lower construction cost and lower operating complexity," she said. "Model Two has higher upfront cost and creates additional staffing questions, but it may reduce other risks—continued informal entry, perceived exclusion, and possible conflict around after-school access."

Jade looked up at the phrase *other risks*.

Grace had said it without ceremony.

But it changed something.

The risks Jade saw had entered Grace's language without becoming decoration.

Priya asked the group to begin not with preference but with what each model made visible and invisible.

The question slowed the room.

A neighbor said Model One made safety visible but belonging less visible.

A teacher said Model Two made student access visible but staffing less settled.

David said Model One preserved clarity but risked pretending the site began at the street. Model Two responded to actual use but created a more complex building edge.

Grace said Model One was easier to defend financially but might be harder to defend publicly if the project appeared to ignore youth input. Model Two was harder to defend financially but might better serve the project's stated purpose.

Jade said Model One felt like adults had listened and then returned to the beginning. Model Two felt closer, but only if the secondary approach was not treated as conditional belonging.

No one solved it in that moment.

But the room had changed.

They were no longer asking which perspective would win.

They were asking what each model failed to see.

### Trust as a Way of Seeing More

Trust is often treated as a moral virtue. It can be. But trust also has an epistemic function—it helps people see more than they could see alone.

This does not mean trusting everyone equally or abandoning discernment. Some claims are false. Some perspectives are distorted by fear, ideology, self-interest, inattention, or power. Trust across difference requires judgment. But judgment without trust becomes trapped inside itself.

If David trusts only design expertise, he will miss realities that do not arrive as drawings. If Grace trusts only what can be defended in formal terms, she will miss harms visible only after people withdraw. If Priya trusts only the feeling of connection, she will miss disagreement hidden beneath warmth. If Jade trusts only lived experience, she will miss constraints that determine whether a place can remain open after the first difficult year.

Each person needs the others not because the others are complete but because each incompleteness differs. That is the beginning of epistemic interdependence—we need others to reveal the world our own position filters out.

### Core Principle

Every perspective is incomplete.

Trust becomes necessary because human perception is partial. We need other people not because they see the whole, but because they may see what our own perspective misses.

### Back to the Fence

Near the end of the meeting, Priya returned to the photograph taped to the wall.

The index cards still hung beneath it.

Patched fence.

Property boundary.

The way in.

Trust problem.

She added a new card beneath the others.

*No single card is enough.*

Jade read it and looked at Priya. "That sounds like a summary."

Priya laughed softly. "It is."

"Are we allowed to disagree with it?"

"I hope so."

David took a pen and added another card.

*The design has to remember all of them.*

Grace added one after him.

*The budget does too.*

Jade waited.

Then she wrote on her own card and taped it slightly crooked beneath the photograph.

*The entrance should know we were already here.*

No one moved it.

The fence in the photograph remained ugly, patched, and unresolved.

But the wall beneath it now held more than competing claims. It held a record of how the project had learned to see. Not completely. Not finally. Not without risk.

Enough to know that one view would not be enough.

Enough to keep the others present.
