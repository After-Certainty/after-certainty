# **Trusting People Like Us**

> *Why do we reach for people who already understand us?*

David Okafor kept a model of the community center on a table in his studio.

It was not large—roughly the size of a bread box, built from basswood and matte board, the kind of model architects make when they want to think with their hands. The roof lifted off. Interior walls could be repositioned. A strip of chain-link, cut from a hobby-shop mesh, ran along the back edge of the lot where the real fence still stood, sagging in places where teenagers had pushed through it for years.

David had been revising the entrance for a week.

In the full-scale building, people would enter from the street side. That was where the sidewalk met the property line. It was where signage would go, where lighting would fall at dusk, where parents would pause before sending children inside. On paper the logic was clean. In the model David could test it: approach, threshold, lobby, circulation. He moved a tiny paper figure through the sequence and felt the design settle into place.

He called an architect he had worked with before—a woman who understood how he thought about durability, maintenance, the slow wear buildings accumulate when they are actually used. They spoke for forty minutes. She asked good questions. She noticed a circulation problem he had already corrected. By the end of the conversation David felt clearer, not because she had challenged him, but because she had confirmed the direction he was already moving.

"We can workshop the details," he told Priya later, when she asked whether the design was ready to share more widely. He said it as if the workshop would be about refinement, not direction.

### Familiar Circles

Priya was organizing the project's first round of community meetings. She made lists of people to invite: teachers she knew from the school where she worked, parents from the PTA, a few neighbors who had spoken up at city forums in the past. She trusted these people. They showed up. They listened. They cared about the neighborhood in ways she recognized. When she imagined a successful meeting, she imagined a room full of people like them—concerned, practical, willing to stay past eight o'clock if the conversation mattered.

She had also heard about the teenagers who used the lot—the ones who cut through the fence, who knew the empty asphalt better than any surveyor did. Priya thought about inviting them. She even wrote a name on a sticky note and left it on the corner of her desk for two days. Then she removed it. The first meeting needed to be manageable. She told herself there would be time later for voices that might disrupt the room before the room had learned to hold together. It was a reasonable choice. It was also a familiar one.

Grace Liu was not in those rooms yet, at least not as a participant. She was at her desk downtown, reading budget memos and liability guidelines, tracing the approval path the project would have to survive. She had managed public buildings before. She knew which assumptions killed projects quietly—maintenance costs nobody wanted to discuss, timelines that looked generous until winter arrived, designs that photographed well and aged badly. She had watched promising civic projects become scandals when year-three costs appeared and nobody could explain why nobody had planned for them. Defensibility was not abstraction for Grace. It was the difference between a building that still served people a decade later and a ribbon-cutting that became an embarrassment. When she needed clarity, she called colleagues who had survived the same meetings she was heading toward. They spoke in terms that rarely appeared on posters or public renderings: contingency funds, lifecycle costs, liability exposure, maintenance obligations. They spoke a shared language, and that saved time.

Jade Calloway was at the lot. She and two friends sat on the cracked asphalt near the fence, sharing headphones, watching the late afternoon move across the empty space. They knew every gap in the chain-link. They knew which section the city patched last year and which patch had already been peeled back. They knew where the shade lasted longest in August, which corner stayed dry after rain, which stretch of asphalt was smooth enough to skate across if nobody had left broken glass there first.

Adults talked about the lot as if it were waiting to become something. Jade experienced it as something already in use. It was not beautiful. It was not safe in any official sense. It did not have programs, signage, lighting, or permission. But it was one of the few places in the neighborhood where no one immediately told them to leave.

That was what the hole in the fence meant to her. Not vandalism exactly. Not even rebellion most days. Access. A way into a place the official world had neglected but not released.

None of this was sinister. David was not hiding the model out of contempt. Priya was not excluding people on purpose. Grace was not burying the project in bureaucracy. Jade was not refusing to participate. Everyone was doing what experienced people do when a complicated thing begins: they reached for perspectives that had helped before.

### Homophily in Ordinary Life

This is one of the quietest forms trust takes. We trust people who resemble us—not only in identity, but in experience, vocabulary, instinct, expectation. Sociologists have a name for this pattern: homophily, the tendency of similar people to connect more easily than dissimilar people.[^c1-homophily] The word sounds technical, but the experience is ordinary. A shared reference lands before it has to be explained. A concern sounds plausible because it sounds familiar. A suggestion feels workable because the person suggesting it has solved problems you recognize.

### Where Similarity Helps

The pattern appears wherever coordination matters. Military units develop cohesion through shared training and shared codes of response; under pressure, people need to trust not only one another's intentions but one another's instincts. Professional networks circulate work among people who already speak the same dialect of competence; a recommendation often means, "This person understands the standards without needing them translated from the beginning."

Referral hiring may be the clearest everyday version. A manager needs to fill a role quickly and asks a trusted colleague whether she knows anyone reliable. The colleague recommends someone she has worked with before—someone whose judgment already had a trusted person standing behind it. Often, the hire works out. The manager saves time. The team avoids the uncertainty of starting from zero with a stranger whose standards remain unknown.

But the same shortcut narrows who gets seen. The recommended candidate enters through a door already open. Other candidates remain outside—not because they lack ability, but because no one with existing credibility has vouched for them yet. Over time an organization can fill with capable people who resemble one another in background, network, and instinct. Coordination improves. The range of what gets noticed quietly shrinks. In each case similarity lowers the cost of working together.[^c1-trust-coordination] Less translation is required. Fewer misunderstandings need repair. Trust begins where prediction becomes easier.

There is nothing fake about this advantage. Similarity genuinely helps. Projects move faster when people do not have to rebuild basic trust at every step. David's conversation with his colleague saved weeks of rework. Priya's meeting list produced a room where people were willing to speak. Grace's calls prevented a budget assumption that would have collapsed later. Trusted circles are not worthless. Often they are exactly what a project needs to begin.

### When Familiarity Feels Complete

The trouble is what ease can quietly suggest. When coordination becomes simple, understanding can begin to feel finished.[^c1-kahneman-ease] David's model looked coherent because the people reviewing it shared his assumptions about entrances, flow, and form. Priya's meetings felt productive because disagreement stayed within a range everyone already knew how to manage. Grace's warnings sounded responsible because they matched the institutional grammar she and her colleagues had learned to hear as serious. Each person was seeing something real. Each was also seeing through relationships that made some realities easier to notice than others.[^c1-larsen-cohesion]

The lot behind the fence was still there while all of this happened. David had represented it in miniature, but he had not yet watched teenagers move through the real space. Priya had invited people who cared about the neighborhood, but not the people who used the empty asphalt the way Jade did. Grace was protecting the project's future, but mostly in conversations with others trained to think about futures the same way she did. Similarity had made the early work possible. It had also made it easier to believe the early work was enough.

When everyone in the room understands quickly, the room can feel full. A coalition can feel warm while missing the person who knows where the fence has been cut. A design can look resolved while the actual users of a place are entering somewhere else entirely.

### Core Principle

Similarity lowers coordination costs. David, Priya, and Grace were not wrong to begin inside circles that already worked. Those circles helped the project move.

The question was whether they had shown enough.

### Back to the Model

David turned the model slightly on the table and studied the street-side entrance one more time. It still made sense to him. It would make sense to the people he already knew how to listen to. The tiny paper figure moved cleanly through the sequence because David moved it there. What he could not yet see was whether the entrance made sense to the life already moving through the lot—to the worn path along the fence, the patched wire, the teenagers who had been using the place long before the city announced a community center would rise there.

That question had not failed to arrive because anyone was careless. It had not arrived yet because similarity had done its job too well.

Similarity had made coordination easier. The work ahead would test whether it had made understanding complete.

[^c1-homophily]: McPherson, Smith-Lovin, and Cook, "Birds of a Feather."

[^c1-trust-coordination]: Fukuyama, *Trust*, ch. 3; Svendsen, *Trust*, "The Origins of Trust," pp. 26–35 — on social trust lowering transaction and control costs relative to formal oversight.

[^c1-kahneman-ease]: Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* — on cognitive ease, prediction, and the feeling that a familiar account is complete.

[^c1-larsen-cohesion]: Larsen, *The Rise and Fall of Social Cohesion*, ch. 1 — on trust and perceptions of a meritocratic social middle versus an untrustworthy bottom.
