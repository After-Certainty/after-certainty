# **Author's Note**

This book grew out of a long attempt to understand something that felt
increasingly difficult to name.

Across religious, political, legal, and organizational settings, I kept
encountering institutions that were neither collapsing nor
reforming—institutions staffed by sincere, capable people, yet
repeatedly associated with harm that never quite resolved. The usual
explanations—corruption, hypocrisy, bad faith—felt incomplete. They
explained some cases, but not the ones that were hardest to leave or
hardest to criticize.

This book is not an argument against authority, belief, or institutions
themselves. It is an attempt to describe a structural condition that
emerges when legitimacy no longer has a clear endpoint—when
accountability becomes something that can be deferred indefinitely
without appearing irresponsible.

My aim here is clarity, not accusation. The patterns described in this
book are not unique to any one tradition, and they do not require malice
to persist. In fact, they often survive precisely because they feel
morally serious from the inside.

Nothing in these pages requires the reader to abandon their commitments.
It asks only that we look carefully at what stability can conceal, what
harm can be made survivable, and what we trade away when continuity
becomes the highest good.
