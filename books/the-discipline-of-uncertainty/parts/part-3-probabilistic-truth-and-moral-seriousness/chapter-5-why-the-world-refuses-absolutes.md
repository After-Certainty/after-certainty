# **Chapter 5**

## **Why the World Refuses Absolutes**

A city plans for a flood that may not arrive this decade. The plan is not
built on certainty. It is built on **distributions**: historical rainfall,
climate variance, infrastructure age, tail risk. The responsible question is
not "will it flood?" but "what harm do we accept if we prepare too little?"

A hospital ethics committee faces a similar shape: a treatment may help most
patients and harm a few; withholding may protect some and fail others. The
committee does not escape morality by saying "it depends." It **enters**
morality with eyes open—naming populations, harms, and uncertainties without
pretending a single sentence can end the tradeoff.

> Reality presents distributions, not binaries.

### **1. Counterfactuals and tail risk**

Most consequential decisions live in **counterfactual space**: we will never
observe the road not taken. **Tail risk**—rare, high-harm events—dominates
ethics in medicine, finance, and climate. Tail risk makes absolutes tempting
("zero tolerance") and often counterproductive without careful design.

Zero tolerance can feel morally pure while shifting harm into hidden corners:
unreported errors, underground markets, silent suffering. Probabilistic
seriousness asks where harm actually moves when a rule is absolute on paper.

### **2. Minimizing harm versus maximizing truth**

Real decisions are often about **minimizing harm under constraint**, not
maximizing a clean boolean "rightness." A hospital triages. A central bank
balances employment and inflation. A parent chooses imperfect safety.

Moral seriousness includes choosing under tradeoffs, not pretending tradeoffs
do not exist. Seriousness also requires **naming who bears the downside** if
the bet is wrong—patients, workers, communities—not hiding behind averages.

### **3. Being right versus being responsible**

There is a difference between **being right** in a narrow predictive sense
and **being responsible** in a moral sense. You can be right that a risk is
low and still be irresponsible if the harm of being wrong is catastrophic and
preventable.

Responsibility uses probability; it is not defeated by probability. Judges,
physicians, and engineers all live here. The public often punishes them for
living here unless institutions teach **why** partial knowledge still demands
action.

### **4. Why absolutes are politically seductive**

Absolutes promise moral clarity in public speech. Distributions sound like
waffling. Leaders who speak in distributions need institutions that reward
restraint—a rare configuration.

When restraint is unrewarded, leaders perform certainty. Audiences then learn
to confuse performance with competence. The loop strengthens prophecy culture.

### **5. Discipline at the pivot**

Discipline lives at the pivot where **truth is partial** and **action is
required**. It refuses false prophecy while refusing paralysis.

At that pivot, good speech sounds like: "Given what we know, this is the best
available allocation of risk; we will watch these indicators; if they move,
we will revise." That is not relativism. It is **accountable partiality**.

### **6. Expected value is not the whole of ethics**

Decision theory offers expected value: multiply outcomes by probabilities and
choose. It is a powerful tool. It is not a moral exhaust system.

Some harms are not commensurable in a single ledger: dignity, coercion, betrayal,
structural injustice. Probabilistic seriousness does not require pretending
everything converts to numbers. It requires **explicit non-comparison** when
comparison would be dishonest—naming which values cannot be traded, and why,
while still acting under constraint.

Ethics committees, courts, and leaders do this daily, sometimes clumsily.
Clumsiness is preferable to false arithmetic that laundered a hard choice
into a fake certainty.

is moral relativism in disguise.
