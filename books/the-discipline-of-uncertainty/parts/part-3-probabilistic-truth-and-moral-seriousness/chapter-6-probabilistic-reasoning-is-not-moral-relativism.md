# **Chapter 6**

## **Probabilistic Reasoning Is Not Moral Relativism**

A prosecutor weighs evidence short of certainty. A physician treats before
diagnosis is final. A parent removes a hazard before injury occurs. None of
these actors is saying "anything goes." They are saying **accountability
continues under incomplete knowledge**.

An organizational ombuds investigates harassment complaints where witnesses
disagree and records are incomplete. The ombuds cannot wait for courtroom
certainty to recommend interim protections. Probabilistic seriousness means
**acting on weighted concern** while preserving fair process—not declaring
guilt as destiny, not waiting for perfection as excuse.

> Uncertainty does not erase accountability.

### **1. Why "it depends" is not an excuse**

**"It depends"** becomes an excuse when it ends inquiry: no data, no
commitment, no cost for being wrong. Discipline uses "it depends" as the
start of inquiry: depends on what variables, with what stakes, under what
constraints?

The difference is whether uncertainty triggers care or evasion. Relativism
ends judgment. Probabilism **structures** judgment under partial sight.

### **2. Responsibility without certainty**

**Responsibility without certainty** is ordinary in serious professions.
Standards of proof, safety factors, and precautionary thresholds exist
because waiting for absolutes is itself a decision—with consequences.

Moral seriousness is not certainty. It is willingness to act and be judged
when certainty is unavailable. Seriousness also requires **traceability**:
assumptions written down, harms named, revision conditions stated in advance
so later updates are not treated as scandal.

### **3. Moral seriousness under incomplete knowledge**

Under incomplete knowledge, seriousness shows up as:

- transparent assumptions,
- revisable claims,
- attention to who bears harm if wrong,
- and refusal to launder prophecy as expertise.

In leadership, seriousness sounds boring and is load-bearing: "We are 70%
confident in this diagnosis of the problem; we are 40% confident this fix
works; we will measure these three signals weekly."

### **4. Relativism versus probabilism**

**Relativism** says there is no shared evaluative ground. **Probabilism** says
ground exists but claims are partial. Discipline rejects relativism. It also
rejects performers of false certainty.

The test is conduct: does partial knowledge still produce **obligations**—to
protect, to disclose, to repair, to revisit? If yes, you are in probabilistic
seriousness. If no, you are likely in evasion wearing statistical language.

### **5. Public speech under distributions**

Public leaders often face a cruel choice: sound certain or sound weak.
Discipline seeks a third path: **clear about stakes and distributions**,
clear about what would change the assessment, clear about responsibility if
wrong.

That path is harder than slogans. It is more honest than prophecy. It also
builds institutions that can survive being wrong without collapsing into
total verdicts or defensive absolutes.

### **6. Standards as moral architecture**

Professions encode probabilistic seriousness into **standards**: reasonable
doubt, preponderance of evidence, safety factors, duty of care. These are not
moods. They are **architecture**—shared agreements about how much uncertainty
society will tolerate before action and blame attach.

When leaders bypass architecture with destiny speech ("we know how this ends"),
they may feel morally decisive. They often erode the very standards that let
a society punish real negligence without punishing honest revision.

Discipline treats standards as part of the moral world, not as technicalities
beneath "real" conviction. Conviction without standards becomes performance.
Standards without conviction become bureaucracy. Serious institutions need both.
