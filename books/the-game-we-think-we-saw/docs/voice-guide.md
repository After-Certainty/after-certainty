# The Game We Think We Saw — Voice and Method Guide

## Purpose

Guide prose decisions so chapters feel sports-grounded, essayistic, and precise rather than managerial, prosecutorial, or slogan-driven.

## Signature movement

The prose should generally:

1. begin with an ordinary sports observation,
2. notice a tension hidden inside it,
3. widen into a structural or philosophical question,
4. test the pattern through real sporting cases,
5. compress the insight into a memorable but defensible line,
6. introduce a counterexample or boundary condition,
7. return to the original physical image.

The exact sequence may vary. Do not make every chapter mechanically identical.

## Tone

- reflective
- precise
- humane
- essayistic
- intellectually serious without sounding academic
- morally attentive without prosecutorial certainty
- accessible to sports readers without simplifying the conceptual argument
- patient enough to let scenes reveal the idea before naming it

## Paragraph style

- Favor developed paragraphs over single-sentence staccato sequences.
- Use short paragraphs selectively for emphasis.
- Avoid repetitive rhetorical fragments.
- Do not overuse “This is not…” constructions.
- Avoid stacking several compression lines together.
- Let one strong sentence carry a section rather than producing a slogan every paragraph.

## Sports-first rule

Stay inside the game.

Do not add explicit translations such as:

- “The same thing happens in business.”
- “This applies to political leadership.”
- “Leaders in every organization should…”
- “Whether on the field or in the boardroom…”
- “The lesson for executives is…”

The reader should recognize broader applications without being escorted to them.

## The scoreboard must not become a villain

Do not claim that the scoreboard lies or that objective outcomes do not matter.

The governing distinction is:

> The scoreboard is not wrong. It is complete only about the question it was built to answer.

Measurement, replay, analytics, rules, and authority solve real problems. The book examines the remainder created when their legitimate answers are treated as total explanations.

## Avoid easy binaries

Do not reduce the material to:

- data versus humanity
- athletes versus institutions
- good officials versus bad officials
- courage versus self-care
- protester versus patriot
- demanding coach versus compassionate coach
- stars versus systems

Each chapter must include a real complication or counterexample.

## Research voice discipline

Avoid unsupported phrases such as:

- “everyone believed”
- “the public decided”
- “people saw”
- “it was widely understood”

Replace them with documented and appropriately scoped claims. Distinguish documented events, contemporary interpretation, retrospective interpretation, and author analysis (see [`research-plan.md`](research-plan.md)).

## Approved voice benchmarks

Use these lines as conceptual and tonal anchors, not phrases to repeat excessively:

- A result can be final while its cause, legitimacy, cost, and meaning remain unsettled.
- The scoreboard is not wrong. It is complete only about the question it was built to answer.
- Leadership increases the capacity around the leader. Dependency increases the number of situations only the leader can save.
- The machine settles the call only after people have settled what the machine should mean.
- Authority earns trust not by eliminating judgment, but by making judgment inhabitable.
- A victory can prove that a method produced a result. It cannot prove that the method deserved to.
- The audience can see the size of the moment. Only the athlete can feel the condition of the body entering it.
- Meaning leaves the speaker, but it does not enter a world without evidence.

## Weak habits vs better habits

### Weak (framework-first)

“This chapter is about measurement. Sports analytics shows that metrics compress reality.”

### Better (observation-first)

Begin with a box score, a possession chart, or a role whose value disappears in the official record, then let the compression problem appear.

### Weak (off-field escort)

“Whether in sports or in the boardroom, leaders confuse outcomes with explanations.”

### Better (sports-first)

Stay with the play, the coaching staff, the locker room, or the ruling; leave the boardroom unmentioned.

### Weak (scoreboard villain)

“The scoreboard lies and tricking fans into thinking sports are fair.”

### Better (incomplete instrument)

Treat the scoreboard as correctly answering a narrow question whose authority expands past its design.
