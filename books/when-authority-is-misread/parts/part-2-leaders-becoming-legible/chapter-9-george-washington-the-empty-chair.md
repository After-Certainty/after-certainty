# Chapter 9: George Washington — The Empty Chair

Someone rises from the head of the table and leaves the chair
empty.

Everyone notices. For a moment the room reorganizes around the
absence. Conversation slows. Authority has not vanished; it has
become visible as a choice rather than a fixture. Later, when the
transfer of places has become familiar, people forget how easily
the person who stood could have remained seated—how little the
furniture itself would have objected, how ready many in the room
would have been to call permanence wisdom. Habit turns the empty
chair into scenery. Scenery does not warn.

This ordinary conversion of refusal into invisibility is the
pressure that shaped how **George Washington** has been
remembered.[^c9-chernow]

---

Washington appears in American civic memory as inevitable: steady,
disinterested, above ambition. Authority, in that telling, behaved
properly because the man holding it was properly formed. He did
not seize power because he did not want it. He did not overstay
because temptation never seriously pressed him. The retrospective
clarity is misleading. During his life his authority was neither
uncontested nor secure. The Continental Army teetered repeatedly
toward collapse for want of food, pay, clothing, and congressional
coherence. Officers mutinied over arrears and recognition.
Soldiers deserted. Civilian governance under the Articles of
Confederation was weak, improvised, and distrusted by the very men
asked to bleed for it. The idea that the new nation might revert
to monarchy, dictatorship, or some armed patronage system was not
fringe speculation. It was spoken among people who had just
finished fighting one king and did not trust the vacuum left
behind.[^c9-ellis]

Military command made Washington uniquely capable of converting
wartime authority into permanent rule, and many around him assumed
he eventually would. Popularity plus an unpaid army is a classical
recipe for caesarism. At Newburgh in 1783, discontented officers
circulated anonymous appeals urging the army to refuse
demobilization until Congress satisfied claims for pay—an
atmosphere in which a commander willing to ride that anger might
have turned grievance into political force. Washington confronted
the officers, repudiated the conspiracy’s logic, and redirected
loyalty toward civilian authority rather than toward himself as
remedy.[^c9-newburgh] The scene is often told as pure virtue. It
was also institutional triage under visible danger: if the army
taught itself that the legislature could be coerced by refusal to
stand down, the republic would have been born as a garrison with a
civilian costume. Shortly afterward he resigned his commission
before Congress. The resignation was not a casual exit. It was a
public theater of subordination—the most powerful military figure
in the republic placing his sword back under legislative control.
Monarchy anxieties did not disappear because of one ceremony. They
receded because the person who could most easily have answered
them with a crown declined, repeatedly, and because other people
chose to treat the decline as precedent rather than as eccentric
humility.

Between resignation and presidency lay the unsettled years of the
Confederation: debt, commercial conflict among states, Shays’
Rebellion as a regional alarm about order and property, and a
widening conviction among nationalists that a stronger frame was
required if independence were not to dissolve into jealous
provinces. Washington’s private correspondence in those years
shows irritation with weakness and fear of humiliation—not the
serene indifference of a man untouched by ambition for the
country’s standing. Wanting the country to hold is not the same as
wanting to own it. The distinction matters because later myth
collapses desire for national strength into proof that personal
appetite had fled. He could want power for the union and still
refuse to make himself the union’s permanent center. That
combination is rarer than either hunger alone or purity alone.

The Constitutional Convention of 1787 drew legitimacy in part from
Washington’s presence as presiding officer. His silence in debate
did not erase his weight; it concentrated it. Delegates argued
under a gaze that represented both wartime survival and the fear
of what wartime survival could become if unchecked. When the
presidency was invented, he became its first occupant not as an
abstract republican experiment but as a living answer to the fear
that no one else could hold the office without tearing the country
apart. He accepted a role designed under the shadow of his own
example. That is a peculiar inheritance: institutions built to
survive a founder often begin by needing the founder they claim to
transcend. The chair was built with him already imagined in it—and
then with the hope that after him it would still be leaveable.

---

The presidency tested restraint under conditions different from
military resignation. Governing required affirmative use of power,
not only refusal. Creating departments, managing debt and credit,
managing diplomacy with Britain and France, and suppressing
domestic resistance were not acts of withdrawal. During the
Whiskey Rebellion, Washington used federal force to suppress armed
resistance to excise enforcement in western
Pennsylvania—demonstrating that the republic would coerce
compliance when fiscal authority was challenged.[^c9-whiskey] The
episode is awkward for pure Cincinnatus narratives because it
shows concentrated executive and military capacity deployed
against citizens. Restraint, in this register, was not pacifism.
It was a claim that coercive state power could be used and then
returned to constitutional limit. Any account that isolates his
refusals from his uses of force misunderstands both. An empty
chair later does not cancel a field of arms earlier. It only shows
that the person who could command still answered the question of
permanence with exit.

Cabinet conflict between Hamilton and Jefferson made another kind
of pressure visible: the republic would factionalize while still
learning how to argue without armies. Washington tried to hold the
executive above party while depending on men who were inventing
parties. Neutrality in the European wars, the Jay Treaty, and
public abuse of his administration showed that leaving the
military chair empty did not empty political hatred. Restraint of
tenure coexists with heated office. The myth that forgets
temptation also forgets how noisy the room remained while he still
sat.

He declined a third term. The decision was not required by
constitutional text; it became a norm because he performed it and
successors, for a long interval, treated the performance as
binding. The Farewell Address warned against sectional faction,
foreign entanglements, and the corruption of public spirit by
party passion—advice that later generations would quote
selectively while ignoring how contingent its authority
was.[^c9-farewell] The transfer of power to John Adams in 1797 was
a non-event that later became mythology: power moved without
inheritance or coup. Non-events do not compress well while they
happen. Once avoided, they feel inevitable. The temptation that
had been present at Newburgh, at the resignation, at the invention
of the presidency, and at the edge of a third term faded from the
story. What remained was a simplified lesson: that authority, when
held by the right person, naturally limits itself. The effort
required to prevent consolidation dropped out of civic education.
The story became safe.

That lesson cannot survive contact with the republic Washington
actually led. He enslaved people. Mount Vernon’s wealth and the
social world that sustained his public virtue rested on coerced
labor. The household that taught manners of command over human
property also trained the habits of a general and president. He
signed the Fugitive Slave Act of 1793, aligning federal power with
the recovery of people who fled enslavement. His will provided for
the freeing of those he personally enslaved after Martha
Washington’s death—a delayed manumission that registered both a
troubled conscience and a refusal to disrupt the household economy
while it still served him.[^c9-slavery] Historians of slavery and
the early republic have insisted, correctly, that self-restraining
authority at the summit of the state is not separable from the
unrestrained authority exercised over enslaved people within
plantation households and under federal law. Ona Judge’s flight
from the presidential household and the Washingtons’ pursuit of
her return make the contradiction concrete rather than abstract.
The empty chair at the head of the national table does not empty
the cabins. A myth of civic virtue that needs Washington’s
refusals while forgetting the people denied every chair is not
inheritance; it is selective memory dressed as gratitude.

Slavery’s presence in the founding was not a private quirk of one
plantation. It was constitutive of representation, law, and wealth
in the constitutional order Washington helped launch. Three-fifths
enumeration, the slave trade’s delayed restriction, and the
fugitive clause were not footnotes to a liberty project; they were
load-bearing compromises. To praise Washington’s exits from office
while treating those compromises as unfortunate scenery is to
mislocate where authority was most absolute. The president could
leave. The enslaved person in the executive mansion could not.
That asymmetry is part of what “self-restraining authority” meant
in practice: restraint selectively applied at the summit,
domination routinized below.

Indigenous nations encountered another face of the early
state—treaties, war, and land pressure conducted in the name of a
people who voted for expansion more readily than for limit.
Washington’s Indian policy mixed diplomacy, military campaigns,
and a civilizing rhetoric that presumed dispossession as destiny
moderated by procedure. Again the empty chair misleads if it is
the only image retained. A republic can rotate executives and
still prosecute a durable project of seizure. The myth of
self-limitation must be tested against who was limited and who was
taken from.

The early republic was also narrow in membership and thick with
coercive instruments beyond slavery. Citizenship, suffrage,
Indigenous dispossession, and gendered exclusion structured who
could even imagine rising from a head chair. Treaties broken, land
seized, and federal power projected westward were not peripheral
accidents; they were part of how the constitutional order
stabilized for some by destabilizing others. Washington’s
restraint of personal executive permanence coexisted with a state
learning to expand, tax, punish, and exclude. Remembering only the
transfer of presidential power while forgetting those foundations
trains the wrong admiration: admiration for a virtue that never
had to democratize its own table, and for limits that applied
vividly to tenure while applying weakly to domination.

---

Around these decisions a civic mythology hardened. Washington as
Cincinnatus. Washington as proof that character can replace
institutional suspicion. Washington as the man who would
never—rather than the man who repeatedly could have and did not.
Schoolbook memory prefers essence to sequence. What had been a
series of deliberate, high-risk choices became a single trait:
virtue. The compression erased contingency. It collapsed the
difference between *could have taken power* and *would never take
power*. Once restraint is treated as nature rather than choice
under pressure, it becomes portable and misleading. The lesson
shifts from *design constraints that survive ambitious people* to
*find leaders like Washington*. That is precisely the opposite of
what his most serious decisions implied. A politics that hunts for
saints to excuse weak structures will eventually discover that
saints are scarce and that ambition is not. One further
compression completes the lullaby: because he left, American
authority is somehow uniquely inoculated against authoritarian
consolidation. Comparative history does not support that hygiene.
Norms are real and fragile. Myths that treat them as destiny make
them more fragile by discouraging the boring work of
maintenance—statutory limits, legislative oversight, independent
courts, and publics that refuse to confuse loyalty to a person
with loyalty to an office.

His signals were often negative ones. He did not narrate refusal
as continuous moral performance designed to instruct future
centuries in real time. He resigned. He declined. He left. Silence
allowed interpretation—and later allowed myth. Without narrative
reinforcement of danger, audiences inferred meaning from outcome.
Stability arrived; temptation disappeared from view; character
remained as explanation. The Farewell Address tries, late, to put
words around caution. Words travel less reliably than the comfort
of success. Success teaches the wrong pedagogy when it is
remembered as inevitability.

Moral posture emphasized stewardship without entitlement. He
treated authority as temporary. He did not argue that restraint
entitled him to permanent gratitude converted into obedience.
Effectiveness consisted largely of what did not happen: the army
did not become a ruling class; the presidency did not become
lifelong; succession did not require blood; the early republic did
not immediately resolve into the European templates its critics
feared and its imitators half-admired. These absences stabilized a
constitutional order while resisting narration. The paradox is
familiar. Success made the underlying risk look imaginary.
Inheritance then failed in the usual way: later leaders and
publics borrowed the legitimacy of Washington’s example without
renewing the discipline that made the example possible.
Institutions rested on a story of natural virtue while needing
structural checks the story rendered optional. When checks are
treated as insults to character, the empty chair becomes a museum
piece rather than a practice.

Even the vocabulary of “precedent” can mislead. A precedent is a
remembered choice elevated into expectation. Expectations erode
when later actors discover that reverence costs less than
obedience. The wrong lesson is not that restraint never happened.
The wrong lesson is that restraint, once performed by one person,
has been installed in the furniture forever.

A founder can stabilize authority by refusing to consolidate it. A
later public can destabilize judgment by remembering the refusal
as destiny. Looking carefully keeps the temptation visible—and
refuses to detach presidential self-limitation from slavery,
federal coercion, and the narrow republic that called itself the
people. Celebration of peaceful transfer is not canceled by that
refusal. Celebration becomes honest only when it stops pretending
that one man’s exits redeemed an order built on other people’s
captivity and exclusion.

At ordinary scale the pattern is recognizable without allegory. A
board trusts a founder who repeatedly refuses a sweetheart deal;
oversight thins; later successors inherit trust without inheriting
refusal. Non-events taught the group that checking was unnecessary.
Checking was unnecessary only while the person who made checking
feel insulting was still there. Inheritance failure is what happens
when virtue is stored as story instead of renewed as practice and
structure.

*Successful restraint becomes dangerous when people remember the
virtue and forget the temptation.*

Interpretation shapes the moral ledger before judgment begins, but
it does not settle the judgment. Washington is misread when the
empty chair is taken as proof that no one wanted to sit—or that
sitting forever would have been unnatural for anyone properly
formed. He is also misread when later abuses of executive power are
answered only by nostalgia for his character rather than by redesign
of incentives, terms, oversight, and lawful coercion. Character
matters. It does not substitute for architecture. Looking carefully
does not cancel the importance of transfers of power. It keeps
celebration from becoming alibi.

---

The chair is still empty.

It does not prove that the room was free of desire for permanence.
It proves that someone chose to rise. The room notices in the
moment; later the empty chair can harden into scenery.
Civic mythology prefers that hardening because it feels like
safety. Safety of that kind trains a people to hunt for
Washingtons instead of building constraints that do not depend on
finding them—and to praise the empty chair while ignoring who was
never invited to sit, stand, or speak at the table. The chair stays
empty only while someone keeps standing up—or while the table itself
is redesigned so that no single seat can again hold what one person
once chose to leave. Redesign is less flattering than reverence. It
is the only way the empty chair remains a warning rather than a
lullaby.
