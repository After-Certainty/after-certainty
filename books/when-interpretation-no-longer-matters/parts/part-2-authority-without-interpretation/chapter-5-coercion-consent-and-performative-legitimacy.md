# **Chapter 5**

## **Coercion, Consent, and Performative Legitimacy**

Interpretation can cease to matter publicly even while it persists privately.[^c5-ref-1-how-meaning-moves] This chapter examines a form of authority in which legitimacy is enacted through performance and enforced compliance rather than negotiated through persuasion or shared understanding.

In such systems, interpretation does not disappear. It retreats. Meaning remains intelligible, but it no longer alters authority’s standing in public space. Authority persists through visible conformity rather than evaluative agreement.[^c5-ref-2-when-authority-outlives-accountability]

This chapter shows how legitimacy can be maintained when interpretation survives only where it cannot be spoken.[^c5-ref-3-hannah-arendt-the-origins]

## **The Event in Brief**

Joseph Stalin (1878–1953) consolidated power in the Soviet Union during the 1920s and 1930s following the death of Vladimir Lenin. Through political maneuvering, institutional control, and elimination of rivals, he became the central authority within the Communist Party.[^c5-ref-4-stephen-kotkin-stalin-paradoxes]

The 1930s were marked by rapid industrialization, forced collectivization of agriculture, widespread famine, and mass repression. The Great Purge (1936–1938) involved show trials, public confessions, executions, and imprisonment in labor camps.[^c5-ref-5-sheila-fitzpatrick-everyday-stalinism]

Public life became saturated with ritual affirmation: unanimous votes, orchestrated demonstrations, denunciations of “enemies,” and constant symbolic praise of leadership. Official narratives framed repression as defense of the revolution.[^c5-ref-6-arendt-origins-fitzpatrick-everyday]

Despite the visibility of compliance, private diaries, letters, and memoirs later revealed widespread awareness of contradictions, fear, and skepticism.[^c5-ref-7-orlando-figes-the-whisperers]

The system endured for decades. Authority persisted not because it resolved contradiction, but because public dissent carried extreme cost.

## **Leader Focus: Joseph Stalin**

This is a case where public interpretive constraint disappeared while private understanding persisted without consequence.

Stalin’s authority did not depend on persuading the population that policies were coherent or beneficial. It depended on ensuring public behavior aligned with official narratives.[^c5-ref-4-stephen-kotkin-stalin-paradoxes]

Citizens often understood failures and dangers clearly. Interpretation persisted privately. What changed was the cost of expression. Public disagreement no longer functioned as interpretation; it functioned as self-exposure.[^c5-ref-5-sheila-fitzpatrick-everyday-stalinism]

Rituals of affirmation—votes, denunciations, confessions, scripted praise—became visible markers of legitimacy. Compliance signaled safety. Silence signaled uncertainty. Interpretation became a private activity without public leverage.[^c5-ref-6-arendt-origins-fitzpatrick-everyday]

The case is instructive not because of ideology alone, but because it shows how authority can persist when legitimacy is enforced through performance rather than belief.[^c5-ref-7-orlando-figes-the-whisperers]

## **What Replaced Interpretation**

Interpretation was replaced by enforced performance.[^c5-ref-8-erving-goffman-the-presentation]

Legitimacy no longer depended on whether claims were understood or accepted. It depended on whether they were enacted publicly. Meaning functioned as a script to perform rather than a claim to evaluate.[^c5-ref-9-jrgen-habermas-the-theory]

Understanding became irrelevant to legitimacy so long as performance was visible and consistent.[^c5-ref-10-michel-foucault-discipline-and]

## **How Authority Reproduced Itself**

Authority reproduced itself through coercive ritual.[^c5-ref-11-foucault-discipline-and-punish]

Public displays of agreement reinforced the appearance of unanimity. Each act of compliance increased the perceived cost of dissent for others. Authority reproduced itself through normalization of performance under threat.[^c5-ref-12-timur-kuran-private-truths]

Reproduction occurred through fear-driven coordination rather than interpretive repair.[^c5-ref-13-when-authority-outlives-accountability]

## **What Still Functioned**

Private interpretation continued to function.[^c5-ref-14-figes-the-whisperers]

Individuals recognized contradictions and understood risks. Informal networks preserved shared understanding beneath the surface.[^c5-ref-15-fitzpatrick-everyday-stalinism]

What persisted was understanding without expression.[^c5-ref-16-how-meaning-moves]

## **What Was Lost**

What was lost was public interpretation as a shared evaluative process.[^c5-ref-17-habermas-communicative-action]

Reasons could not accumulate pressure. Evidence could not weaken legitimacy. Interpretation ceased to connect meaning to consequence in public space.[^c5-ref-18-when-authority-outlives-accountability]

This explains why exposure, reform, or clarification failed to constrain authority.[^c5-ref-19-kotkin-stalin]

## **Why This Case Matters Structurally**

This case demonstrates how authority can persist even when belief is thin or absent.[^c5-ref-20-arendt-origins]

Performative legitimacy depends on enforced visibility. Interpretation is neither sealed nor repurposed—it is displaced into silence.[^c5-ref-21-kuran-private-truths-public]

Legitimacy can be maintained when compliance replaces persuasion as the operative condition of authority.[^c5-ref-22-chapter-4-when-authority]

> **When legitimacy is performed under threat, understanding no longer needs to agree—or appear.**
