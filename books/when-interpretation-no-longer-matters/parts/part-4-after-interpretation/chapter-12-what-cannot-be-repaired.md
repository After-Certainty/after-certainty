# **Chapter 12**

## **What Cannot Be Repaired**

The Cultural Revolution shows a rarer failure: interpretation remained abundant while repairability vanished.[^c12-ref-1-how-meaning-moves] Part IV's second move is to name when clarification, exposure, and corrective ritual no longer slow authority—they accelerate it.[^c12-ref-2-when-authority-outlives-accountability]

That is not an argument against responsibility. It is a guard against misdiagnosis—treating displaced interpretation as if it were merely incomplete.[^c12-ref-3-hannah-arendt-the-origins]

## **The Event in Brief**

Mao Zedong (1893–1976) led the Chinese Communist Party to victory in 1949 and established the People’s Republic of China. After earlier campaigns such as the Great Leap Forward, Mao launched the Cultural Revolution in 1966 to reassert ideological purity and revolutionary commitment.[^c12-ref-4-roderick-macfarquhar-and-michael]

Students organized as Red Guards were encouraged to criticize party officials, intellectuals, and perceived “bourgeois” elements. Public struggle sessions, denunciations, and mass mobilization campaigns became widespread. Institutions—including schools, bureaucracies, and professional networks—were destabilized or dismantled.

Study of Mao’s writings intensified. Citizens memorized slogans, debated doctrine, and participated in collective criticism and self-criticism. Interpretation was not suppressed; it was required.[^c12-ref-5-frank-diktter-the-cultural]

The result was prolonged instability, factional conflict, and institutional breakdown. Attempts at correction repeatedly generated new waves of accusation and purification.

Repair did not stabilize authority. It radicalized it.

## **Leader Focus: Mao Zedong**

This is a case where interpretation remained publicly active but became an instrument of authority rather than a constraint.

Mao’s authority did not collapse because interpretation failed too early. It destabilized because interpretation was mobilized as an instrument of authority itself.[^c12-ref-4-roderick-macfarquhar-and-michael]

During the Cultural Revolution, citizens were encouraged—indeed required—to interpret, criticize, denounce, and reinterpret continually. Texts were studied, slogans refined, meanings debated. Interpretation intensified.[^c12-ref-5-frank-diktter-the-cultural]

What disappeared was interpretive leverage. No interpretation could constrain authority because all interpretation was already aligned with it. Revision, confession, and denunciation did not repair legitimacy; they reproduced it. Each corrective act deepened instability rather than restoring accountability.[^c12-ref-6-macfarquhar-and-schoenhals-maos]

The case is instructive not because of ideological extremity alone, but because it shows how interpretation can become anti-repair—how meaning-making itself can accelerate collapse once it no longer mediates legitimacy.[^c12-ref-7-when-authority-is-misread]

## **What Replaced Interpretation**

Interpretation was replaced by instrumentalized correction.[^c12-ref-8-michel-foucault-powerknowledge-pantheon]

Meaning was no longer evaluated to determine whether authority should persist. It was generated to demonstrate vigilance and purity. Interpretation became performance rather than constraint.[^c12-ref-9-jrgen-habermas-the-theory]

Correction did not slow authority. It fueled it.[^c12-ref-10-how-meaning-moves]

## **How Authority Reproduced Itself**

Authority reproduced itself through recursive purification.[^c12-ref-11-macfarquhar-and-schoenhals-maos]

Campaigns of critique and self-critique destabilized institutions, relationships, and expertise. Each wave of correction created new targets and new threats. Authority reproduced itself not by resolving error, but by continually redefining it.[^c12-ref-12-diktter-the-cultural-revolution]

Reproduction occurred through perpetual destabilization rather than stabilization.[^c12-ref-13-when-authority-outlives-accountability]

## **What Still Functioned**

Interpretive activity remained intense.[^c12-ref-14-macfarquhar-and-schoenhals-maos]

Citizens reasoned, debated doctrine, and interpreted texts with seriousness. Understanding did not vanish. What vanished was the ability of interpretation to limit authority rather than amplify it.[^c12-ref-15-habermas-communicative-action]

What remained operative was interpretation without braking power.[^c12-ref-16-how-meaning-moves]

## **What Was Lost**

What was lost was repairability.[^c12-ref-17-when-authority-outlives-accountability]

No amount of clarification, exposure, or correction restored accountability. Each attempt at repair increased volatility. Interpretation ceased to function as a stabilizing mechanism.[^c12-ref-18-how-meaning-moves]

This explains why reform efforts repeatedly failed and why collapse accelerated rather than resolved.[^c12-ref-19-diktter-the-cultural-revolution]

## **Why This Case Matters Structurally**

This case demonstrates the final limit of interpretive remedies.[^c12-ref-20-arendt-origins]

Unlike systems where interpretation disappears, Mao’s regime shows how interpretation can remain hyperactive while losing all corrective capacity. Repair does not fail quietly—it backfires.[^c12-ref-21-when-authority-is-misread]

Some authority failures cannot be repaired even when interpretation is abundant—and recognizing that limit matters before prescribing more of the failing medium.[^c12-ref-22-chapter-4-when-authority]

> **When interpretation becomes the engine of authority, repair no longer slows collapse—it accelerates it.**
