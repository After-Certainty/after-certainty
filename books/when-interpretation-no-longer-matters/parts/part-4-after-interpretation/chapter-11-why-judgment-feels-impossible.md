# **Chapter 11**

## **Why Judgment Feels Impossible**

Under the Khmer Rouge, moral clarity about atrocity was often immediate—and powerless.[^c11-ref-1-how-meaning-moves] Part IV begins with that paradox: judgment can sharpen while losing the pathways that once connected recognition to consequence.

Readers coming from essay-length work on **judgment without finality** will recognize the feeling: condemnation that is morally serious but structurally inert. Here the limit is not incomplete understanding but displaced interpretation—judgment as witness rather than repair.

Condemnation does not fail because it is inaccurate. It fails because it no longer connects to consequence. Judgment becomes expressive rather than corrective.[^c11-ref-2-when-authority-outlives-accountability] Moral exhaustion in such cases signals a structural limit, not a personal failure.[^c11-ref-3-hannah-arendt-the-origins]

## **The Event in Brief**

Pol Pot (1925–1998), leader of the Khmer Rouge, took control of Cambodia in April 1975. The regime sought to create a radically agrarian society by dismantling urban life and abolishing existing institutions.[^c11-ref-4-ben-kiernan-the-pol]

Cities were emptied. Currency was eliminated. Schools, media, religious institutions, and professional roles were dismantled. Intellectuals and perceived political enemies were imprisoned, tortured, or executed. The regime established prison centers such as S-21 (Tuol Sleng), where forced confessions and systematic violence occurred.[^c11-ref-5-david-p-chandler-voices]

Between 1975 and 1979, approximately 1.5 to 2 million people died from execution, forced labor, starvation, and disease.

The system did not merely suppress dissent. It dismantled the institutional and linguistic structures through which interpretation could coordinate resistance or evaluation.

Moral clarity about suffering remained possible. Mechanisms for consequence did not.

## **Leader Focus: Pol Pot**

Pol Pot's authority did not depend on winning interpretive contests. It depended on removing the institutions through which contests could matter.[^c11-ref-4-ben-kiernan-the-pol]

The regime targeted teachers, professionals, religious leaders, and urban populations not only as enemies but as carriers of alternative evaluative languages. Forced confessions at S-21 did not repair legitimacy; they demonstrated that interpretation itself had been captured.[^c11-ref-5-david-p-chandler-voices]

Observers inside and outside Cambodia could name what was happening. What they could not do—once mediation was destroyed—was make naming alter standing. Judgment remained accurate and inert.[^c11-ref-6-kiernan-the-pol-pot][^c11-ref-7-when-authority-is-misread]

## **What Replaced Interpretation**

Interpretation was replaced by obliteration of evaluative space.[^c11-ref-8-arendt-origins]

Authority no longer required belief, alignment, or performance. It required only compliance within a system that eliminated conditions for interpretation to matter. Meaning was neither debated nor enacted; it was overridden.[^c11-ref-9-michel-foucault-society-must]

Judgment could be made—but not applied.[^c11-ref-10-how-meaning-moves]

## **How Authority Reproduced Itself**

Authority reproduced itself through systematic destruction of mediation.[^c11-ref-11-orlando-figes-the-whisperers]

Institutions that enable interpretation—education, media, civil society, professional networks—were dismantled. Without shared language for evaluation, authority reproduced itself through terror and isolation rather than persuasion or spectacle.[^c11-ref-12-foucault-society-must-be]

Reproduction occurred through elimination of alternatives rather than reinforcement of meaning.[^c11-ref-13-when-authority-outlives-accountability]

## **What Still Functioned**

Minimal private understanding persisted.[^c11-ref-14-chandler-voices-from-s21]

Individuals recognized suffering, danger, and injustice. What did not persist was any pathway for interpretation to coordinate response or constrain authority.[^c11-ref-15-kiernan-pol-pot-regime]

What remained operative was awareness without agency.[^c11-ref-16-jrgen-habermas-the-theory]

## **What Was Lost**

What was lost was judgment with consequence.[^c11-ref-17-when-authority-outlives-accountability]

Moral clarity no longer translated into leverage. Condemnation could not accumulate pressure. Interpretation ceased to link recognition to action.[^c11-ref-18-how-meaning-moves]

This explains why judgment feels both urgent and futile in such cases.[^c11-ref-19-arendt-origins]

## **Why This Case Matters Structurally**

This case demonstrates the limit condition of post-interpretive authority.[^c11-ref-20-arendt-origins] Unlike alignment-based, identity-saturated, or performative regimes, total destruction of interpretive mediation leaves no internal or external pathway for correction.[^c11-ref-21-when-authority-is-misread]

Some authority failures cannot be addressed through better interpretation, stronger condemnation, or clearer moral language—because the mediating institutions are gone.[^c11-ref-22-chapter-4-when-authority]

> **When interpretation cannot connect judgment to consequence, moral clarity no longer restores leverage.**
