# **Chapter 9**

## **What Judgment Can Still Do**

Someone writes down what happened after it is already too late to change the outcome.

The page may be a diary kept under a mat, a list of names copied from memory before the names thin further, a testimony drafted years later for a tribunal that may or may not arrive while the speaker is still alive, a letter that will never reach the person it addresses. The hand that writes is not inventing meaning. It is trying to keep a fact from dissolving. A village emptied. A relative taken at night. A school that became a detention site. A photograph of a face that existed. The writer knows, while writing, that the sentence will not reopen a road, refund a harvest, restore a profession, or bring the dead back into the kitchen. Writing arrives after leverage has already been broken. The page is late. The page is still done.

Watch what the hand is doing more carefully than consolation usually allows. It is not primarily arguing a theory of the state. It is attaching words to events that would otherwise remain only as private weather—pain without address, absence without file, a face remembered until the remembering person is also gone. The list of names is especially stark. A list does not explain. It does not persuade a committee. It does not rewind a convoy. It insists that particular syllables belonged to particular bodies, and that those bodies were removed from the world by other human beings acting under orders, classifications, and permissions. That insistence is already a form of judgment. It arrives without a courthouse. Even the physical awkwardness of the record matters: smudged pencil, duplicated spellings, dates that conflict by a day because trauma disorders time. Imperfection does not disqualify the page. Imperfection proves a human was recovering what power tried to make unrecoverable.

That lateness is the ordinary pressure this chapter opens from—not because every late record is Cambodia, and not because private note-taking is automatically heroic. Most people have known a smaller version: the email that documents a wrong after the decision is irreversible; the medical chronology assembled after discharge; the family timeline written when a parent can no longer tell it; the workplace timeline kept after a career has already ended. Accurate naming continues. The destination for naming has thinned. The tension is not ignorance versus knowledge. It is accurate naming with nowhere institutional to send it.

Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge, from April 1975 to early 1979, makes that tension historically large without needing to turn atrocity into a clever diagram. Cities were emptied. Currency was abolished. Schools, media, religious institutions, and professional roles were dismantled or recoded into instruments of agrarian obedience. Forced labor, starvation, disease, and execution killed on a scale that responsible historians still give as a range rather than as a single tidy figure: on the order of one and a half to two million people.[^c9-kiernan-overview] People inside those years could recognize suffering. Recognition did not restore the offices through which recognition becomes repair. The question this chapter holds is colder than “Did they understand?” It is: what can judgment still do when it can no longer repair, prevent, or reverse the harm it names?

---

On April 17, 1975, Phnom Penh fell to the Khmer Rouge. The victory was announced as liberation after years of war, bombardment, refugee flood, and the collapse of the Lon Nol government. What followed in the capital was not ordinary postwar reorganization. Within days, residents were ordered out—hospital patients still attached to drips, women in labor, children, civil servants, merchants, monks, people with no political file at all. Loudspeakers and armed instructions produced a city in motion against its will. Streets that had been dense with urban life became corridors of forced departure. Families learned, in hours, which belongings could be carried and which would be abandoned to looting, decay, or the regime’s indifference. The emptying was not a rumor later embroidered by enemies; it was a lived sequence of walking, waiting, lying about status, separating from neighbors, and discovering that the city itself was being cancelled as a way of living.[^c9-kiernan-evacuation]

Other towns followed related patterns of depopulation and redistribution into rural control. The point was not tourism of ruins. The point was to break the densities in which alternative claims could find each other. A crowded capital concentrates rumor, skill, memory of prior institutions, and the awkward fact of strangers who might still compare notes. Scatter those people into cooperatives under cadre watch, and comparison becomes harder, slower, more lethal. Emptied avenues were therefore not merely an aesthetic of revolution. They were an anti-mediation technology: fewer intersections at which judgment could meet judgment and become something with a destination.

Emptying the cities mattered as policy and as symbol at once. Urban life carries mediation: markets that price exchange, schools that credential skill, newspapers that circulate claim and counterclaim, temples and churches that hold moral vocabularies older than the current faction, professional guilds and offices through which people can appeal, petition, publish, or simply insist that a name still belongs to a person rather than to a category. To empty Phnom Penh and other towns was to attack those mediations physically. People were moved into rural cooperatives and work regimes in which survival depended less on argument than on assignment, ration, surveillance, and the capacity to look useful while remaining invisible. The regime’s agrarian utopia was not only an agricultural plan. It was a project of removing the social machines through which alternative evaluation could coordinate.

Currency abolition pressed the same logic into daily exchange. When money is cancelled, bargaining does not become freer; it becomes commandeered. Status shifts from possessed value to political classification—who is “new people,” who is “base people,” who has a suspect biography, who can be denounced as contaminated by cities, schooling, foreignness, or religion. Markets cease to be places where disagreement about price can still be ordinary. Need becomes petition; petition becomes danger. Schools closed or were reinvented as instruments of revolutionary discipline rather than as places where contested claims could be tested over years. Independent media ceased to function as spaces of public argument. Religious institutions were attacked; monks were forced out of robes; temples lost their standing as shelters for a moral language not authored by the Party center. Professional roles—teachers, doctors, engineers, former officials—became dangerous marks. Glasses, soft hands, a foreign language, a relative abroad: ordinary traces of expertise and cosmopolitan life could read as evidence against the bearer.[^c9-kiernan-institutions]

None of this requires readers to pretend that pre-1975 Cambodia was an unproblematic liberal public sphere waiting to be mourned in abstraction. War had already shattered ordinary life. American bombing, civil conflict, corruption, and refugee crisis had already injured trust and swollen the capital with desperate arrivals. The Khmer Rouge did not invent suffering in Cambodia. What the 1975–1979 regime did, with unmatched thoroughness in this country’s modern history, was to destroy remaining institutional pathways through which recognition of harm could become shared leverage. Terror works in many systems. Here terror arrived paired with the demolition of the places where speech might still have found an address.

Forced labor and famine were not side effects casually ignored while ideology floated above bodies. They were built into the cooperative system’s extraction of work under inadequate food, punishing schedules, and punishment for perceived insufficient zeal. Days began and ended under orders that treated human energy as a renewable quarry. People died digging canals and reservoirs, planting and harvesting under targets that did not respect soil or season, hauling loads, collapsing on roads between work sites. They died of untreated disease in places where medical knowledge had been scorned as bourgeois contamination or reduced to meager improvisation. They died because rations were political instruments as much as caloric supplies—allocated according to classification, withheld as discipline, spoken of as if hunger were a loyalty problem. They died because local cadres could escalate accusations into killings without anything resembling independent adjudication. A stolen potato, a wrong class origin, a relative who once worked for the prior government, a careless sentence overheard: the catalogue of lethal premises did not need coherence to kill. Execution was not only the end of particular enemies. It was a standing technology for sorting the living.[^c9-kiernan-death]

Death toll estimates for Democratic Kampuchea remain contested in their precise upper and lower bounds—as any count assembled from fragmentary records, demographic reconstruction, and survivor testimony must remain contested—but the scholarly range around one and a half to two million dead is enough to forbid comforting miniaturization. The uncertainty is not a license for denial. It is a reminder that destruction also destroys the archives by which destruction is later measured. Bodies disappeared into graves, forests, fields. Families lost the documents that would have made later claims easy. Whole networks of witnesses were themselves killed. When later historians argue over tens or hundreds of thousands inside that dreadful band, they are not performing academic vanity. They are working among ruins.

The leadership of the Communist Party of Kampuchea—including Pol Pot—matters to the chronology of command, faction, and ideology. Centering this chapter on a single personality, however, would misname the structural wound. Personality studies can satisfy the hunger for a villain with a face. Cambodia’s catastrophe was also an institutional abolition: the removal of courts, papers, professions, currencies, congregations, classrooms, and newsrooms that once, however imperfectly, gave judgment somewhere to travel. Cadres at many levels carried out evacuations, classifications, and killings. Local terror often exceeded any tidy script from Phnom Penh’s inner circle. The regime’s force was distributed through cooperatives, security apparatus, and a political culture that treated suspicion as vigilance. Reducing that to one man’s psychology flatters our appetite for biography and disables the book’s actual question—what remains of judgment after the institutions capable of consequence have been destroyed.[^c9-kiernan-leadership]

---

Judgment did not wait politely for 1979. People named what was happening while it happened: hunger as policy consequence, disappearance as method, cooperatives as sites of coercion. A mother could see that her child was starving because the ration was a ration, not because metaphysics had intervened. A former teacher could recognize that expertise had been redefined as guilt. A monk defrocked by force could know that a sacred language had been prohibited as rival authority. Naming frequently had nowhere to go. Escape routes were few; borders were lethal; international attention arrived late and often partially, entangled in Cold War frameworks that could blunt recognition as easily as they could amplify it. Inside the country, public standing was not available for corrective speech. A person who accurately described famine risked becoming the next example. Accuracy without office is not the same as absence of accuracy. It is accuracy stripped of brake.

That distinction must stay sharp against a cynical misreading. Cynicism says people did not know, or knew and therefore somehow consented, or that moral language is always decoration. The Cambodian record refuses those consolations. Knowledge of suffering was widespread in ordinary form—bodies starving, relatives missing, instructions that could not be questioned safely. What failed was not the human capacity to recognize wrong. What failed was the capacity to convert recognition into institutional consequence while the regime still stood. Chapter 2’s kitchen-table tragedy—understanding without leverage—returns here at genocidal intensity. People can know and still be unable to reopen standing. Cambodia shows that impossibility at the scale of a country forcibly remade.

Chapter 8’s Cultural Revolution must not be stamped onto this sequence as a portable template. Mao’s years of hyperactive criticism without repair and Cambodia’s years of emptied cities and abolished mediation are neighboring historical worlds with distinct chronologies, actors, and forms of harm. Casual equivalence is a way of refusing to see either. What travels, carefully, is a colder lesson about leverage: corrective speech and moral clarity are not automatically engines of rescue once the offices that receive them have been dismantled or converted into instruments of terror. In China, criticism could roar while settlement failed. In Cambodia, the roar itself was often cancelled along with the schools and papers that might have carried it. Different engines. Shared absence: judgment without a pathway back into repair.

Nor should this chapter be drafted into contests with the performative rooms of Chapter 5 or the sealed narratives of Chapter 6, as if atrocity needed a ranking system to become thinkable. Unfinished applause, narrative enclosure, deferred constraint, anti-repair, witness after consequence—these are different displacements of interpretation’s power to matter. Cambodia’s work in the sequence is specific. It asks what remains when the pathway to consequence is not merely delayed, reframed, or flooded with false correction, but physically and institutionally destroyed.

---

Tuol Sleng, known as S-21, forces the chapter’s hardest visual fact into view. The former school in Phnom Penh was converted into a security prison where thousands of people—many of them cadres and their families caught in purges as well as other condemned categories—were photographed, interrogated, tortured into confession, and then killed, with most executions carried out at the killing fields of Choeung Ek.[^c9-chandler-overview] Blackboards and classrooms became holding spaces. Childhood learning furniture became the architecture of disappearance. The photographs remain. Faces look at the camera with expressions that later viewers will try, helplessly, to read—fear, exhaustion, blankness, a last attempt at composure. Names were entered. Numbers were assigned. Confessions were extracted and typed. The apparatus kept records of destruction with a bureaucratic density that still shocks: documentation of annihilation rather than of justice.

Linger on the photographs longer than theory usually permits. Each image is a record that a person sat for the machine that would help kill them. The camera’s claim is almost administrative: here is the face matching the file. Later, the same claim becomes something else—proof that this person was not an abstraction, proof that killing had a before. A wall of such faces, in museums and books that came after, does a work no paragraph of structural analysis can replace. It makes refusal of disappearance sensory. You cannot look without knowing that the emptied city and the death-range statistics were composed of eyes.

Chandler’s study of S-21 refuses to let those records be mistaken for ordinary interpretive life. Forced confessions were not fact-finding in the sense liberal institutions claim for investigation. They were productions that completed a political script—enemy networks invented under pain, admissions shaped to confirm paranoia about traitors, paperwork that made killing look like procedure. Interrogators demanded narratives of conspiracy that could expand outward to implicate more names, more networks, more reasons to continue the purge. The archive did not restore standing to the accused. The archive made disappearance administratively legible to the apparatus doing the disappearing.[^c9-chandler-confessions] For this book, the point is nearly unbearable in its precision. Even under a regime that destroyed so many mediations of public judgment, some forms of recording intensified—photography, dossiers, signature rituals of confession—while those recordings served terror rather than repair. Not all writing is witness. Some writing is the inventory of a killing machine.

And yet the same dossiers, photographs, and surviving testimony later became materials by which survivors, researchers, and courts could refuse total erasure. That reversal does not redeem the prison. It names a bitter conversion available only after the regime’s fall: documents made for destruction can, under different institutional conditions, become documents against disappearance. The photographs that once filed prisoners into death can hang as evidence that particular persons existed. The confession files that once justified execution can be read against their authorship as records of coerced speech. Names retrieved from those files can reenter family memory and public memorial. Judgment arrives late to these rooms. Late is not the same as never.

Outside S-21’s particular horror sat a wider landscape of cooperatives, security centers, and unmarked graves. Most Cambodians who died under the regime never entered Tuol Sleng’s photographed corridor; they died of overwork, hunger, disease, and local execution without becoming iconic exhibits. Survivor memoirs and oral histories—accounts such as those gathered around family annihilation, child laborers, and the ordinary logistics of staying alive one day longer—insist on particularity against any temptation to speak only in millions.[^c9-ung-memoir] A million is a statistic only if you let it remain a million. On the ground it was always this uncle, this sister, this teacher whose name a child still spells incorrectly, this empty place at a mat. Judgment that refuses disappearance begins there: not by converting grief into theory first, but by keeping names attachable to lives.

After the Vietnamese invasion toppled Democratic Kampuchea in early 1979, survivors faced the long secondary labor of reconstruction: finding who was left, burying who could be found, inventing livelihoods without the old professional maps, telling stories to children who had never seen the city as it had been. Refugees carried testimony across borders into camps and diasporas where speech could finally risk more than an immediate grave. Inside Cambodia, making sense of neighbors who had been cadres, victims, both, or something jagged in between became its own unfinished moral work. International recognition of the crimes unfolded unevenly across decades—geopolitics often obstructing what moral language alone would have demanded. Tribunals came late. Some senior figures died before full legal consequence. Many mid-level killers lived on as neighbors. Law’s partial arrival does not cancel the earlier years when judgment had almost no institutional handhold. It does confirm that consequence, when it finally appears, often appears as incomplete justice rather than as restored past.

Preservation itself became contested labor: museums, memorials, school curricula, family silence and family speech, foreign scholarship, local activism, denialist counter-stories that preferred to call the dead exaggerations. The fight after the killing is not identical to the killing. It is still part of what judgment must do when the first offices of prevention are already gone. To keep a photograph; to teach a date; to refuse a clean forgetting that would make later politics easier—these acts do not pretend to be resuscitation. They are maintenance of the harm’s visibility against the continuous temptation to finish the crime as amnesia.

Notice how the late record can become socially inconvenient precisely because it works. A name restored to public speech may reopen old neighborhood alliances. A museum photograph may implicate someone still living. A classroom lesson may disrupt a national story that preferred shorter grief. Witness without repair is therefore not a soft, frictionless piety. It can cost the witness again—in safety, in belonging, in the willingness of others to listen. The cost is part of the evidence that disappearance still wants finality.

---

What, then, can judgment still do when repair, prevention, and reversal are already gone?

It can refuse disappearance.

That sentence is easy to sentimentalize into a greeting-card consolation, and this book will not do that. Refusal of disappearance does not feed the hungry of 1977. It does not reopen a closed school in time. It does not interrupt an interrogation already underway. Witness without repair is not a substitute engine of rescue. It is a remaining consequential act of a different kind: the act of keeping the harm from completing itself as oblivion. Oblivion is not a neutral fog. Oblivion is an achievement sought by many perpetrators and, more quietly, by exhausted publics that prefer not to keep looking. A name written after the murder is not the murder undone. It is the murder prevented from becoming nobody’s business.

Call the pattern, earned rather than announced as a slogan at the door:

**Witness without repair.** Judgment can still refuse disappearance.

Witness here is not passive spectatorship. It is the labor of record, testimony, naming, preservation, teaching children what was done, holding photographs where faces can still be met, insisting that forced confessions are not voluntary truths, refusing the later soft speech that dissolves policy into weather. Witness is judgment continuing after leverage has been broken—continuing without pretending it has become something else. The writer of the late page does not claim to have saved the person named. The writer claims that the person named will not be processed into silence without residue.

In the grammar of this book, that is not a rescue program. Part III has already watched correction lose reach: questions reclassified as disloyalty, criticism drafted into purification, and now judgment stranded after institutions of consequence are destroyed. The arc does not end in technique tips. It ends in sober inventory. Some failures of authority cannot be answered by better interpretation alone because the mediating pathway between recognition and consequence has been smashed. Cambodia forces that inventory without inviting despair’s twin—cynicism that calls witness useless because witness cannot rewind time. Uselessness for repair is not uselessness for truth’s remaining social life. Societies that keep records still inherit obligations those records impose. Societies that lose records inherit only rumor and denial’s easier weather.

Theory should stay late and quiet here, because the photographs and names are doing the primary work. Still, a thin conceptual clarification helps prevent two misuses. First: judgment-as-witness is not moral victory disguised as analysis. Condemnation of the Khmer Rouge is justified; what is gone is leverage during the years when leverage would have mattered most. Seriousness about atrocity includes seriousness about that absence. Second: judgment-as-witness is not a license to treat every contemporary frustration as “like Cambodia.” The emptied city, the abolished currency, the school turned prison—these are not metaphors for bad meetings. Analogy that flattens scale is another form of erasure. The chapter travels by structural recognition, not by competitive suffering charts or by decorating ordinary politics with genocidal prestige.

What still functioned, amid annihilation, was often small and costly: memory carried in bodies, caution taught as habit, fragments of Buddhist and familial ethics whispered when safe, the stubborn human capacity to notice that a child is starving, the later willingness of survivors to speak when speaking became possible. Hidden scraps of writing, where they existed, were already practicing the late page’s office under maximum danger. What was lost was not the bare ability to evaluate. What was lost—during the decisive years—was the public and institutional circuitry that turns evaluation into constraint. Authority reproduced itself less by winning arguments than by removing the world in which arguments could organize opposition. That is why clearer moral language, arrived at from afar, often felt both necessary and strangely beside the point to people trapped inside. Clarity was not missing. Circuitry was.

---

Look again at the opening hand.

Someone is still writing after it is too late. The list of names is incomplete—there was never going to be a complete list. The diary’s ink has bled where water reached it. The testimony pauses because the throat closes on a particular evening in 1976 that will not become sentences without tearing. A survivor checks a photograph against a memory and cannot be sure, then is sure, then cannot sleep. Nothing on the page repairs the cooperative, rebodies the photographed prisoner, refunds the years stolen from a childhood. The consequential remainder is narrower and still real. The page prevents the harm from finishing as unmarked silence. It keeps a route open—not back into the past, but forward into whatever partial courts, classrooms, memorials, family kitchens, and historical books will later need evidence that these people were not rumors.

In Phnom Penh’s emptied avenues of April 1975, judgment still belonged to anyone who could see a hospital patient forced into the street. That judgment had almost nowhere to file itself. At S-21, the regime filed other papers: photographs and confessions that made killing look like administration. After 1979, survivors and scholars began the long work of turning remaining traces into public memory against oblivion—Kiernan’s reconstruction of a regime of race, power, and mass death; Chandler’s reading of a prison that spoke in forced voices; memoirs that insist on the grain of particular lives.[^c9-kiernan-overview][^c9-chandler-overview][^c9-ung-memoir] None of those later acts rewind the calendar. All of them answer a question this book cannot escape once leverage is gone: if judgment can no longer correct, what is judgment still for?

It is still for refusing to let disappearance become the last word.

The late written record is therefore not a consolation prize for failed politics. It is the form judgment takes when repair is already impossible and erasure remains optional. Optional is the key word. Erasure can still be advanced by neglect, by denial, by the soft laziness that treats millions as atmosphere. Witness is the counter-labor. Someone writes the name. Someone keeps the photograph. Someone says, to a room that would prefer to move on, that moving on without naming is just disappearance with better manners.

Return once more to that first sentence, and let it read differently. Someone writes down what happened after it is already too late to change the outcome. At the chapter’s beginning, lateness sounded like futility. By the end, lateness also names the only remaining office. The page cannot restore the dead. The page can still keep the dead from being processed into nobody. In Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge, when institutions capable of consequence were destroyed, that was not nothing. It was not enough. Enough was already gone. What judgment could still do was refuse the final victory of obliteration—and that refusal, written late, remains one of the ways a human world insists it was a world.
