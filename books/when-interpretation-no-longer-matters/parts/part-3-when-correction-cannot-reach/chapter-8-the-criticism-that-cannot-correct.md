# **Chapter 8**

## **The Criticism That Cannot Correct**

The meeting is called a retrospective, or a performance review, or self-criticism hour. Chairs form a ring that looks like equality and functions like surveillance. Someone at the front asks for candor. The request sounds generous. It is also timed.

What follows is rarely discovery. Discovery would rearrange standing. What follows instead is the careful production of *approved* failure: a defect already licensed for confession, a shortfall that confirms the room’s current story about itself. Speak the flaw that the hierarchy is ready to absorb. Do not speak the flaw that would reopen who may judge whom.

The person who volunteers first has rehearsed—self-scouring enough to count as courage, bounded enough not to implicate the agenda. Colleagues nod. Someone redirects toward lessons learned. The notebook fills with sentences that sound like repair. What they often are is ritual: proof that criticism happened on schedule.

Most of us have felt the difference between criticism that searches and criticism that completes a script. One kind of speech is received as loyalty dressed as honesty. The other is received as disloyalty dressed as inquiry. After enough repetitions, intelligent people learn which honesty will be praised and which will become the next agenda item with their name on it. The more a system treats visible criticism as proof of health, the more it may require criticism without allowing criticism to revise authority. Speech intensifies. Repair does not follow.

That confusion is what the Cultural Revolution makes historically large. In China after 1966, interpretation and criticism became hyperactive. Study intensified. Denunciation multiplied. Confession, struggle, and doctrinal argument filled schools, work units, and streets. To a distant observer trained to treat speech as medicine, the fever of criticism can look like the immune system working. Up close, another structure appears. Criticism was required. Correction that might have restored predictability did not arrive with it. Each wave of purification manufactured new enemies, new obligations to perform vigilance, new reasons to destabilize the last temporary settlement. The presence of criticism was not the presence of repair.[^c8-macfarquhar-overview][^c8-dikotter-overview]

---

Mao Zedong (1893–1976) had already led the Chinese Communist Party through civil war, the founding of the People’s Republic in 1949, and a sequence of campaigns that made mass mobilization into political habit. Land reform, thought reform, anti-rightist rectification—whatever one concludes about each campaign’s justice or cruelty—had already taught millions that politics could arrive as a meeting one could not refuse, a classification one could not outargue alone, a demand that private life become publicly usable speech. The Party-state learned, repeatedly, how to move people through study and struggle. People learned, repeatedly, how to survive those movements with careful mouths.

The Great Leap Forward of the late 1950s—an attempt to remake agriculture and industry at forced speed—left famine measured in staggering human cost, institutional disarray, and a period in which other Party leaders tried, carefully, to reassert more managerial forms of governance. Local exaggeration, fear of reporting failure, and utopian targets had already shown how obligatory enthusiasm could starve correction of the very information correction needs. Recovery in the early 1960s did not erase that lesson; it suspended it under thinner ice. Mao’s prestige remained immense. His practical dominance over daily administration was more contested than later mythology sometimes admits. When he and allies later sought to reopen revolutionary intensity, they did so inside a landscape already trained for campaigns—not onto a blank field of passive bodies.[^c8-macfarquhar-leap]

The Cultural Revolution, launched in 1966, cannot be reduced to one man’s psychology alone without losing the institutional story: a revolutionary founding generation still living, a Party-state dense enough to organize millions, a prior repertoire of struggle and rectification ready for reuse, and a political environment in which purity language and factional contention could be ignited from the center and then run beyond anyone’s tidy control. Mao mattered as ignition, status, and veto. He did not matter as the only engine.

In the mid-1960s the center moved against what it cast as revisionism and capitalist restoration inside the Party and cultural institutions. Official calls for rebellion against “capitalist roaders” began as permission—sometimes ecstatic, often terrifying—for students and other youth to treat established authority as suspect. Red Guards formed in schools and universities, then spilled into society as a mass movement of denunciation, parade, ransacking, and struggle. Teachers, officials, writers, and neighbors were hauled into public sessions where humiliation completed what investigation never needed to finish. Institutions that had only recently begun to settle into specialist routines were attacked as nests of privilege or soft thought. Classes stopped. Careers ended. Homes were searched for evidence of incorrect spirit. Bureaucracy did not merely receive criticism. In many places it was disassembled by it.[^c8-dikotter-redguards][^c8-macfarquhar-redguards]

What began as attacks on particular cultural figures and officials widened into a general climate in which almost any settled hierarchy could be accused of harboring a soft line. Some participants believed they were purifying socialism. Some sought status, revenge, belonging, or safety by arriving early to the winning posture. Motives did not need to be uniform for the structure to take hold. Once public humiliation became a recognized instrument of politics, the instrument invited use.

The violence and disruption of those years were real, and no responsible account should convert them into abstract metaphor for “toxic culture” elsewhere. Millions were persecuted; deaths and lasting injuries ran into counts that historians continue to refine rather than to treat as settled theater props; families were split by accusation; cities and provinces drifted into factional fighting that the center itself later struggled to demobilize.[^c8-dikotter-scale] This chapter does not need a single headline number to do its work, and it refuses the vice of swapping one catastrophe for another as if severity were a trading card. What it needs is a structural observation: the period’s most striking feature, for a book about interpretation and authority, is not that speech disappeared. It is that speech became mandatory as purification while losing the capacity to restore a durable settlement.

Study of Mao’s writings intensified even as ordinary schooling collapsed. The *Quotations from Chairman Mao*—the Little Red Book—circulated as portable scripture of the campaign. Work units held study sessions. People memorized slogans, parsed correct lines, and practiced the distinction between genuine revolutionary criticism and the wrong kind of doubt. Self-criticism became an expected genre: a person learned to produce a narrative of error that ended in renewed loyalty. Criticism of others became equally mandatory: failure to denounce could itself be read as concealment. Interpretation was not suppressed the way a later reader, thinking of some other regimes, might expect. Interpretation was required—on paper, in meetings, in struggle sessions, in wall posters that covered public space with contested meaning.[^c8-dikotter-study][^c8-macfarquhar-study]

That single fact disables one of the most comforting stories this book keeps meeting. The comforting story says: when authority goes wrong, what we need is more critique. Critique as such becomes the remedy—exposure, confession, the public naming of fault. Mao’s China forces a harder sentence. Critique can be commandeered. It can become the medium through which authority reproduces itself while institutions lose the very predictability that would make correction meaningful. A student who criticizes a teacher is not automatically a citizen recovering accountability. A factory worker who denounces a supervisor is not automatically repairing governance. They may be participating in a ritual whose next iteration will denounce them, or their friends, or the temporary winners of last month’s struggle, once the definition of purity advances another step. Criticism here is not only a constraint on power. It is an output of authority—required display, renewable fuel, proof that vigilance occurred on schedule.

---

Shift the furniture into a Chinese work unit or classroom of 1967. The demand is no longer soft corporate candor. It is revolutionary vigilance. Someone must speak. Silence has become a political skin. The first speeches do not invent enemies at random; they invent them according to available templates—class background, a wrong book, an overheard joke, association with an official already fallen, insufficient enthusiasm in the last parade. The template is interpretive. People argue over which category fits. They cite Mao. They quote newspapers. They contest who has interpreted the correct line with more fidelity. The heat of those arguments can look like intellectual life under pressure. In important respects it *is* intellectual life—under pressure of a particular kind. Meaning is being made continuously. Texts are being read. Cases are being classified. What that labor does *not* reliably produce is a restored order in which legitimate authority becomes answerable, limited, and durable.

Struggle sessions made the difference between criticism and repair brutally visible. The person criticized knelt, stood with head bowed, wore a placard, endured shouts and blows, produced confession as a performance that completed the meeting’s purpose. A neighbor might be required to add a detail. A student might be required to raise the temperature. A spouse might be pressed to confirm the charge, converting intimacy into evidence. Confession here was not therapeutic insight arriving late. It was a public conversion of a person into a lesson. The audience learned what counts as an enemy this week. The confessing person learned—sometimes sincerely, sometimes only as survival—to inhabit that lesson with enough speech to exhaust the room. Afterward, the institution was not healed. The institution had been taught to need another session. Yesterday’s prosecutor became tomorrow’s suspect with a regularity that no longer surprises historians of the period and still should unsettle anyone who treats criticism as an automatic solvent for power.[^c8-dikotter-struggle][^c8-macfarquhar-struggle]

Watch the recursive twist carefully. Each corrective act can feel, on the day it happens, like overdue accountability. An arrogant official is dragged out. A teacher who struck students is exposed. A writer’s soft line is named. For victims of ordinary abuse, those hours can even contain a bitter justice. Then the template widens. The category of abuse expands to include insufficient zeal, wrong associations, old remarks newly remembered, a book on a shelf, a relative abroad, hesitation in the last meeting. Accountability without a stabilizing standard becomes a scramble for the moving standard. People accelerate their own criticism not because they have suddenly discovered more truth, but because speed itself becomes protective coloration. The early denouncer hopes to look pure. The late denouncer looks like concealment. So criticism accelerates. Settlement retreats.

Schools and universities illustrate the anti-repair dynamic with special clarity. During the Cultural Revolution’s most chaotic early phase, campuses became staging grounds for faction and denunciation. Teachers’ knowledge became evidence against them. Curricula dissolved. When education finally began to reassemble, what had been damaged was the expectation that expertise, seniority, and doctrinal debate could settle into reciprocal discipline. Authority returned, when it returned, often as exhaustion and demobilization rather than as interpretive reconciliation. Campaigns that were supposed to cleanse generated factional warlords of the spirit; purification had become an engine with its own momentum.[^c8-macfarquhar-schools]

Bureaucracy offers a parallel lesson. A functioning administration needs more than ideology. It needs files that stay filed, skills that accumulate, offices that can be addressed without first being destroyed. Criticizing bureaucracy for arrogance or insulation can be just. Turning criticism into a standing reason to smash offices, parade officials, and dissolve professional networks does something else. It replaces one problem—unresponsive authority—with another: authority that cannot stabilize long enough to be held to predictable standards. People still interpret. They argue which office deserves to be smashed next. They debate which cadre has waved the Little Red Book with insufficient zeal. The debate can be intricate. Intricate debate is not the same as institutional repair.

Factional conflict made the point irreversible for anyone still hoping that a final correct confession would end the process. Rival Red Guard groups, and later rival revolutionary committees and armed factions in some provinces, accused one another of false revolution while competing to appear more Maoist than their enemies. Each side could cite texts. Each side could stage struggle. Each side could produce lists. The proliferation of correct lines did not converge on settlement; it competed for the monopoly of purification. When the People’s Liberation Army was drawn deeper into suppressing and redesigning local power, that military involvement was not the triumph of free interpretation. It was an admission that criticism-as-governance had generated disorder the campaign language could not resolve by itself.[^c8-macfarquhar-factions][^c8-dikotter-factions]

---

Here the contrast with coercive silence becomes indispensable—and must remain a contrast, not a ranking of monstrosities. Chapter 5 examined rooms in which unfinished applause and obligatory affirmation taught people to withhold public judgment. Under Stalinist ritual, the dangerous act was often being first to stop performing agreement. Visible consensus was manufactured; private understanding retreated into whispers; criticism, when extracted, frequently arrived as confession theater already captured by the apparatus.

The Cultural Revolution’s pressure worked through a different demand. Speech was not primarily the scarce, risky deviation from silence. Speech—critical speech, denunciatory speech, confessional speech—was mandatory. People were not mainly trapped by the unfinished ovation’s logic of who sits first. They were trapped by a regimen of vocal vigilance in which failing to criticize could be as exposing as criticizing the wrong target. Blankness itself looked like a political posture waiting to be interpreted. The safer move, locally, was often to speak—within the advancing script—and to hope one’s speech landed on the approved fault before the approved fault moved.

Imagine the difference as the difference between two kinds of rooms. In one room, everyone must keep clapping until someone else stops. In the other, everyone must keep accusing until someone else becomes the next object of accusation, or until the day’s quota of vigilance is visibly spent. Both rooms can destroy an evening, a career, a life. Both rooms teach people to read one another more carefully than they read claims. Only the second room treats continuous critical speech as the official proof that politics is healthy. That is why a reader who arrives hoping that “more criticism” is always the medicine can misread Mao’s years as evidence that speech was flourishing toward freedom. What flourished was speech under conscription.

Diagnosis fails when it cannot tell engines apart. In the Soviet meeting of Chapter 5, public interpretation is displaced; compliance becomes a visible fact while belief remains private. In Mao’s campaign years, public interpretation is often mobilized as purifying activity—wall posters, study, struggle, continuous reclassification of friends and enemies—while the capacity of that activity to stabilize accountability collapses. One pattern starves the public sphere of usable dissent. The other floods the public sphere with dissent-shaped rituals that radicalize rather than settle. Both can destroy lives. They do not destroy by identical sequences, and treating them as interchangeable costumes for “totalitarianism” erases the very mechanism this chapter is supposed to isolate: **anti-repair**.

Anti-repair names what happens when corrective forms remain busy and still deepen instability. Clarification does not quiet the crisis; it supplies new materials for accusation. Exposure does not restore limits; it demonstrates that exposure itself has become a career and a survival strategy. Confession does not close a case; it trains everyone to expect the next case. The loop is cruel precisely because it can recruit moral energy. People do want corruption named. People do want privilege checked. Those wants can be sincere even inside a machinery that converts them into perpetual emergency. Sincerity does not restore repairability. It can accelerate the machine by giving it convinced operators.

Mao himself remains central to the ignition of the Cultural Revolution without becoming a one-man theory of everything. Personality mattered: his status, his strategic willingness to smash Party routines that constrained him, his cultivation of a cult of textual and personal authority. Institutional conditions mattered at least as much: a Party that had practiced mass campaigns before; a society organized into work units that could be commanded into study and struggle; youth cohorts available for mobilization; and an ideological language in which continuous revolution could justify continuous unsettlement. Reducing the decade to psychoanalysis of one ruler flatters our appetite for a single villain and disables the book’s actual question—what kind of speech still matters when speech has become the regime’s preferred evidence of purity.[^c8-macfarquhar-mao]

Nor should this chapter be used to absorb what later appears under the Khmer Rouge into the same moral slot. Cambodia’s destruction has its own chronology, scale, and forms of annihilation; Chapter 9 will insist on judgment after consequence without treating Mao’s China as a portable template you can stamp onto every communist catastrophe. Casual equivalence is a way of refusing to see. Seeing, here, means recognizing a Chinese historical sequence in which criticism proliferated while repairability failed—and then refusing to let that recognition become a license for careless analogy.

---

Plenty continued under the noise, which is precisely why absence-of-thought cannot be the diagnosis.

Interpretive activity remained intense. People reasoned about texts, sorted rumors, weighed which faction’s line would protect them this month, revised earlier denunciations when the wind changed. Moral vocabulary did not vanish. Revolutionary ethics saturated speech. Coercion remained real. Belonging remained real. Children learned which classmates’ parents had fallen. None of that returns us to the fantasy that interpretation was absent. It returns us to the colder finding: interpretation continued as obligatory practice after it had ceased to function as a brake.[^c8-dikotter-everyday]

Repairability is what thinned until naming a fault no longer made authority more answerable—it made politics more volatile. In ordinary governance, criticism can still fail and still retain its orientation toward correction: a report reveals waste; a newspaper exposes predation; a subordinate names a safety risk; somehow, sometimes, the naming revises what may be done next. During the Cultural Revolution’s radical phases, naming fault more reliably revised the list of who must now be purified. The orientation flipped from correction-toward-settlement to purification-toward-next-target. That flip is why reformist hopes kept discovering that the tools they trusted—speak out, criticize, confess, correct—had been annexed by the engine producing the crisis.

**Presence of criticism ≠ presence of repair.**

The sentence is easy to misuse. It does not counsel silence as virtue. It does not claim that every institution that solicits feedback has become Beijing in 1967. It names a diagnostic threshold. When corrective speech multiplies while each corrective act radicalizes instability, you are no longer watching medicine that needs a higher dose. You are watching a ritual whose success criterion is purity display rather than restored answerability. More of the same ritual will not recover what the ritual has already displaced.

This is why informational optimism fails the case so cleanly. Optimism says: if only people can speak, legitimacy will recover. The Cultural Revolution shows people speaking under requirement, interpreting under requirement, denouncing under requirement—while institutions lose the continuity that would make recovered legitimacy something a person could live inside. Optimism’s cousin, therapeutic optimism, fails next: if only confession is deep enough, the group will heal. Struggle confession was often deep enough to destroy. Healing did not arrive on schedule. What arrived was training in a genre of speech that treated persons as fuel.

---

The retrospective is still in session. Notebooks are still open. Someone is still being praised for naming the flaw the agenda already intended to hear. Someone else is calculating whether the harder flaw can be said without becoming next week’s problem. The facilitator asks for psychological safety and continuous improvement—or for vigilance and self-criticism—depending on the decade and the ideology. The structural question is the same, and it is not solved by better facilitation tip sheets alone. Does this criticism reopen standing so that faults can alter what authority may demand? Or does this criticism complete a purification rite whose real product is the demonstration that criticism occurred?

After the Cultural Revolution’s long demonstration that corrective speech can be hyperactive while repairability collapses, the licensed confession no longer looks like a mere bureaucratic quirk. It looks like a possible embryo of a different engine. Not an identity. Not a moral equivalence. An embryo: criticism staged as health while standing remains unopened. When the room applauds the approved failure, it may be practicing the very substitution this chapter names—performance of correction in place of correction’s work.

Once you have watched Mao’s years of hyperactive corrective speech, the contemporary meeting does not become mass persecution by analogy. That leap would be the old moral promiscuity this book refuses. What becomes visible is smaller and more usable. A room can be full of critique and empty of repair. A culture can worship candor and still punish the candor that would revise power. A person can learn to survive by offering the approved failure every quarter, and call that honesty, and not be entirely wrong about the local incentives even while something larger has gone wrong about the function of speech. The ritual is not remedy merely because it feels like courage. Courage can be conscripted. Honesty can be scheduled. Confession can be filed. None of those facts, by themselves, means that authority has become answerable.

In the Chinese historical case, demobilization eventually came—through Army intervention, Party reconstruction, the fall of particular factions, Mao’s death, and the later political turn that renamed the decade’s extremes as chaos to be escaped rather than as permanent revolution to be renewed.[^c8-macfarquhar-end][^c8-dikotter-end] Settlements returned in forms that themselves require ethical scrutiny. The point for this chapter is not to baptize the aftermath. The point is that the fever of criticism did not spontaneously convert into durable repair. Instability had to be interrupted by forces other than more purification. If the engine is hungry for purity display, feeding it more display will not invent brakes.

The criticism that cannot correct is therefore not a paradox invented by theorists sitting at a remove from archives. It is a practical machinery that appeared, at scale, when interpretation was drafted into authority’s service as continuous vigilance. Texts were read. Sessions were held. Enemies were named. Confessions were produced. Schools and offices shook. People suffered under speech that claimed to be medicine. And authority, rather than being restored to measurable limits by that speech, learned to reproduce itself by keeping the ritual hungry—criticism as output, not only as constraint.

So the chairs remain in a ring. The request for candor remains. The notebook remains ready for the fault that confirms the room’s current purity story. If this chapter has done its work, that scene no longer looks like an automatic instrument of accountability. It looks like a technology that can serve repair—or can become an engine whose output is only more motion. In Mao’s Cultural Revolution, for years at a stretch, motion won. Criticism roared. Repair, as a stabilizing return of answerable order, did not arrive on the voice of criticism alone. The presence of one never guaranteed the presence of the other. Seeing that inequality of presence is not cynicism about speech. It is the beginning of knowing when speech still can correct—and when it has already been drafted into something else.
