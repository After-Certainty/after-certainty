# **Author’s Note**

Several contemporary scenes in this book are composites.

The engineer facing a pull-request queue, the meeting that celebrates green arrows on a dashboard, the physician signing documentation at the end of a clinic day, the controller watching a sector shift from quiet to dense, and related present-day workplace moments are constructed from documented research, common professional workflows, and recurring institutional patterns. They do not portray identifiable individuals. They are written to make a structural pressure visible—how acceleration relocates scarce work—without asking the reader to treat any single fictionalized person as a reported case.

Historical scenes are reconstructed from cited sources: Taylor’s published accounts and the scholarship that has contested them; Arnold and Faurote’s reporting on Highland Park; museum and official investigations of the Model 299 crash; the Rogers Commission report on Challenger; the Kemeny Commission and subsequent human-factors literature on Three Mile Island; and related institutional records. Where historians disagree, or where a primary source is rhetorical rather than independently audited, the notes say so. The aim is fidelity to the structural lesson those episodes make available, not dramatized certainty about every contested detail.

The software and organizational material is informed by my professional experience. It is not an account of any specific employer, team, or colleague. Names, settings, and particulars in the contemporary scenes have been invented or combined so that the pattern can be examined without attaching it to a private workplace. Where a study, agency assessment, or public report supplies a number or timeline, that claim belongs to the cited source; the surrounding narrative is illustrative.

Current-event claims—including studies, agency assessments, workforce plans, and regulatory actions from 2026—reflect information available at the time of writing. Some of those claims may require re-verification before final publication, because studies are revised, plans are updated, and regulatory status can change. The book examines durable structural patterns. It does not predict how unresolved cases, policies, or deployments will end, and it should not be read as forecasting a particular company’s, agency’s, or product’s trajectory.

Where the manuscript describes automation, AI tools, or throughput mandates, the aim is diagnostic rather than prosecutorial. Acceleration can remove real toil and improve access. Automation can spare people work that was never worth their judgment. Throughput can widen who receives care, service, or infrastructure. The problem pursued here is not speed itself. It is what happens when institutions fail to redesign around the new bottleneck after a faster step relocates judgment—and proceed as if the constraint had simply disappeared.

Readers looking for an anti-AI brief, a productivity manual, or a celebration of slowness will not find one. The governing claim is narrower and, I hope, more useful: a faster step does not necessarily create a faster system. It often relocates the constraint. The chapters that follow ask whether the places that absorb that relocation remain staffed, visible, and interruptible.
