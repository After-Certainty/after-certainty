## 1. Taylor / Scientific Management — Chapter 4 (The Stopwatch)

### 1.1 Time-study practices (what was measured, how)

- **Primary claim (Taylor):** Under scientific management, “the work of every workman is fully planned out by the management at least one day in advance,” with written instructions specifying **what**, **how**, and **exact time allowed**; successful completion within the limit yields a wage premium of **30% to 100%** over ordinary wages.
  - Source: Frederick W. Taylor, *The Principles of Scientific Management* (1911). Project Gutenberg text: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6435/pg6435.html  
  - PDF excerpt (National Humanities Center): https://nationalhumanitiescenter.org/pds/gilded/progress/text3/taylor.pdf

- **Unit-time study as foundation:** Taylor states that “an accurate study of unit times must be made by one or more men connected with the planning department,” with machine tools standardized and tables of feeds/speeds prepared; small shop details (belts, tool shapes, tool-room issue under check systems) must be standardized rather than left to individual taste.
  - Source: Taylor, *Shop Management* (1903/1911 ed.). Gutenberg: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6464/pg6464.html

- **Pig-iron handling as “elementary” illustration (Taylor’s own description of the measured task):** Worker picks up a pig of iron **~92 lb**, walks a few feet/yards (in the Bethlehem field example: up an inclined plank onto a railroad car), drops it; gang averaged **~12½ long tons** per man per day; Taylor’s “first-class” target **47–48 long tons**/day.
  - Source: *Principles* (Gutenberg link above). Context: Spanish-American War pig-iron price rise; ~**80,000 tons** stored in open field at Bethlehem.

- **Stopwatch / directed rest (Taylor’s narrative of Schmidt):** Worker told by a man with a watch when to pick up, walk, sit and rest—“Now pick up a pig and walk. Now sit down and rest…” (paraphrase of Taylor’s dialogue; do not invent wording beyond the primary text if quoting).
  - Source: *Principles* pig-iron section (same Gutenberg URL). **Treat as Taylor’s rhetorical account**, not independently verified stopwatch logs (see §1.2).

- **Instruction cards (planning → shop):** Instruction card specifies drawings, piece/cost numbers, jigs/fixtures/tools, where to start each cut, depth and number of cuts, speed and feed, time within which each operation must be finished, and pay rate (piece / differential / premium).
  - Source: Taylor, *Shop Management* (functional bosses / instruction-card clerk discussion); useful secondary summary of Taylor’s A.S.M.E. analysis: http://nraoiekc.blogspot.com/2013/08/functional-foremanship-fw-taylor.html (verify against *Shop Management* primary).

### 1.2 Bethlehem Steel / “Schmidt” pig-iron story — cite with caveats

**Usable (as Taylor’s published claim):**

| Claim (from *Principles*) | Status |
|---|---|
| Setting: Bethlehem Steel / Bethlehem Iron pig loading, late 1890s (Taylor: Spanish War context) | Taylor’s account |
| Baseline ~12½ tons/man/day → target ~47 tons | Taylor’s account |
| Worker called “Schmidt”: Pennsylvania Dutchman; wages **$1.15**/day; selected for physique/ambition/thrift; promised higher pay for higher output | Taylor’s account |
| Real identity later identified as **Henry Noll** (sometimes spelled Knolle in secondary literature) | Scholarly reconstruction |

**Scholarly caveats (do not present Schmidt story as unproblematic “fact”):**

- Charles D. Wrege & Amedeo G. Perroni, “Taylor’s Pig-Tale: A Historical Analysis of Frederick W. Taylor’s Pig-Iron Experiments,” *Academy of Management Journal* 17, no. 1 (1974): 6–27. Abstract: Taylor’s loading account “has been accepted virtually without question”; investigation “reveals it to be…” (full text behind JSTOR/publisher).  
  - DOI: https://doi.org/10.2307/254767 / https://doi.org/10.5465/254767
- Follow-on: Wrege & Hodgetts, “Frederick W. Taylor’s 1899 Pig Iron Observations…” *AMJ* (2000): https://doi.org/10.2307/1556350
- Secondary discussion of story-as-object-lesson vs. documentary accuracy: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/014920630102700505 (notes Locke 1982 challenges some Wrege/Perroni criticisms; **dispute is real—flag uncertainty**).

**Drafting guidance:** Use Schmidt as *Taylor’s paradigmatic illustration of relocating judgment from worker to planner*, then one sentence noting historians have contested details and framing of the Bethlehem experiments.

### 1.3 How planning / supervision offices absorbed workers’ former work

- **Explicit transfer of planning:** “all of the planning which under the old system was done by the workman, as a result of his personal experience, must of necessity under the new system be done by the management in accordance with the laws of the science”; worker cannot “work at his machine and at a desk at the same time”; “one type of man is needed to plan ahead and an entirely different type to execute the work.”
  - Source: *Principles* (Gutenberg).

- **Functional foremanship / planning department (Taylor’s Bethlehem subdivision, as reported in contemporary management literature summarizing Taylor):**  
  **Planning department:** (1) Order-of-Work / Route Clerk; (2) Instruction-Card Clerk; (3) Time and Cost Clerk.  
  **Shop:** (4) Gang Boss; (5) Speed Boss; (6) Inspector; (7) Repair Boss.  
  **Works-wide:** (8) Shop Disciplinarian.  
  Planning separated from execution; planning department analogous to engineering department for design.
  - Cross-check primary: Taylor’s A.S.M.E. papers / *Shop Management*. Secondary digest of the eight bosses: VU digital collecties excerpt on planning departments (functional bosses list).

- **Daily written loop:** Orders to men in writing; daily returns in writing to planning department showing what was done; route of each casting/forging laid out before arrival; each operation gets an instruction card with time required.
  - Source: *Shop Management* (Gutenberg).

### 1.4 Adaptation / variation returning as “exceptions”

Concrete, citable patterns (not metaphorical filler):

- **Inspector + Repair Boss as institutionalized exception-handling:** Standardization presupposes dedicated roles for quality deviation and machine/tool recovery—work that previously sat inside craft judgment.
  - Source: Functional-foremanship lists in *Shop Management* / Taylor A.S.M.E. analyses.
- **Differential / premium pay as exception economy:** Only those who hit the scientifically set task get the large premium; variation becomes classified as success/failure against the card rather than adaptive craft.
  - Source: *Principles* task idea (30–100% premium).
- **Soldiering / output restriction as worker-side return of discretion:** Taylor treats systematic restriction of output as the central problem scientific management must crush—i.e., workers’ adaptation returns as resistance that management then measures and polices.
  - Source: *Principles* sections on soldiering (Gutenberg).
- **UNCERTAIN / secondary for “exceptions returning elsewhere”:** Labor turnover, strikes, and informal workarounds at Taylorized plants are widely discussed in labor history; for manuscript deepening, prefer a **named secondary historian** (e.g. Kanigel *The One Best Way*; Nelson on Taylorism) rather than inventing plant anecdotes. **Do not invent shop-floor exception stories.**

### 2.2 Synchronization, inspection, maintenance, pace, inability to pause

- **Work brought to the worker:** Moving line so work moves to stationary workers (rope-pulled early; later chain-driven). Ford corporate history: https://corporate.ford.com/articles/history/moving-assembly-line/
- **Chain drive regulated work speed** (not only reduced handling). The Henry Ford: https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/articles/ford-methods-and-the-ford-shops
- **Subdivision:** Magneto workers install a few parts and shove flywheel to next worker (1913 photo/context). AskUs FAQ.
- **Pace pressure / incomplete work:** Corporate history notes difficulty finishing before the car moves; missing parts; workers colliding—strict timing disliked. https://corporate.ford.com/articles/history/moving-assembly-line/
- **Wife’s letter (primary flavor):** Letter to Henry Ford from wife of assembly-line worker, **23 Jan 1914**, asking investigation of line conditions (cited as collection item). AskUs FAQ lists it among primary sources.
- **Maintenance / continuous improvement:** Separate lines for engines, chassis, bodies feeding final assembly; constant search for time/cost savings. AskUs FAQ / Henry Ford articles.
- **Inspection:** Arnold-era descriptions emphasize sequential stations and component delivery discipline; **UNCERTAIN** for a single canonical “inspection station” quote—prefer Arnold/Faurote book pages if deepening further rather than inventing station names.

### 2.3 Where the constraint moved

Well-supported pattern for the chapter’s conceptual job:

1. **Stationary assembly constraint:** labor time / carrying components to each chassis (Arnold’s carriers vs assemblers).  
2. **After moving line:** constraint shifts toward **feeding the line** (parts supply synchronization), **human endurance / turnover** (380%; $5 day as wage response), **coordination across fused lines**, and **quality at pace** (incomplete/missing work under timing).  
3. **Market feedback loop:** lower price → more demand → pressure for still more efficiency (Henry Ford institutional narrative).

**Library of Congress overview (institutional secondary):** https://guides.loc.gov/this-month-in-business-history/October/Ford

## 3. Aviation Checklists — Chapter 6 (The Checklist)

### 3.1 Origin story — Model 299 / B-17 prototype crash (verify carefully)

**Verified core facts:**

- **Date/place:** **30 October 1935**, Wright Field, Dayton, Ohio.  
- **Aircraft:** Boeing **Model 299** (prototype of what became the **B-17**).  
- **Cause (official board finding):** Not structural/engine failure; **locked rudder and elevator surface controls** (primarily elevators)—gust locks not released—made airplane uncontrollable after takeoff.  
  - National Museum of the U.S. Air Force fact sheet (institutional; fetch may be blocked by CDN—URL still citable): https://www.nationalmuseum.af.mil/Visit/Museum-Exhibits/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/610002/model-299-crash/  
  - Air Force Historical Foundation reprint of board findings excerpt: https://afhistory.org/30-oct-1935/
- **Crew (commonly cited):** Maj. **Ployer P. (“Pete”) Hill** (pilot); Lt. **Donald L. Putt** (copilot); Boeing test pilot **Leslie R. Tower** (fatally injured); others aboard. Hill and Tower died; others survived with injuries (details vary slightly by secondary—prefer museum/AF sources for fatalities).  
  - Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum: https://airandspace.si.edu/air-and-space-quarterly/winter-2023/set-checked  
  - Air & Space Forces Magazine: https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/1004fortress/

**Checklist origin — mark attribution carefully:**

- **Institutional / museum consensus:** Crash showed aircraft **too complex for memory alone**; standardized **preflight checklists** followed as practice. NASM and NMUSAF curator commentary support this causal story at high level.
- **UNCERTAIN / conflicting popular details:** Who exactly wrote the first lists (Boeing engineers vs Army Air Corps test pilots), the “1.8 million miles without accident” statistic, and exact checklist counts (**takeoff / flight / before landing / after landing**) appear in secondary retellings (Gawande *Checklist Manifesto*; aviation blogs). Prefer: “Air Corps / Boeing flight-test community developed checklists after the investigation” without inventing quotes or unverified mileage claims unless traced to a primary or museum source.
- **Gawande:** Useful popularizer, **not** a primary archive for 1935. NASM article quotes him; treat as secondary.

### 3.2 How checklists changed practice; do-confirm vs memory

**Modern institutional language (use for “how practice works now,” not as 1935 vocabulary):**

- FAA / industry methods (FAA Order 8900.1 guidance summarized in operator materials):  
  - **Challenge-Do-Verify (CDV):** challenge → action → verify (deliberate, keeps crew “in the loop”).  
  - **Do-Verify (DV)** / “cleanup”: accomplish from memory/flow, then verify with checklist (faster; higher inherent risk of missed items).  
  - Consistency of method valued.  
  - Reference portals quoting FAA 8900.1 §3-3403: https://code7700.com/checklist_flows.htm  
  - FAA AC **120-71B** (standard operating procedures / checklist roles, challenge-response): https://www.faa.gov/documentlibrary/media/advisory_circular/ac_120-71b.pdf  
  - NASA Ames CR-177549, *Human Factors of Flight-Deck Checklists* (Degani & Wiener): https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2022-11/NASA%20Ames%20Rpt%20CR%20177549%20.pdf

**Note on terminology:** Book phrase **“do-confirm”** ≈ industry **do-verify** / challenge-response verification; avoid inventing a third technical term—map to FAA language.

### 3.3 Why checklists support rather than replace judgment

Citable framing (without invented quotes):

- Crash lesson: complexity exceeded reliable **unaided memory**, not that pilots became unnecessary.  
- Modern SOP doctrine: checklists coordinate crew attention, verify configuration, and free cognitive capacity for **monitoring, decision, and non-normals**—they do not substitute for PIC authority or airmanship (see FAA AC 120-71B roles; sterile cockpit protects attention for judgment-critical phases).  
- Abnormal/emergency checklists are typically more methodical (CDV) precisely because judgment under stress needs scaffolding, not automation of thought.

### 4.2 Schedule / “pressure” as Rogers describes it (high-level, not sensational)

Primary: Vol. I Ch. 8 — https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch8.htm (archive mirror also used in research)

- After orbital flight tests (1982), NASA planned acceleration of flight rate; early ambition toward high rates; **1985 projection: 24 flights/year by 1990**; even “two per month” looked overambitious.
- **1985:** nine missions already strained capabilities; Commission doubted **15** flights scheduled for **1986** were achievable.
- Effects: compressed training; spare-parts shortages; resources focused on near-term problems; dilution of attention per flight; overtime at KSC (examples of long weeks/shifts); “can-do” culture accepting challenges that drain resources.
- **Explicit Commission finding on *outside* political pressure for 51-L:** After investigating rumors, Commission concluded the decision to launch was made by appropriate NASA officials **without outside intervention or pressure**.  
  → For the book: emphasize **internal schedule/operational pressure and communication failure**, not unverified White House/TV-deadline mythology.

Recommendation VIII (Flight Rate): nation’s reliance on Shuttle as principal launch capability created **“relentless pressure”** to increase flight rate; set rate consistent with resources; control manifest changes.  
https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1recomm.htm

### 4.3 Where authorization failed in the chain

- **Flight Readiness Review path** exists Levels IV→III→II→I; for 51-L, **relevant Level III / contractor concerns were not adequately communicated to Level I and II**. https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch5.htm
- **Launch constraints** on SRB O-ring issues and consecutive **waivers** were **not known** to Moore (Level I) or Aldrich (Level II) at FRR time. Ch. 5 / Ch. 7.
- Night of 27 Jan: Thiokol engineering **no-go** reversed by **Thiokol management** under NASA challenge; **Level II not elevated** into the temperature decision as such.
- Ch. 7 (safety org failures): problem reporting, trend analysis, criticality representation, safety involvement in critical discussions deficient. https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1ch7.htm

### 4.4 Post-accident redesign themes (high-level)

From Commission recommendations + NASA implementation reports:

- **SRM joint/seal redesign**; independent NRC oversight of redesign; test across temperature/operating conditions. https://www.nasa.gov/history/rogersrep/v1recomm.htm ; actions PDF: https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/rogersrep/actions.pdf
- **Office of Safety, Reliability and Quality Assurance** (Associate Administrator to NASA Administrator); independent of program pressures.
- **Improved communications** / launch-constraint policy / recorded FRRs; astronauts in management; STS Safety Advisory Panel.
- **Criticality 1 review**; maintenance safeguards; **flight rate** matched to resources.
- Themes for Part II return: **stop authority that can travel up the chain**; safety independent of schedule; redesign of both hardware **and** communication.

## 5. Three Mile Island (28 March 1979) — Chapter 8 (The Alarm Panel)

### 5.1 Control-room / human-factors experience

Primary: President’s Commission (Kemeny), *The Need for Change: The Legacy of TMI* (1979).  
NRC ADAMS draft/scan: https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML1927/ML19275A948.pdf  
OSTI: https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/6986994  
Readable excerpt mirror: https://pddoc.com/tmi2/kemeny/causes_of_the_accident.htm

**Citable human-factors facts from Kemeny:**

- Control panel **huge**, **hundreds of alarms**; some **key indicators** placed where operators **cannot see them**.
- First minutes: **more than 100 alarms**; **no suppression** of unimportant signals so operators could focus on significant ones.
- Displays not clear enough: pressure and temperature shown, but **no direct indication** that their combination meant coolant was **flashing to steam**.
- Design oriented to **large-break** accidents (little operator time) while TMI was a **slowly developing small-break**; human–machine interaction under confusion under-designed.

**PORV / indicator (critical comprehension failure):**

- Pilot-operated relief valve opened as designed then **failed to reseat** → small-break LOCA; continued **>2 hours** before understood.
- Control-room light showed that a **close signal had been sent**, **not** actual valve position—operators believed PORV closed.
  - Kemeny report (NRC PDF above).
  - Staff analysis: https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML1927/ML19275A963.pdf  
  - Human factors evaluation: https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML1930/ML19308C512.pdf

**Training / mental model:**

- Operators throttled **high-pressure injection (HPI)** while chasing pressurizer level (trained concern about “going solid”); Kemeny: if HPI had not been throttled, core damage might have been prevented despite stuck-open PORV.
- Procedures inadequate for stuck-open PORV as LOCA; “inappropriate” actions under required inadequate procedures (Kemeny framing).

### 5.2 Why signal abundance failed comprehension

Drafting bullets (all grounded above):

- **Volume without priority** (100+ alarms, no filtering).
- **Indication ≠ state** (command light vs valve position).
- **Parameters without meaning** (P/T without “you are making steam”).
- **Training for the wrong rare state** (large-break / solid-pressurizer fear) vs actual small-break LOCA dynamics.
- Result: more data, less **usable** situation awareness—the chapter’s conceptual center with Challenger (warning unauthorized vs warning unintelligible).

### 5.3 Post-accident human-factors reforms (high-level)

| Reform | Institutional source |
|---|---|
| **NUREG-0737** TMI Action Plan requirements (Oct 1980) | NRC NUREG series |
| **NUREG-0737 Supplement 1** (Jan 1983): Safety Parameter Display System (SPDS); **Detailed Control Room Design Reviews (DCRDR)**; upgraded **Emergency Operating Procedures**; emergency response facilities | https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/nuregs/staff/sr0737/sup1/index |
| Shift toward **symptom- / function-oriented EOPs** (reduce need for perfect event diagnosis under stress) | OSTI lessons-learned on EOPs: https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/6307206 |
| Simulator-supported training / validation of control-room changes | Embedded in Supplement 1 / licensee DCRDR practice |

## 6. Part III — Protected judgment: institutional examples (1–2+ with sources)

Pick **two** for the manuscript; others as backups.

### A. Sterile cockpit (aviation) — protects attention for judgment

- **Rule:** 14 CFR **§121.542** / **§135.100** (1981): no non-essential duties/activities during critical phases—taxi, takeoff, landing, and flight **below 10,000 ft** (except cruise).
- **Sources:**  
  - NASA ASRS *Directline* explanation + regulatory text: https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/publications/directline/dl4_sterile.htm  
  - eCFR / GovInfo CFR text: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2022-title14-vol3/pdf/CFR-2022-title14-vol3-sec121-542.pdf  
- **Book use:** Institutionalized **pause of distraction**—judgment protected by forbidding competing talk/tasks when consequence density is highest.

### B. NASA ASRS — anonymous / confidential reporting (learning without punishment)

- **Founded:** Operations began **15 April 1976** (FAA–NASA MOA **15 Aug 1975**).  
- **Design:** Voluntary, confidential, non-punitive; NASA as independent third party de-identifies; FAA funding with limits on use in enforcement; immunity incentives for timely reports (see FAA AC / ASRS immunity pages).  
- **Sources:**  
  - NASA overview: https://www.nasa.gov/human-systems-integration-division/aviation-safety-reporting-system-overview/  
  - ASRS confidentiality: https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/overview/confidentiality.html  
  - ASRS immunity: https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/overview/immunity.html  
  - First quarterly report (NTRS): https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19760026757/downloads/19760026757.pdf  
  - Case for confidential reporting (ASRS PDF): https://asrs.arc.nasa.gov/docs/rs/60_Case_for_Confidential_Incident_Reporting.pdf  
- **Book use:** Protects the **signal path for judgment** after near-misses—speed of operations does not erase institutional memory.

### C. Hospital surgical time-out / Universal Protocol (backup or third example)

- **Joint Commission Universal Protocol** for preventing wrong-site / wrong-procedure / wrong-person surgery; effective **1 July 2004** for accredited organizations.  
- **Three elements:** pre-procedure verification; site marking; **time-out** immediately before incision (active communication; fail-safe—do not start until concerns resolved).  
- **Sources:**  
  - AHRQ NCBI Bookshelf summary: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK2678/  
  - Universal Protocol text box: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK2678/box/ch36.box1/?report=objectonly  
  - AORN facts sheet: https://www.aorn.org/docs/default-source/aorndocuments/toolkits/correct-site-surgery/ccstk-universal-protocol-facts.pdf
