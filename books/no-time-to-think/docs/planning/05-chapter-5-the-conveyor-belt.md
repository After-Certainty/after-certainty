## Chapter title

Chapter 5 — The Conveyor Belt

## Governing question

When the system sets the pace, where can unfinished consequence accumulate?

## Ordinary opening image

A component moving steadily toward the next worker.

## Case / terrain

Ford’s moving assembly line combined interchangeable parts, subdivided labor, and continuous material movement. Beginning in 1913, it dramatically reduced the time required to build a Model T and helped make automobiles more affordable. It also set the pace externally and contributed to severe worker dissatisfaction and turnover.

## Conceptual movement

Acceleration often comes from redesigning a system rather than demanding greater personal effort. That is a genuine achievement. But when the work moves continuously toward the person, the system determines when the next task arrives and how much unfinished consequence can accumulate.

## Questions to preserve

- What did mass production make possible that craft production could not?
- Which forms of skill and autonomy were traded for affordability and scale?
- When the system sets the pace, where can a worker pause without stopping everyone?
- What happens when the conveyor belt becomes a queue of digital work?

## Return image

The line made assembly faster. The constraint moved into supply coordination, quality inspection, machine uptime, worker endurance, and every place the line could not stop.
