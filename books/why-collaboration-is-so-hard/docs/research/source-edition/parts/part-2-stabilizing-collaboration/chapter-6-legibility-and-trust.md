# **Chapter 6**

## **Legibility and Trust**

A nonprofit board asks the executive director for "clearer metrics" on program impact. The request sounds reasonable. Behind it is a board that does not attend site visits, does not read long-form field reports, and feels anxious about funding cycles. The director responds with dashboards: outputs, reach, cost per beneficiary. The dashboards calm the board. They also narrow what the organization optimizes for.

The collaboration between board and staff has not become more trusting. It has become more **legible**—and legibility has partially substituted for trust.

### **Visible Work Versus Invisible Work**

Organizations reward what they can see. **Visible work** maps cleanly to artifacts and timelines: features shipped, cases closed, funds raised. **Invisible work**—relationship repair, context-holding, risk anticipation—does not.

Under pressure, groups often shift investment toward visible activity even when invisible work is what prevents failure. Meetings multiply. Updates proliferate. The collaboration **appears** more active while actual coordination thins. That shift is not vanity. It is a rational response when funders, executives, or the public can only judge what shows up on a slide.

### **Why Collaboration Looks Inefficient**

From outside a healthy collaboration, coordinated action can look messy: debate, revision, pauses, side conversations. From inside, that mess may be how the group tests judgments before committing shared resources.

When observers demand efficiency, they often mean **compress the visible cycle**—fewer meetings, faster decisions, less talk. Compression can raise throughput in the short term. It can also destroy the loops that surface misunderstanding before it becomes expensive. The inefficiency is often the group's immune system, not its dysfunction.

### **Pressure for Premature Clarity**

Anxiety produces **pressure for premature clarity**: define success now, pick a metric now, end ambiguity now. Clarity is not the enemy. Premature clarity is—because it forecloses learning in exchange for orientation.

Premature clarity often arrives as templates, mandatory fields, and rigid workflows. Those tools can help newcomers and cross-functional handoffs. They can also signal that the organization trusts process more than participants' judgment. When every field must be filled before work proceeds, the group optimizes for form completion, not for the problem.

### **Narrating Progress**

When trust is thin, groups require **narrated progress**: status updates, percent-complete estimates, roadmap theater. Narration is a kind of labor. It consumes time that could go into the work itself.

Narration is not always waste. It can rebuild shared orientation across distance and turnover. It becomes coercive when narration is demanded from contributors who already lack time, or when the narrative must be optimistic to be acceptable. The tell is frequency without consequence: updates that no one acts on, but everyone must produce.

### **Legibility Substituting for Trust**

A recurring pattern: **legibility substitutes for trust**. Instead of investing in relationships, credible repair after conflict, and believable follow-through, the system demands measurable activity and explicit artifacts.

Legibility is cheaper to audit than trust. It also misaligns incentives. People learn to optimize what is counted, not what is needed. A program that hits reach targets while burning out field staff has succeeded on the dashboard and failed on the ground.

Trust-heavy collaboration still uses metrics. The difference is sequence: trust with legibility as a tool, rather than legibility as a replacement for trust. Metrics should answer questions the group already shares, not stand in for the hard work of believing one another's unseen labor.

> Groups often prioritize what can be explained over what is actually helping.
