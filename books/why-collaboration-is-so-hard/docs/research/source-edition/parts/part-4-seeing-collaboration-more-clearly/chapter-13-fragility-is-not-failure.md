# **Chapter 13**

## **Fragility Is Not Failure**

Visitors to a working research lab sometimes expect quiet focus and steady progress. What they find is argument, failed experiments, and moods that swing with results. The lab is not failing. It is **fragile in the sense that matters**: open to revision, dependent on shared contribution, unable to pretend the outcome is owned in advance.

Collaboration in healthy systems feels similar from inside: less coherent than retrospectives imply, less calm than outsiders assume, more dependent on structures that help the group survive uncertainty without demanding that live work feel like a finished story.

### **Instability as a Normal Condition**

**Instability** under shared contribution is normal, not exceptional. Diffuse ownership, uneven visibility, and partial coherence produce wobble even when people are skilled and sincere. The wobble is not proof that the wrong people were hired. It is what happens when many judgments enter a field no one fully controls.

Treating instability as defect leads to over-correction: more control, more metrics, more performed unity. Treating instability as structure leads to better questions: What practices help us survive this week? What load is uneven? What disagreement needs a container rather than a winner?

### **Retrospective Coherence**

Groups often experience coherence **only in retrospect**: after the paper is published, after the season ends, after the crisis passes. Narratives then tighten. Heroes and turning points appear. The tightening is useful for memory and for teaching newcomers what the group values. It is misleading as a template for live collaboration.

Expecting live work to feel like retrospective story is a recipe for disappointment—and for interventions that punish the messiness required to get to a story worth telling later.[^c13-sensemaking]

### **Uncertainty That Persists**

Even stable collaboration carries **persistent uncertainty**: markets shift, patients worsen, evidence updates, stakeholders change. Stability is not the absence of uncertainty. It is continued contribution while uncertainty remains—enough partial coherence to act, enough revision capacity to learn when the first act was wrong.

Organizations that confuse stability with certainty often shrink ambiguity tolerance faster than they shrink scope. They keep the same ambition but demand earlier closure. Contributors learn to offer only defensible ideas, which are usually the least informative ones.

### **Strain Versus Collapse**

**Strain** feels tense but still permits revision, dissent, and repair. People argue; they also update. **Collapse** removes those paths: compliance replaces contribution, residue replaces learning, performed agreement replaces shared movement.

Leaders who cannot distinguish strain from collapse often intervene destructively—adding control when the group needed time, or tolerating collapse when the group needed constraint. Strain can look unprofessional from a distance. Collapse can look efficient until the invoice arrives in the next collaboration.

> Fragility is not evidence that collaboration is broken. It is evidence that collaboration is real.

Real collaboration produces outcomes no one fully owns. That condition will never feel as tidy as solo mastery. The final chapter asks why humans keep attempting it anyway—and what becomes possible when they do.

[^c13-sensemaking]: On how groups narrate coherence after the fact, see Weick, *Sensemaking in Organizations*.
