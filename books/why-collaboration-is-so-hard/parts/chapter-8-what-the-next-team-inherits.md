# **Chapter 8**

## **What the Next Team Inherits**

The acronym appeared halfway through the meeting.

A consultant had been invited to help three public agencies coordinate a new regional initiative. The work involved transportation, housing, and emergency services—systems that touched the same people but rarely shared information well. The consultant had spent the morning asking careful questions about decision rights, budgets, and data.

Then she used four letters to describe a proposed planning model. Across the table, someone laughed. It was not a loud laugh. More like a breath escaping at the wrong moment. Two other people smiled without looking at one another. The consultant paused.

“Did I say something?”

A program manager shook his head. “No. Sorry. It’s just that we had an initiative with almost the same acronym a few years ago.”

Someone at the far end of the table said, “We don’t talk about that.” The room laughed this time. The consultant joined in carefully, the way people do when they know they have entered a story without understanding it. Then the meeting continued. The acronym disappeared from the slide. The feeling it produced did not.

For the rest of the morning, the consultant noticed small changes. When she suggested a shared governance group, several people became cautious. When she proposed rotating facilitation, one person said they had tried that before. When she asked whether the agencies could begin with a pilot, someone replied, “Pilots have a way of becoming permanent around here.”

None of these responses was unreasonable. Each carried information. But the information belonged partly to a project that had ended before the consultant arrived. Every collaboration begins with people remembering other rooms.

They remember the meeting where a concern was invited and later used against them. The project that began as temporary and consumed three years. The leader who promised shared ownership and kept final control. The workshop where everyone spoke honestly and nothing changed. The emergency that justified shortcuts long after the emergency ended.

A new effort may have a different purpose, different people, and different constraints. It does not begin empty. The past enters before the agenda. We often treat failed collaboration as though it ends when the project ends: the team disbands, funding expires, a report is filed, recommendations are named. The failure appears contained. Yet a retrospective can travel farther than the cost that produced it. The next room inherits the caution without inheriting the joke, the burned person, or the unofficial spreadsheet that kept the earlier work alive. What looks finished on paper may still be entering before the agenda.

Collaboration leaves residue.[^c8-weick] It remains in language, habit, policy, and the nervous system. Someone hears a familiar phrase and becomes guarded. Someone volunteers less than they once would have. A manager asks for more detailed plans because the last project produced surprises. A team insists on stronger authority because ambiguity previously became chaos. An organization adds approval steps after discovering that trust did not prevent harm.

The next collaboration inherits these responses without always inheriting the stories that produced them. This is how the past becomes structure.[^c8-march-simon] At first, residue is personal. A person who was publicly embarrassed becomes more careful before speaking. Someone whose contribution disappeared becomes less generous with unfinished ideas. A worker who absorbed invisible labor until burnout learns to ask, early and repeatedly, who owns each task. These adaptations make sense. Pain teaches. The trouble begins when adaptations outlive the conditions that made them necessary and become the environment other people must enter.

A leader once withheld information until the last moment. The organization responds by requiring weekly status reports from every future project.

A cross-functional team failed because no one had authority to resolve disputes. The next initiative assigns one executive final control over everything.

A volunteer partnership collapsed after informal promises were misunderstood. The institution requires every future interaction to pass through a formal agreement.

Each structure contains a memory. The memory may no longer be visible. The status report looks like ordinary governance. The executive authority looks like clear accountability. The formal agreement looks like professionalism. Only people who lived through the earlier failure know what fear the structure was designed to prevent. This does not make the structure wrong.

Some failures reveal genuine weaknesses. A group that could not identify who had authority may need clearer decision rights. An organization surprised by repeated delays may need better reporting. A partnership damaged by vague commitments may need written agreements. The lesson becomes dangerous when protection from the last failure becomes the purpose of the next collaboration.

Then the new work is asked to prove it will not become the old work before it has had a chance to become anything at all. This is one reason experienced people can appear cynical to newcomers. The newcomer sees possibility. The experienced person sees recurrence. A new director proposes a listening tour. Someone who has attended three previous listening tours wonders what will happen after the listening.

A consultant recommends a working group. Staff remember the group that produced recommendations leadership never read. A leader announces that the initiative will be different because this time everyone will have a voice. People who have heard that sentence before notice that authority still sits in the same place. The newcomer experiences resistance. The experienced person experiences pattern recognition.

The sorting can look like temperament: one person wants to move; the other wants to stall; one believes; the other has given up. The moral clarity fades when the present is allowed to contain more than character. The new leader may genuinely be different. The current project may have stronger sponsorship and clearer constraints. The experienced person may be protecting the group from repeating a failure no one else understands—or treating resemblance as destiny. What looks like a difference in disposition may still be a difference in how much of the past each person is being asked to carry into the room.

Neither view is complete. Residue makes interpretation difficult because the present often contains enough of the past to justify caution and enough difference to make certainty unfair. Cynicism solves this difficulty by turning caution into a worldview. It says the project will not work. Leadership will not listen. The process is theater. The people with power will rewrite the story later.

Cynicism can sound like superiority, as though the cynical person has escaped the naïveté of everyone still willing to try. Often it is something sadder. Cynicism is disappointed prediction hardened into identity.[^c8-hirschman] The person believed before. They invested, attended the meetings, offered judgment, repaired relationships, and kept working after the visible enthusiasm disappeared. Then the organization changed direction, claimed success, assigned blame, or moved on without acknowledging what the effort cost.

Cynicism protects them from paying that price again. It asks less of hope. The cynicism is a risk-management strategy. If you expect nothing, the institution cannot surprise you by offering nothing. If you assume the decision has already been made, you will not mistake consultation for influence. The strategy reduces pain. It also reduces the very contribution collaboration needs.

The person still attends. They no longer bring the unfinished observation. They wait for someone else to name the contradiction. They know where the plan is likely to fail and decide that discovering this is now leadership’s responsibility. From the outside, this can look like disengagement. From inside the person, it may feel like accuracy. Learned silence often begins this way. Not with a prohibition. With evidence.

Someone raises a concern. The concern is dismissed, reframed, or acknowledged without consequence. Later, the predicted problem occurs and is described as unforeseeable. The next time, the person remains quiet. Others notice. The room becomes easier. Leaders may interpret the absence of objection as improved alignment. In reality, the system has taught one of its sensors to stop sending a signal. This is how residue can make a future collaboration look healthier at precisely the moment it becomes less informed.[^c8-hirschman]

What has disappeared may not be conflict. It may be contribution. Silence can spread even among people who did not experience the original failure. Organizations teach through stories, jokes, and observation. A project becomes known as a career dead end. A committee becomes a place where recommendations go to disappear. Nobody needs to issue formal guidance. People learn the landscape from those who survived it. The acronym becomes a warning. The joke preserves it without reopening the wound.

“We tried collaboration once.”

“Nothing is more permanent than a pilot.”

The joke creates belonging among people who remember. It also makes hope embarrassing. The group protects itself by making skepticism the price of admission. Originally, the organization punished doubt. Later, the survivors punish belief. Both responses narrow what people are allowed to contribute. A group can become trapped between forced optimism and compulsory cynicism. One denies the cost of failure. The other denies the possibility of revision.

Residue also survives through blame. After collaboration collapses, people need an explanation that gives the failure a shape—the leader was controlling, the team resisted, someone should have known. Each explanation may contain truth, and each may be too small. A bad leader may have operated inside a system that rewarded confidence and punished revision. A resistant team may have inherited unacknowledged costs from earlier initiatives. A partner may have broken a promise that neither institution had enough authority or capacity to keep.

Structural explanation does not absolve people. It asks a different question: What made the harmful behavior likely, useful, or survivable? Without that question, organizations replace the person and preserve the arrangement. The next leader receives the same pressures. The next team learns the same defensive habits. The next project fails differently enough to appear new.

This is how institutions repeat themselves while insisting they have learned. A lessons document may list what happened without changing what the organization rewards. The retrospective says decisions should be made closer to the work; the next leader is still punished for surprises. The report says frontline concerns must be surfaced earlier; the schedule still makes early concerns expensive. The review says shared ownership was unclear; the organization names one owner without giving them control over the dependencies. The lesson becomes another sentence added to the process. The underlying pressure remains.

This is residue becoming ritual: a mandatory risk section no one believes can alter the plan, a stakeholder review added after a project failed to include the right people, a sign-off required from teams without enough time to understand what they are approving. Each protection was created for a reason. Together, they make contribution slower, more formal, and less trusting.

Eventually, people experience the weight of history without access to its logic. They call it bureaucracy. Often bureaucracy is institutional memory after the story has been lost. Some rules survive only because no one has enough authority to remove them. Some protect reputation rather than people. Some were designed around conditions that no longer exist. But removing a structure without recovering the memory inside it can recreate the harm the structure was built to contain.

The work of revision begins with curiosity. Why is this here? What happened before it existed? Who was protected by it? Who has been burdened by it? What current condition would make it unnecessary? This is slower than asking whether the process can be simplified. It treats the process as sediment, not merely clutter. Many organizations call this technical debt, process debt, or institutional inertia. Those phrases can be useful. They can also carry contempt, making the old system evidence that previous people were careless, irrational, or resistant to improvement. More often, the system reflects adaptations to pressures the present team has not yet encountered.

Understanding the past does not require preserving all of it. It allows the present to change it without pretending it came from nowhere. The regional initiative with the unfortunate acronym had failed several years earlier after beginning with considerable enthusiasm.

Three agencies had agreed to coordinate services for people moving between temporary housing, public transportation, and emergency care. The idea was straightforward. Each agency saw a different part of the same person’s life. Better information-sharing might reduce repeated assessments and missed handoffs.

A steering committee was created. Working groups formed. The project received a memorable name. For several months, people contributed intensely.

Frontline staff mapped where clients were falling between systems. Analysts compared data fields. Legal teams identified privacy constraints. Leaders announced that the initiative would create a seamless regional response.

Then the seams became visible. The agencies had different funding rules. They defined a successful outcome differently.

One could change software quickly but had little authority over operations. Another could change operations but depended on a state system it did not control. A third had the strongest data and the least flexibility in how the data could be shared.

The steering committee discussed these tensions, but the public promise had already been made. Each agency began protecting the part of the initiative it could report as progress. Meetings multiplied. The shared plan became less shared and more detailed. Frontline staff continued doing translation work outside the formal process. When the project narrowed into a smaller data-sharing agreement, leaders described it as a successful first phase.

Many participants experienced it as failure. Some information moved more reliably than before, but the official story omitted the labor, conflict, and abandoned ambitions that participants remembered. People did not merely distrust future regional initiatives. They distrusted the language used to describe them. Shared governance meant no one could decide.

Pilot meant permanent work without permanent funding. Success meant leadership had found a way to narrate whatever remained. This is why the consultant’s innocent acronym changed the room. She had not named the old project. She had touched the vocabulary through which the old project survived. Repairing residue begins by allowing the old story to become complete enough that the people who lived through it do not have to choose between silence and sarcasm. The consultant stopped the afternoon session and asked about the earlier initiative. At first, people offered brief summaries. It was too ambitious. Governance was unclear. The technology was not ready. Then someone said, “That’s the official version.” The consultant waited.

A frontline manager explained how staff had maintained unofficial spreadsheets because the shared system never included the information they needed. A legal adviser described warnings that had been treated as solvable details until the public announcement made revision politically difficult. A former project lead said she had been given responsibility for coordination without authority over any agency’s budget.

Another person noted that the project had still produced something valuable: people across the agencies now knew whom to call during complicated cases. The room held two truths. The initiative had failed to become what it promised. It had also created relationships that remained useful years later. Failure had not been total. Success had not been clean. The old project became less useful as a joke and more useful as knowledge.

That movement matters. Residue becomes most dangerous when the past can be remembered only as triumph or disaster. Triumph erases cost. Disaster erases what people learned and built along the way. A more complete memory asks what the collaboration made possible, what it extracted, what assumptions failed, what capacities remained, and who paid for continuity. These questions do not produce a simple lesson. They produce a usable inheritance: a past that can be examined without governing every future decision.

“Let go” often means the institution would like relief from the consequences of what it has not repaired.

People cannot be instructed out of residue. Trust does not return because leadership announces a fresh start. A new name does not create new conditions. A fresh start without changed authority, changed consequences, or changed exit costs asks people to forget what the body still remembers. Reassurance travels. Evidence accumulates more slowly.

Repair requires evidence that the system has changed where the earlier cost was produced—authority made visible, concerns that can alter decisions, maintenance redistributed, dissent recorded while the work is underway. Tone helps. Apology helps. A sincere leader can reopen possibility. But credibility accumulates through consequences: a concern changes the plan, a pilot reaches its end and is funded, stopped, or redesigned, a report names what did not work without converting it into someone else’s failure. These moments tell people the present may resemble the past without being required to repeat it. Collaboration does not require forgetting. It requires enough evidence that memory can become information rather than prophecy.

Diagnostic questions matter less as a checklist than as a way of separating the present problem from the inherited one. The skeptical person may not be resistant to collaboration; they may be responding to a high exit cost. The leader asking for detail may be carrying accountability for surprises the organization punishes harshly. The person insisting on written commitments may have spent years repairing informal promises no one else remembered making.

Understanding these pressures does not mean accepting every defensive behavior. A leader can still over-control. A skeptic can still sabotage. A keeper of history can still use complexity as power. The questions restore proportion. They help the group see when a personal conflict is carrying a structural inheritance. One of the most powerful questions is also the simplest: What would make this attempt different?

Not what slogan will distinguish it. What condition has changed? Is there new authority? More time? A smaller scope? A repair mechanism that did not exist before? A clearer boundary? A different distribution of risk? A person who says, “This time will be different,” is offering reassurance. A person who can point to changed conditions is offering evidence.

The distinction matters because residue is often a record of promises outrunning structure. The old initiative promised integration without shared authority. The new one might begin with a narrower commitment: a single handoff between two agencies, limited data, defined ownership, and a date when the arrangement must be reviewed. Grand promises produce emotional energy. Bounded commitments produce believable next steps.

The new regional team eventually chose a smaller beginning. They did not create a shared governance council for all three agencies. They chose one recurring failure: people discharged from emergency care who lacked transportation to temporary housing after evening bus service ended. The agencies already knew the problem. They had previously treated it as belonging partly to everyone and fully to no one.

This time, they named the constraints. The transit agency could not add a route immediately. The housing program could not keep its front desk open all night. The hospital could identify eligible patients earlier but could not operate transportation. A community partner could provide rides on certain evenings if funding and liability were clear. The plan was modest: a defined group of patients, a three-month period, one agency responsible for payment, one partner responsible for rides, the hospital responsible for identifying need before discharge, a weekly review of missed cases, and a decision at the end to stop, continue with funding, or redesign.

The people in the room did not feel transformed. Some remained skeptical. One person said the plan looked suspiciously like another pilot. The difference was that the concern did not remain a joke.

The group wrote down what would prevent the pilot from becoming permanent by neglect. Funding would end on a specific date. Continuation would require an explicit decision. The community partner would not be expected to absorb unpaid expansion. Missed cases would be reviewed without treating frontline staff as the sole source of failure.

These conditions did not guarantee success. They made the risk more inhabitable. During the first month, the plan worked unevenly. Several patients received rides. One was missed because transportation need was identified too late. Another waited nearly two hours because the community partner had only one available driver. The weekly review did not produce a green dashboard. It produced a better map.

The hospital moved the transportation question earlier in the discharge process. The partner clarified the hours it could reliably cover. The housing program created a late-arrival protocol.

The group changed the work before declaring the work aligned. That was different.

Near the end of the pilot, the consultant used the old acronym again by accident. This time, nobody laughed. Someone noticed and said, “We should probably choose a different name.” The room agreed. The change was small. Names matter less than conditions, but the decision recognized that language carries history. They chose a plain name. No promise of transformation. No claim that the region had become seamless. The name described the handoff. For once, the work was not required to sound larger than it was.

The old acronym did not disappear from institutional memory. People still used it occasionally as shorthand for what had gone wrong. But it no longer governed every new proposal. The story had become more complete.

The earlier initiative had overpromised, diffused authority, and relied on invisible labor. It had also created relationships and revealed where the agencies did not fit together.

The next team inherited both. That may be the most honest thing one collaboration can leave another. Not a clean lesson. Not a warning never to try again. A record of what became possible, what became costly, and what must be different before hope deserves another investment. The meeting where the acronym first appeared ended several months earlier with careful laughter. The later meeting ended with a smaller plan, an unfinished map, and people who still did not entirely trust the process.

They stayed anyway.

Not because the past had been erased.

Because it had finally been allowed into the room without being given the final word.
