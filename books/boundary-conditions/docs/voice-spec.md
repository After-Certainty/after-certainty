# GENRE AND READER CONTRACT

**Organizational fiction** (modern business novel): a real workplace under stress, told as a **page-turning story** first—not a disguised essay, case study, or leadership blog.

Readers should feel:

- pulled through scenes the way they are pulled through a well-paced workplace novel,

- smarter about coordination, boundaries, and reliability when they finish a chapter,

- emotionally invested in **whether Nate and the org will break the right way**, not merely intellectually correct.

This book is **more emotionally reflective** than the lightest examples of the genre: Nate’s interior life and family parallel matter. Reflection must **ride inside scenes**, not pause the story for thesis paragraphs.

**Not primarily about technology.** Tech is the pressure chamber—authorization, identity, incident machinery—through which human coordination and institutional behavior are revealed.

---

# ENGAGEMENT (WHAT “SIMILAR STYLE” MEANS HERE)

Without naming other titles, target these **observable habits**:

| Habit | On the page |
|--------|-------------|
| **Scene-first** | Open with a concrete situation—clock, place, message, meeting—before abstraction |
| **Forward motion** | Each scene changes what characters can or cannot do next |
| **Dialogue carries weight** | Bridges, 1:1s, and DMs do exposition through conflict and choice, not narrator summary |
| **Accessible prose** | Short-to-medium sentences; plain words; jargon only when the room would say it, then grounded in stakes |
| **Recognizable humor** | Wry recognition of enterprise absurdity (templates, bots, exec language)—not sitcom quips |
| **Chapter pull** | End with pressure, a decision, a reveal, or a question the reader needs answered |
| **Human stakes** | Customer risk, career risk, family logistics, reputation—never “the company” as abstract villain |

Pacing is **brisk in Acts I–III** (incident heat). **Recalibration chapters** (see [recalibration.md](./recalibration.md)) deliberately slow down—still scene-based, not essayistic.

---

# CHARACTERS (ROOTABLE, DISTINCT, NOT CARTOONS)

Characters should feel like **people readers have worked with**, not symbols in a morality play.

| Role | Voice on the page |
|------|-------------------|
| **Nate** | Calm, synthesizing, dry when tired; competence without swagger; interior restraint—insight through observation |
| **Caleb** | Younger, committed, learns in public; humor under stress; generational mirror to Nate |
| **Warren** | Politically careful, not evil; short measured sentences; performs calm for executives |
| **Owen** | Product pragmatism; tired clarity; legitimate revenue and customer fear |
| **Priya** | Platform truth-teller; precise, unromantic about ownership |
| **Eli** | Credible escalator; no drama; earns attention by *not* crying wolf |
| **Hadley** | Perceptive, grounded; challenges Nate without nagging spouse cliché |
| **Family** | Rachel (support that centralizes work), Mark (avoidance that forces Nate’s role), Daniel (dignity in decline) |

**Engagement rule:** readers should **care what happens to Nate and Caleb** in the next scene, not only whether the technical fix is correct.

Avoid: villain CEOs, idiot middle managers played for laughs, genius savior engineers, strawman Security or Product.

# TONE

- Intelligent, realistic, systems-oriented, **emotionally restrained but not cold**
- Contemporary workplace English—contractions, idioms of tech enterprises without parody
- **Clarity over flourish**; cinematic readability; one earned image beats three decorative ones
- Emotion through **behavior, silence, physical detail, and dialogue**—not manifesto narration
- **Dialogue often and at length** in organizational scenes; family scenes may be tighter and quieter

---

# NARRATION

- **Close third on Nate** unless [act-chapter-index.md](./act-chapter-index.md) assigns another POV
- Anchor Nate early (name, body, clock, screen) when practical
- **Plain sense first:** what happened on the bridge, in the doc, on the call—then at most one earned comparison
- **Camera-observable:** glass walls, parking garage migration, laptop rotated on a desk, rain on airport windows
- Technical concepts **in scene** (sequence diagram, repro steps, Sev One channel)—not white-paper dumps
- **Show systems through choices:** who schedules the meeting, who signs the field, who goes silent—not labeled “dysfunction” from the narrator

---

# DIALOGUE

Deep revision pass: **[04-dialogue-voice.md](./agents/04-dialogue-voice.md)** (after agents 01–03).

- **Sayable under stress**—what tired engineers and directors actually say on a bridge
- **Meetings are dramatic engines:** disagreement, interruption, polite deflection, sudden clarity
- **Profanity sparing but real** when heat warrants; do not sanitize into corporate brochure voice
- **Rapid attribution** on bridge calls: name or role before stacked “he said” confusion
- Product vs Security disagreements stay **human and legitimate**
- Executive and comms language may sound performative—that is in-world, not author sneer

---

# PARAGRAPH AND PACING

- **No outline bullets in manuscript prose**—workbook lists become woven sentences
- Prefer **paragraphs that move** over stacked fragments unless rhythm is intentional (bridge roll call, staccato recognition)
- Alternate **organizational pressure** and **intimate human scenes** within each act (see [side-story-chapter-map.md](./side-story-chapter-map.md))
- Urgent acts: overlap of Slack, bridge, family text—without losing POV clarity
- Recalibration act: fewer simultaneous channels, quieter scenes, **still forward-moving**

---

# TECHNICAL AUTHENTICITY

- Authorization, delegated identity, profile sync, consent, and audit trails must **feel credible** to senior engineers, architects, security, and product leaders
- Explain through **action:** repro steps, who owns the flag, what Legal will sign—not lecture
- Avoid brand-name companies and celebrity-CEO cosplay
- AI-assisted security analysis appears as **pressure and tooling**, not magic oracle or cartoon doom

---

# ANTI-PATTERNS (GENRE BREAKERS)

- Literary distance, MFA lyricism, or thesis paragraphs that sound like a consulting deck
- Melodrama, caricature villains, simplistic corporate satire
- “Hero engineer saves the company” fantasy
- Theme labels in narration (*invisible labor*, *boundary conditions*) without dramatic proof
- Author/planning meta in prose (Act numbers, “this chapter,” beat names)
- Slow middle scenes where nothing changes except mood
