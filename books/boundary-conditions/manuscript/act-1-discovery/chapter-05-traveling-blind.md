# Chapter 05 — Traveling Blind

Nate’s return flight was scheduled for Sunday at six in the evening, which meant he spent most of the weekend pretending he could be in two places at once and the last four hours proving he could not.

Saturday night had ended with Caleb’s bridge quieter and two pull requests actually merging. Sunday morning began with the leadership thread doing what leadership threads did when engineering made progress without giving executives a story they could repeat.

His mother made pancakes again. His father read the newspaper on his phone in the way older men did when they wanted the gesture of news without the risk of being asked to comment on it. Rachel packed leftovers into foil with the efficiency of someone who had hosted before.

Nate ate standing up with his laptop open on the kitchen counter.

The leadership summary for Sunday six a.m. Eastern was no longer calm in the way the Thursday night brief had been calm. It was calm in a different register—tighter, more defensive, as if the company had decided the appearance of control required sharper adjectives.

*Cross-functional mitigation in progress. Limited regional containment implemented. No confirmed active exploitation. Ongoing assessment of historical exposure.*

No confirmed active exploitation.

The phrase had become a refrain. Nate had started hearing it the way he heard airline safety videos, language designed to soothe people who needed to believe the plane was conceptually fine.

In the private leadership thread, the tone was less soothing.

A vice president Nate had met once at an all-hands asked why Security had escalated to Sev One when the prior assessment had not triggered the same response. Another executive wondered whether the timing was driven by automated scanning tools that made every old finding look new. Warren replied with measured paragraphs about duty of care and regulatory context, which was Warren’s way of saying *stop asking me in Slack* without saying it.

Nate did not reply in that thread.

He had learned, over years of being useful, that leadership conversations were not always requests for information. Sometimes they wanted reassurance. Sometimes they wanted a scapegoat thin enough to fit through a phone screen.

---

His parents’ departure started before lunch.

His mother checked the oven twice even though nothing was in it. His father folded a jacket he had already folded and asked Rachel whether the rental car return was on the airport side or the hotel side, then asked again eleven minutes later with the same tone of mild offense, as if the question had been rude the first time.

“Airport side,” Rachel said patiently. “Same as when we picked you up.”

“Right,” his father said. “Airport side.”

Nate watched the repetition and felt the familiar sibling dread—not crisis, not emergency, just the small signal that his father’s memory was becoming selective about which details deserved permanence.

Rachel caught his eye across the kitchen and shook her head once. Not today. Not at graduation weekend. They would have the logistics conversation later, on a call that would not include pancakes.

Nate’s mother hugged him at the door with the strength of someone who believed hugs could substitute for answers about work.

“Try to sleep on the plane,” she said.

“I will.”

His father shook his hand. “That work thing still going?”

“Quieter on the surface,” Nate said—the same sentence he had used at brunch, which was true in the way surfaces were true.

“Good,” his father said, and meant it.

They left for the rental return. Rachel stayed behind to drive Nate to the airport later, which gave him one more hour of incident channels and one fewer hour of pretending the weekend had been only family.

Caleb called from the bridge at noon Mountain Time, voice clearer than the night before.

“We shipped regional disable in two zones,” he said. “Identity posted updated policy language for review. Legal still owns Monday, but Owen got customer comms into draft with the word ‘precautionary’ instead of ‘breach,’ if that counts as progress.”

“It counts,” Nate said.

“Warren wants to know if you can join a thirty-minute exec readout at four Eastern.”

Nate looked at the clock. Rachel was due to leave for campus in twenty minutes to return rental gowns. His parents were already on the highway toward home.

“I can do it from the airport,” he said.

“Copy.”

He did not tell Caleb about the leadership thread’s subtext—that executives were not asking whether the mitigation was correct, but whether Security had embarrassed the company by naming the problem too loudly. That was a translation job for later, when Nate had a keyboard and not a boarding pass in his hand.

---

Warren called him directly at two fifteen Mountain Time, which meant Warren was eating something while reading a phone and pretending not to.

“Quick question before the readout,” Warren said. “Why now. That’s what they’re asking. Not whether the repro is real. Why now.”

Nate stood at Rachel’s kitchen counter with the departure board open on his laptop and the leadership thread still glowing in another tab.

“Because Eli reproduced a cross-profile write in production timing this week,” Nate said. “Because the prior assessment did not include that path. Because Security’s duty-of-care threshold changed when the failure became operational instead of theoretical.”

“Say it shorter.”

“Because the risk picture changed when we could show harm, not only describe it.”

Warren exhaled. “Good. Use that in the readout. Do not use the word *embarrassed* in any channel.”

“I wasn’t planning to.”

“Caleb’s primary,” Warren said. “Keep it that way through Monday.”

“Already is.”

The call ended. Rachel came in with a garment bag over her shoulder and looked at Nate’s face.

“Warren?” she asked.

“Warren,” he agreed.

“Bad?”

“Not bad. Narrative.”

She made a face that said she had married into the wrong industry by proxy. “Airport in ninety. Try not to save the company in the security line.”

---

At the airport security line, his father’s absence was noticeable in small ways—no slow belt repacking, no commentary about TSA efficiency. Nate moved faster and felt guilty about it.

Rachel took a selfie with the departure board in the background and sent it to a group chat Nate was not in.

The incident followed him through TSA anyway.

PagerDuty chirped once—not an escalation, a reminder that he was still listed as backup anchor. The hourly summary bot invited him to edit Hour 18. A Legal coordinator asked whether “delegated relationship” was customer-facing language or internal taxonomy, which told Nate that Monday would be long before Monday arrived.

He found a seat near the window at the gate and opened the executive readout bridge early because Warren was always early when the audience was senior.

Four executives. Warren. Owen for thirty seconds of customer language. A Security director. Nate. Caleb muted in the background to take notes, which was how Caleb had learned to stay in rooms where titles outranked tenure.

Warren presented the template.

Mitigation underway. Limited exposure scenarios. No active exploitation confirmed. Next update Monday.

An executive whose name Nate associated with revenue asked the question he had been expecting since Thursday.

“Why are we hearing about this now if the assessment found it months ago?”

Warren looked at Nate.

Nate had been in enough exec readouts to know what the look meant. It meant *give them a sentence they can repeat* and also *do not give them a sentence that creates liability*.

“The prior finding did not include a reproducible cross-profile write in production timing,” he said. “This week’s reproduction changed the risk picture. Security escalated when that became clear.”

True enough to stand.

Incomplete enough to worry him.

The revenue executive nodded without looking convinced.

“And we’re sure it’s contained?”

Owen unmuted. “Precautionary regional disable is live in two zones. Customer comms are in Legal review. I’m not calling it contained. I’m calling it visible.”

A Security director added monitoring language. Warren closed with Monday timeline.

The readout ended four minutes early.

Caleb messaged immediately.

*You still there?*

*At the gate.*

*Execs seemed… tired.*

*They’re not tired. They’re managing narrative.*

Nate stared at the sentence after he sent it. Managing narrative was accurate. It was also the kind of phrase that would sound cynical if the wrong person repeated it. He hoped Caleb would treat it as forecast, not worldview.

Boarding started.

He folded the laptop halfway, then opened it again when Warren appeared in a private chat.

*Good job today. Keep Caleb primary. I need you fresh Monday.*

Fresh Monday meant back in the office, back in the center gravity, back in the role the organization knew how to use. Nate typed acknowledgment and joined the boarding line with his phone still warm in his hand.

On the plane he had no Wi‑Fi for the first twenty minutes.

It was almost peaceful.

He used the gap to eat the pretzels Rachel had shoved into his backpack and to watch his reflection in the dark window—tired, present, still listed as backup anchor in a disaster he was no longer running. The seatbelt sign had dinged. The cabin smelled like recirculated coffee and plastic. Ordinary travel, except his shoulders stayed braced the way they did when a bridge was one notification away from asking him to translate fear into sentences executives could repeat.

When connectivity returned mid-climb, the leadership thread resumed with a new question from someone in Corporate Communications about whether the incident should be described internally as a “platform reliability event” or a “trust incident.”

Warren had already answered once—measured, careful, noncommittal. Someone in Legal reacted with a link to a taxonomy doc Nate had never seen outside audits. A Security director said trust incident would alarm the board. Infrastructure’s director of reliability said platform reliability would alarm customers who were not yet affected. The thread became an argument about routing fear, not about facts.

Nate did not reply. He drafted a private note to Caleb instead.

*If comms picks platform reliability, exec brief will shrink Security’s role in the story. If they pick trust incident, Product will fight every mitigation that touches conversion. Make Warren choose in the executive doc, not in Slack.*

Caleb replied three minutes later.

*Posted as a decision row. Warren owes an answer before Hour 20.*

That was Caleb now—not waiting for Nate to see the fight first.

Nate read the thread twice.

Platform reliability event would route fear toward Infrastructure and Customer Systems. Trust incident would route fear toward Security, Legal, and anyone who had signed prior assessment responses.

Nobody in the thread asked which description was true.

That was the part that stayed with him through descent into his city, through the taxi line, through the muted autoplay of a podcast he had not chosen and could not remember selecting.

The exploit was still real. Eli’s reproduction was still real. Priya’s diagram was still real. Regional disable had shipped and would help and would not answer the question Nate had put in the open box on Thursday.

What was still missing was a shared name for the failure.

Every team had chosen a frame that made their corner legible and their risk smaller. Caleb could keep the bridge steady that way. It still would not tell them where they were going.

Nate collected his bag from the carousel at home airport—the same belt, the same fluorescent hum he had left from two days earlier with rain on the windows and a laptop still open on his credenza. The city air was damp and familiar. No Rachel with a joke sign. No parents asking whether the work thing had settled. Just him and the wheel of his carry-on and the incident still buzzing in his pocket like it had followed him through TSA twice.

He ordered a rideshare and opened the engineering channel one more time before getting into the car.

Priya had posted overnight about consent path audit. Eli had added a variant repro. Infrastructure asked for a rollback plan. Owen asked for customer segment counts. Identity reminded everyone that policy semantics were not owned in the thread.

Not our system.

Not our roadmap.

Not our table.

Nate closed the app and watched his breath fog the rideshare window slightly.

Monday would pull him back into the center. He knew that already. Warren had said fresh as if fresh meant restored, when Nate suspected it meant available.

For now he was home—still backup anchor, still the person leadership called when fear needed translating into slides—and still unable to name the problem in any channel where naming it would stick.

The car merged onto the highway toward his neighborhood.

His phone buzzed once more. Caleb had left a message in his inbox: hour nineteen summary drafted, no major changes, bridge steady. Warren had left a separate message Nate did not open until the rideshare was moving.

*Why now is answered for tonight. Platform vs trust still open. Caleb owns it until you land Monday.*

Nate read it twice. Warren had not chosen platform or trust. He had chosen Caleb—which was, Nate suspected, the closest thing to wisdom available at hour nineteen.

He typed back to Caleb before putting the phone face down on his thigh.

*Good work. Keep going.*

He meant it.

He also knew, with the tired clarity that came after long incidents and longer family weekends, that steady was not the same as understood.

Understood was what they had not yet become. Monday would ask for names again. The company was still better at naming fears than naming the failure.
