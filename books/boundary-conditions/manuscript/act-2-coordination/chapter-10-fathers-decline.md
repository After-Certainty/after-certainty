# Chapter 10 — Father's Decline

The neurologist’s office had wall art meant to soothe people who did not want to be there. Nate noticed it because his father noticed the fish in the poster instead of the receptionist asking whether Daniel Mercer had completed the intake form online.

“I did that,” Daniel said, patting his coat pocket as if the form might still be folded inside.

“You did it on paper last time,” the receptionist said gently.

Daniel smiled the way he smiled when he was caught in a small mistake and wanted the mistake to be someone else’s problem. “Paper is more reliable.”

Nate stepped forward.

“He completed the online form,” Nate said. “I can show the confirmation on my phone if that helps.”

The receptionist nodded, grateful and tired in the way medical front desks always were.

Daniel looked at Nate with a flash of irritation Nate recognized from childhood—not anger at help, anger at needing it.

---

The appointment had not been Nate’s idea originally.

His mother had called on Sunday while he was rewriting an hourly summary, voice bright in the way that meant she was managing fear with logistics.

“Your father’s repeating himself,” she said. “Not dramatically. Just enough that I thought we should schedule the follow-up sooner.”

“When?”

“Wednesday. I can drive. Unless you’re—”

“I’ll take him,” Nate said, because his calendar had a two-hour gap labeled *focus time* that had not been focus time in three weeks, and because saying no would have meant trusting the gap to stay empty, which it would not.

He had not told Warren. He had told Caleb, who had reacted with a thumbs-up and a message that said *go*, which was Caleb’s entire philosophy about humans sometimes needing to be humans.

Tuesday night Hadley had found Daniel’s medication list on the printer tray—printed twice, different dates in the header—and a sticky note on the router that said *wifi password* with nothing written under it.

“He was trying to fix the guest network,” Hadley said.

“He was trying to remember it,” Nate said.

They had not moved the sticky note. Daniel would notice if the house changed without explanation.

Wednesday morning Nate arrived at his parents’ house forty minutes early because Daniel had called him twice asking what time the appointment was and whether he needed his insurance card, which was already in Nate’s bag because his mother had texted a photo of the card front and back with instructions to *bring both sides*.

Daniel met him at the door in a jacket suited for a colder month.

“You’re early,” Daniel said.

“I’m on time for us,” Nate said.

In the car Daniel asked about work. Nate gave the short version. Daniel asked again at the first red light as if the short version had not arrived.

At the second light Daniel said, “Rachel called. She said you’re underwater.”

“Rachel is generous,” Nate said.

“Is the loud problem still loud.”

“Yes,” Nate said.

Daniel nodded and looked out the window, satisfied that loud problems stayed loud until someone competent handled them. Nate did not correct the implication.

---

In the exam room Daniel sat on the paper-covered table with his hands on his knees, dignified, suspicious of the blood-pressure cuff as if it were a personal insult.

The doctor was younger than Nate expected and very good at speaking to both of them at once without sounding condescending to either.

“Mr. Mercer, I’m reviewing the cognitive screen from last month and today’s visit notes. I want to ask a few questions about daily routines—medications, appointments, passwords, bills.”

Daniel straightened.

“I handle my bills.”

“Mom handles the bills,” Nate said, then wished he had waited.

Daniel’s jaw tightened.

“I authorize the bills.”

The doctor nodded. “That’s common in households. I’m asking whether you’ve noticed difficulty remembering login steps, appointment dates, or repeating questions within the same conversation.”

“I repeat questions when people don’t listen,” Daniel said.

Nate kept his eyes on the doctor.

“He asked me three times this morning whether the appointment was today,” Nate said. “He asked twice at breakfast whether Rachel had already left for work. She doesn’t live here.”

Daniel stared at the floor.

“I knew that,” he said quietly.

The doctor asked Daniel to walk through paying a bill online—not to test his pride, she said, to understand where steps broke down.

Daniel described opening the browser, finding the bookmark, entering credentials. He stopped at *two-factor* and looked at Nate.

“You changed the phone,” Daniel said.

“We added the second factor last year,” Nate said.

“After the breach scare,” Daniel said, as if the breach were recent.

The doctor made notes without reacting.

“Mr. Mercer, have you written passwords down at home?”

Daniel hesitated.

“I keep what I need,” he said.

Nate said, carefully, “There are notes on the desk and on the router. Some are outdated.”

Daniel’s hands tightened on his knees.

“I will not be treated like a child in my own house.”

“You’re not being treated like a child,” the doctor said. “We’re trying to see whether memory support would keep you independent longer. That includes simplifying login steps and making sure someone trustworthy can help when a step fails—not taking over.”

Daniel looked at Nate.

Nate said, “I want you to stay in charge of what you can stay in charge of. I also want you safe when a step fails at nine at night.”

The room held the silence that followed truth when truth was not cruel on purpose.

---

After the visit, paperwork multiplied the way it had all week at work—fluorescent hallway, each form spawning three more forms, each with a signature line nobody wanted to be responsible for.

Follow-up labs. Medication adjustment. A referral to occupational therapy. A form for advanced directive review the doctor said “could wait” in the tone that meant *should not wait too long*.

Daniel signed where indicated with the careful penmanship of a man who had once built spreadsheets for household budgets and now pressed too hard through duplicate copies.

At the checkout desk the receptionist handed Nate three packets and apologized for the duplication.

“Portal will have copies,” she said.

“Portal requires a login he does not use without help,” Nate said.

The receptionist winced in solidarity.

Daniel was in the hallway studying a wall plaque about parking validation as if it were a contract.

Nate photographed each page and uploaded them to the family thread Rachel had insisted on creating after graduation weekend, before Daniel returned from the restroom.

Rachel reacted with a heart before Nate finished typing the summary.

*Thank you for going. Can you send the lab prep instructions? Mom will ask me and I’ll get the version from 2019.*

Nate sent the prep instructions.

Mark reacted with a thumbs-up and no words, which Nate had learned to read as presence without commitment.

---

Nate coordinated.

He booked the lab for Thursday.
He emailed his mother the summary the portal auto-generated, then translated it into sentences she would actually read.
He created a shared calendar invite labeled *Dad — labs* and another labeled *OT intake call* and wondered, with a discomfort he did not want to examine, why creating calendar objects felt more concrete than posting in the engineering channel.

His phone buzzed during the drive home.

Warren: *Can you join a seam sponsorship discussion at four?*

Nate glanced at his father, who was watching rain on the passenger window and humming a song from a decade Nate could not name.

He typed with his thumb at a red light.

*Can we do four thirty? Medical appointment run.*

Warren: *Four thirty works.*

Daniel said, without looking over, “You work too much.”

“Sev One,” Nate said.

“What’s Sev One.”

“A loud problem at work.”

Daniel nodded as if that explained everything loud problems had ever been.

---

At home Hadley was folding laundry when they walked in.

She took one look at Nate’s face and set down a towel.

“How bad,” she said.

“Early,” Nate said. “Manageable early. Not dramatic.”

“Repeating?”

“Some. Forgetting steps. Defensive when corrected.”

Hadley exhaled.

“I’ll call your mom tonight,” she said.

“You don’t have to—”

“I’m not doing it because I have to,” Hadley said. “I’m doing it because she shouldn’t carry the whole translation alone.”

Nate felt something loosen in his chest that work had not loosened all week.

“Thank you,” he said.

Daniel had gone to the guest bathroom and left his jacket on the back of a chair, pockets still bulging with paper—receipts, a pharmacy printout he had already signed once, a note in his own handwriting that said *appointment Wednesday* with no year, a bank letter with a login URL circled in pen three times.

Nate stacked the papers on the counter and photographed them for the shared family thread.

Rachel reacted with a heart emoji and a message: *Thank you for going. Mark says he can take the lab if you’re underwater at work.*

Mark offering help was new enough that Nate stared at it.

Hadley read over his shoulder.

“Let Mark take the lab,” she said.

“If he actually—”

“Then you find out,” Hadley said. “If he doesn’t, you adjust. You’re not the only adult in the family, Nate.”

He wanted to argue that work was underwater, that the seam meeting was Monday, that Warren still messaged at odd hours.

He looked at the papers again—his father’s handwriting, slightly shaky.

At work, questions landed on whoever would turn fear into calendars and summaries.

At home, the same routing was starting to land on him.

---

Hadley called his mother while Nate made dinner—ordinary domestic noise that felt radical after the exam room.

Nate heard only his wife’s side: *yes*, *Wednesday went fine*, *we’ll help with the portal*, *no, he’s not a project*.

When she hung up, she said, “Your mom cried a little. Not because today was catastrophic. Because someone else translated the doctor for once.”

Nate stirred sauce he did not taste.

“Rachel will still text me first,” he said.

“Rachel texts whoever answers,” Hadley said. “You answer.”

He could not argue with the mechanics.

---

That night he sat at the kitchen table with two laptops worth of obligation—incident table on one screen, medical portal on the other—and tried to do both without doing either well.

The portal wanted a password reset because his father’s email forwarding had broken. The incident channel wanted a cohort count before comms. Priya wanted Legal to read policy draft 19. Warren wanted a seam sponsor name that was not a committee.

Caleb called at nine.

“Just checking you’re alive,” Caleb said.

“Alive,” Nate said.

“Warren says four thirty moved to five.”

“Copy.”

A pause.

“Everything okay?” Caleb asked, careful in the way young engineers were careful when they had learned adults also broke.

“Family stuff,” Nate said. “Nothing that stops the bridge.”

“Okay,” Caleb said. “Bridge is loud anyway.”

After the call, Nate finished the lab booking, closed the medical portal, and opened the engineering channel.

Priya had posted an update on policy draft 19.

Eli had posted variant six in a thread title that made Nate close his eyes for one second before he opened it.

Infrastructure had asked for rollback again.

He read them all and felt the same tired loyalty to both worlds—the one that broke in public channels and the one that broke quietly in guest bathrooms and paper pockets.

He typed in the family thread.

*Lab Thursday 8:40. Mark offered to drive—confirming with him tomorrow.*

Rachel: *Thank you. Dad was calmer with you there.*

Mark: *I can do Thursday.*

Nate stared at Mark’s reply longer than he stared at Warren’s seam agenda.

Then he forwarded the Thursday lab to Mark and deleted himself as driver.

A small boundary.

Fragile.

Real.

He closed the laptops and listened to Hadley moving in the bedroom, the ordinary sound of a life continuing while work and family both kept asking him to name things nobody wanted to sign.

On the counter, his father’s sticky note still said *wifi password* with nothing under it.

Nate did not throw it away.

He wrote the current password on a new sticky, labeled it *guest*, and put it inside the desk drawer where Daniel would find it if he needed it and would not have to admit he had forgotten.

Daniel would find the sticky in the drawer if he needed it. No bridge. No template row. Nate washed the mug and listened to the house settle.

---

Thursday morning Mark sent a photo from the lab waiting room—plastic chair, outdated magazine, thumb blocking half the frame.

*On time. Dad complaining about fasting. Normal.*

Nate was on mute in a bridge about instrumentation when the message arrived. He typed *thanks* with his left hand and kept listening with his right ear tuned to Infrastructure explaining why logging was not a substitute for semantics.

At lunch Warren asked whether family was “stable.”

Nate said, “Manageable.”

Warren said, “Good. We need you stable too.”

The sentence was kind and also a request to keep absorbing.

That afternoon Rachel called about the OT intake paperwork—three pages, two signatures, one field that asked for *primary coordinator*.

“Mom put you,” Rachel said. “I told her to put Mom.”

“Change it,” Nate said.

“I did. Portal bounced it. Something about the account email.”

He fixed it after the bridge, at nine-thirty, with Hadley’s dinner cooling on the stove.

Primary coordinator: *Eleanor Mercer*.

Backup: *Rachel Mercer*.

He did not list himself.

The portal accepted the change on the second try.

Rachel sent a string of hearts.

His mother called to say thank you and to ask, gently, whether Nate could still “keep an eye on the portal” because Daniel liked seeing his name on confirmations even when he could not log in alone.

“I can show him screenshots,” Nate said. “I’m not the login.”

Silence on the line.

“Okay,” his mother said. “Okay.”

He hung up and felt the boundary hold, then wobble—the way a row at work held until someone else’s name was in the owner column.

Friday Warren forwarded him a leadership thread that reframed variant five as “limited cohort exposure” with no owner attached.

Nate replied in the operational channel, not the leadership thread: *Table row five. Owner TBD. Do not comms until validated.*

Caleb reacted with a checkmark.

At home Daniel called to ask whether the lab results were back yet.

“They’re not back yet,” Nate said. “Mark took you Thursday. Remember?”

“Mark,” Daniel said, as if testing the name. “Good. Mark hates machines.”

Nate laughed despite himself.

Hadley looked up from her book.

“You sound like a person,” she said.

“Briefly,” Nate said.

It was enough to carry him into the weekend where brunch would ask him, again, to become the center.

Saturday morning he slept until seven—late for him—and found Hadley already up with coffee and the family thread open.

Rachel had posted a photo of Daniel’s shoes by the door, captioned *Dad ready early, miracle*.

Their mother had replied: *Mark picking him up for brunch?*

Rachel: *We’re hosting. Come at eleven.*

Nate made toast and did not open the incident channel until Hadley said, “After brunch.”

He said, “Deal.”

He broke the deal in the car at a red light when Caleb posted a cohort count.

He read it. He did not reply.

Hadley took the phone at the next light and put it in the cup holder.

“Reachable,” she said.

“Required can wait ten minutes,” he said.

She almost smiled.

At brunch he would hear *easier when Nate does it* again.

He would try, this time, to hear *reachable* and *required* as different words.
