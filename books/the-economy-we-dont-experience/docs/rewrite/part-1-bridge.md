# **Part I**

## **The Economy We Describe**

> Intake: essayistic handoff; no chapter syllabus; target ~500–900 words

Once the chart and the receipt have been allowed to stand together, a quieter question appears. If neither instrument is false, what are we doing when we insist that one of them is the economy?

We are reaching for a description that can be shared. A society of millions cannot live entirely inside one grocery aisle or one renewal notice. It cannot live entirely inside a spreadsheet either. Somewhere between the receipt and the map, language has to become portable enough for legislators, employers, investors, journalists, and households to coordinate without inventing a separate world for every bill. That portability is a practical achievement. It is also where public economic speech begins to spend trust.

Part I stays on that described side of economic life—the systems used to summarize what no one can see at once. Gross domestic product, the unemployment rate, inflation indexes, confidence surveys, and forecasts are not decorations around the “real” economy. They are among the ways large societies make a high-dimensional reality discussable. Without compression, public argument would dissolve into incompatible local weather reports, each accurate and none shared. The chart sees patterns no receipt can prove. That is why it exists.

But description is never only measurement. A number leaves the release that produced it. It becomes a headline, then a clip, then a mood. A careful conditional warning becomes a date people prepare for. An average that should orient begins to substitute for the lives it necessarily averages away. Under the ordinary pressure to speak briefly and confidently, the second clause is usually the first thing to disappear. Employment is strong—though the gains are concentrated. Inflation is easing—though the price level remains high. The first clause travels. The second remains in the report.

When that happens, good-faith speech can sound like positioning even when the arithmetic is sound. The map changes social meaning while remaining methodologically intact. Audiences begin asking not only what the number says, but why they are being told it now. Orientation slides toward signaling not because every official is cynical, but because thin channels reward the portable sentence and punish the incomplete truth that made the sentence honest.

The chapters that follow do not ask whether aggregates are useful. They are. They ask what happens when a necessary summary is asked to erase the experience it could not contain. They watch a statistic travel through a day until it becomes a verdict. They watch a forecast enter a town’s decisions before the future arrives. They return, finally, to the kitchen table where credibility pools when official language no longer seems to include the life against which it is being tested.

This is not a tour of methods for their own sake. It is an account of how description becomes the economy people argue about—and how that argument loses legitimacy when description forgets what it left out. The described economy is indispensable for coordination. The experienced economy remains the place where coordination is believed or refused.

The chart was invented so that no one receipt would have to carry the country. The risk is that the country begins to treat the chart as if no receipt were still due.
