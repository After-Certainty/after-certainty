# **Chapter 7**

## **Elections Without Shared Understanding**

Chapter 6 described leadership under hostile channels—where humility is costly, mapping is slow, and restraint needs coalitions. Elections are where those constraints meet **mass selection**: millions of voters choosing among compressions, not among full models.

Economic voting used to be discussed as if voters shared a rough model: inflation hurts incumbents, unemployment hurts incumbents, growth helps.[^c7-economic-voting] Those heuristics still operate. They operate inside **fragmented interpretive frames** where the same print means betrayal to one coalition and progress to another.

Democracy depends not only on shared outcomes, but on shared understanding of what outcomes mean.

### **Economic Voting Without Shared Frames**

Voters can respond to **felt conditions** while disagreeing completely on causes, responsibility, and what policy would fix them. Elections then become competitions between **credibility stories**, not between technical programs voters could evaluate identically.

A voter does not need to reject data to reject a narrative. She needs a story that names her slice before asking her to trust a model built on someone else's.

### **Housing as a Split Screen**

In a swing **suburb**, one voter opens a mortgage statement with a payment that finally stabilized and hears "inflation victory." Another opens a rental renewal with a double-digit increase and hears "betrayal." The same CPI shelter component sits behind both experiences; the campaigns offer different compressions—who saved you, who abandoned you.

Neither voter needs to be insincere. They are living in different tails of the same distribution. Elections under interpretive stress reward the candidate who **names the tail** without calling the other tail fake.

### **Credibility Gaps Outweighing Policy Gaps**

In eras of thin economic speech, **credibility gaps** often outweigh policy gaps: the candidate who "gets it" beats the candidate with detailed plans. Plans are still necessary for governance. They are not always decisive for selection.

Policy professionals experience this as irrationality. Chapter 5 named the rational form: **expressive rationality**—minimize betrayal risk when models feel weaponized.

### **Resonance on the Trail**

Campaigns invest in **resonance** for the reasons Part II named—overlap is cheap, clips reward recognition, pain scales. A candidate who names rent before naming GDP buys time that a candidate who leads with GDP never receives.

Resonance is not always cynicism. It is often the only speech that passes the receipt test at scale. The danger is when resonance never graduates into mechanism—and when governing reverts to aggregate cheer.

### **Small Business on the Stump**

**Main-street owners** appear in ads because they pass the relational credibility test: "I sign the checks." Their stories are partial—one firm, one town—but they answer the public's question after years of aggregate speech that felt like signaling.

Policy detail still matters in office. On the trail, recognition scales faster than white papers.

### **A County Treasurer Race**

A **county treasurer** running for reelection faces a split screen of her own. Property tax receipts are up—evidence of valuation and growth. Senior homeowners on fixed incomes show up at forums furious about tax notices tied to insurance pass-throughs they never voted on.

Her opponent runs a single compression: *"She raised your taxes."* The treasurer tries a two-clock answer: *"Assessed values rose; we can discuss relief mechanisms for fixed-income households."* The clip that travels is the second sentence, truncated to sound evasive.

The race is a laboratory for Part III: **communication skill does not guarantee electoral reward** when channels punish conditionality—and when **intermediate translators** (the bridge's term) are punished for two-clock honesty by their own coalitions.

### **Vibes-Based Voting as Rational**

So-called **vibes-based voting** is often rational signaling: choose the candidate whose alignment reduces perceived betrayal risk, even if policy details are thin.

Vibes are heuristics under bandwidth limits. Dismissing them as stupidity misreads the environment—and cedes the field to messengers who monetize belonging without governing.

### **Elections as Expressive Signaling**

**Expressive signaling** uses votes to say who we are. Economic conditions become symbols in culture-war narratives. This frustrates policy professionals. It is also predictable when economic speech fails to orient.

An election won on anger is not automatically an election won on a plan.

### **Erosion of Stabilizing Legitimacy**

Legitimacy requires more than winning votes. It requires enough **shared meaning** that losers accept outcomes as interpretable. When meaning erodes, losers accept procedures while believing the game is rigged—a brittle form of stability.

### **Procedures Without Shared Meaning**

**Democratic procedures can persist while shared meaning erodes**: courts function, ballots count, transitions occur—alongside collapsing trust in economic narratives that once bound factions.

Institutional strain shows up as permanent campaign mode for economic truth: every release is a battle, every revision a scandal.[^c7-pew-trust]

### **Mandates Without Interpretive Agreement**

Winning coalitions can agree on **ousting** incumbents without agreeing on what success looks like afterward. Mandates become bundles of resentment rather than plans. Governing then requires either betrayal of signals or betrayal of subsets of the coalition.

Economic communication after elections is where many mandates break—because the compression that won the election cannot govern the economy.

### **Media Ecosystems and Incompatible Facts**

Different audiences consume different **fact ecosystems**. Debates feature not only different values but different baselines about what happened to prices, jobs, and crime-adjacent economic stress.

Elections become contests between baselines, not between policies. Restoring shared baselines is slower than winning a cycle.

### **The Hundred-Day Test**

Governing after a credibility election is not the same as winning one. An administration elected because it "gets" housing pain still faces diverging clocks: national prints and local renewals will not move in lockstep.

The first hundred days are where trust is spent or rebuilt. Supporters want confirmation; opponents want contradiction; cross-pressured households want **continuity of recognition**—you still see our rent. An **expressive mandate** knows what it rejected; it may not know what it bought.

Victory laps that revert to aggregate cheer erase the resonance that won. The test is whether the winner still names the slice that won the election—or whether national victory language returns because it travels better in clips. Leaders who pass accept slower persuasion and faster legitimacy repair; leaders who fail often lose the next cycle before policy has time to work.

The Part IV bridge turns to what still holds when communication breaks down—resilience, reform, and guardrails democracies forget until they fail.

[^c7-pew-trust]: Pew Research Center, surveys on political polarization, media consumption, and trust in government (2022–2024).

[^c7-economic-voting]: Classic economic voting literature (e.g., retrospective economic judgments in elections); applied here to fragmented interpretive frames rather than shared macro models.
