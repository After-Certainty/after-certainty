# **Chapter 6**

## **The Score That Follows You**

Most people know their credit score before they know how it is calculated.

The number appears unexpectedly. A banking app displays it beside a checking account. A lender mentions it over the phone. An advertisement promises to help improve it by twelve points, as though twelve points possessed an obvious meaning.

The number arrives already carrying authority. Few people remember the first time they encountered it. Fewer still remember wondering why such a number should exist at all.

Like many structures that become ordinary, it now feels less like an invention than a fact.

There was a time when borrowing money looked very different.

A banker might have known your parents. They might have watched your business grow over decades or recognized your name from church, school board meetings, or the local hardware store. Lending was often personal because information was local. Character traveled through relationships.

This system had obvious limitations. It was slow. It could be inconsistent. Biases—both generous and harmful—were difficult to separate from judgment itself. As lending expanded beyond neighborhoods into regional and national markets, institutions needed a different way to estimate risk.

The question changed. It was no longer, Do I know this person? It became, How likely is this loan to be repaid?

Probability entered the conversation.

Not philosophy.

Not morality.

Probability.

Credit scoring emerged to answer that question. Payment history, debt levels, length of credit, and other observable patterns became signals. No individual variable claimed to reveal character. Together they produced an estimate—a statistical forecast about future repayment based upon the behavior of many people who had come before.[^c6-credit-scoring]

It was, in many respects, a remarkable achievement.

Lenders could make decisions more consistently. Borrowers who lacked personal connections gained access to financial systems that previously depended on local reputation. Scale became possible because judgment no longer required familiarity.

The score solved a real problem. Like most successful solutions, it began to travel.

A landlord requested it before approving an apartment. An insurance company incorporated it into pricing. Employers in some industries looked at it during hiring.

The score, designed to estimate one kind of probability, quietly became evidence in conversations it had never been created to answer.

What began as a forecast started to resemble a description.

This is a subtle shift.

There is an enormous difference between saying someone presents a particular statistical risk and saying that the person is risky. One is a prediction about future events. The other is a statement about identity.

Predictions are necessarily incomplete. Identities are lived.

Most of us understand this instinctively in other parts of life. A weather forecast predicting an eighty percent chance of rain does not become a moral judgment about the sky. A medical screening estimating elevated risk does not define the entirety of a person's health. We recognize these as tools—useful, often remarkably accurate, yet always describing probabilities rather than certainties.

Credit scores ask us to maintain that distinction. Sometimes we do. Sometimes we forget.

There is something deeply attractive about numbers. They appear impartial. They compress extraordinary complexity into something that fits comfortably on a screen. Faced with uncertainty, a single figure feels reassuring. It promises clarity where stories remain complicated.

The number does not eliminate uncertainty. It merely organizes it.

That organization has enormous value. Modern financial systems would struggle without ways to estimate risk across millions of borrowers who will never meet the people making lending decisions. Markets depend upon shared methods for comparing uncertainty.

Measurement makes cooperation possible. But measurement also changes how we see what is being measured.

Students begin thinking about grades. Cities begin thinking about rankings. Hospitals begin thinking about performance metrics. Organizations begin thinking about quarterly targets. And borrowers begin thinking about scores.

The score becomes something to protect, improve, monitor, and occasionally fear. It follows people through adulthood, appearing whenever a major financial decision approaches. A number originally designed to guide institutional judgment gradually becomes part of personal identity.

People begin introducing themselves to the system through it. This pattern extends well beyond finance.

Universities summarize years of study with grade-point averages. Employers reduce performance into annual ratings. Social platforms count followers. Health applications assign readiness scores. The measurements differ, but the temptation remains the same.

We begin mistaking a map for the landscape.[^c6-map-landscape]

None of these measures is inherently deceptive. Many are genuinely useful. They reveal patterns that would otherwise remain hidden. They create consistency where inconsistency once prevailed. They help strangers cooperate without requiring intimate familiarity.

They also invite a particular kind of forgetting. The estimate begins to overshadow the person being estimated.

Perhaps this is inevitable.

Human beings have always searched for ways to make uncertainty manageable. Sometimes we tell stories. Sometimes we rely upon trusted neighbors. Sometimes we build statistical models.

Each approach illuminates something. Each leaves something in shadow.

The interesting question is not whether credit scores should exist. Modern economies would be difficult to imagine without them. The more interesting question is what happens when a tool built to estimate probability begins quietly shaping identity itself.

History suggests that this is how sediment often forms. A solution proves useful. The solution becomes infrastructure. Infrastructure becomes expectation. Expectation becomes common sense.

Eventually, the estimate feels as though it had always been waiting to be discovered rather than having been designed by people trying to solve a particular problem in a particular moment.

The score continues following us. Not because it tells the whole story. But because it tells one part of the story remarkably well.

[^c6-credit-scoring]: See Ian Hacking, *The Taming of Chance* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1990); Theodore M. Porter, *Trust in Numbers: The Pursuit of Objectivity in Science and Public Life* (Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press, 1995).

[^c6-map-landscape]: See Cathy O'Neil, *Weapons of Math Destruction: How Big Data Increases Inequality and Threatens Democracy* (New York: Crown, 2016).

The challenge, as always, is remembering that the story is larger than the number.
