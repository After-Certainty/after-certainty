# **Part I**

## **Fossils We Don't Notice**

There is a peculiar pleasure in discovering that something familiar has been quietly carrying a history all along.

It rarely happens with the grand events we expect to find in history books. It happens with smaller things. A word whose meaning no longer quite matches its origin. A pattern of work that feels inevitable until someone points out it was invented. A tool that still bears the shape of an older technology, long after that technology has disappeared.

Once noticed, these inheritances are difficult to stop seeing.

The chapters that follow begin with such ordinary companions: the names of the days, the way we measure time, the keyboard beneath our hands, the shape of a mortgage, the lingering intuition of gold. None of them asks the reader to question deeply held convictions. None demands agreement with a political position or a philosophy of history.[^b1-curiosity]

They ask only for attention.

These are the gentlest layers beneath our feet. They have settled so comfortably into everyday life that they no longer feel like history at all. They simply feel like the world.

Perhaps that is why they make such good teachers.

To notice that Thursday once belonged to Thor, or that a keyboard still remembers the frustrations of nineteenth-century machinery, is not to uncover a scandal. It is to practice a way of seeing. The surprise lies less in the facts themselves than in the realization that the ordinary is often assembled from forgotten solutions.

Before we consider the structures that shape power, identity, and institutions, it helps to begin somewhere quieter. Curiosity is easier to cultivate where very little seems to be at stake.

History often introduces itself this way—not with a revolution, but with a calendar; not with a manifesto, but with the unnoticed habits of an ordinary Tuesday morning.

[^b1-curiosity]: See George Lakoff and Mark Johnson, *Metaphors We Live By* (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1980); Joseph Henrich, *The Secret of Our Success* (Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press, 2015).
