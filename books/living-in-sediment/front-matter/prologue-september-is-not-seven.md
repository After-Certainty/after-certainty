# **Prologue**

## **September Is Not Seven**

The year begins with a small lie.

Not a malicious one. Not even an intentional one. It is the sort of lie that becomes possible only after centuries of repetition, when everyone has forgotten there was ever anything else to say.

September means seven.

October means eight.

November means nine.

December means ten.

The words still remember something the calendar does not.

Most of us learn the months before we learn to read. We recite them in classrooms and birthday songs. We write them on permission slips and tax forms. They become part of the invisible architecture of ordinary life, so familiar that they rarely invite curiosity. We know September follows August because it always has. We know December ends the year because that is simply where the year ends.

And yet the names quietly disagree.

Children sometimes notice this first. They have not yet learned which questions are considered unnecessary. If octopus comes from the Latin word for eight, why is October the tenth month? Why does December carry the root for ten while arriving two months late?

Adults usually smile and move on. The mismatch feels like one of those harmless irregularities language accumulates, the way English has silent letters or contradictory spellings. Curious, perhaps. Not important.

History suggests otherwise.

There was a time when March stood at the front of the year. Not because spring is inherently a better beginning than winter, but because beginnings tend to follow necessity rather than abstraction. Winter was a season of waiting. Roads became difficult. Fields rested. Armies stayed close to home. When spring returned, so did movement. Crops were planted. Campaigns began. Civic life accelerated with the weather.

The calendar reflected the rhythm of the world people inhabited. That rhythm changed because of a war.

In 153 BCE, Rome faced conflict in Hispania. Newly elected consuls needed to assume command sooner than tradition allowed. Waiting until March was no longer practical. The Senate moved the beginning of the political year to January so military leadership could mobilize earlier.

The adjustment solved a problem.[^pro-calendar-reform]

The names did not follow.

September remained seven.

October remained eight.

November remained nine.

December remained ten.

The war receded into history. The administrative decision remained. Children continued memorizing names that quietly described a calendar no longer in use.

It is difficult to imagine a better example of how history settles. We tend to picture history as something dramatic: revolutions, treaties, inventions, kings. We imagine museums filled with artifacts behind glass, or monuments standing in city squares. But much of history survives in quieter places. It survives in habits. In measurements. In rituals. In words repeated so often they cease to sound like words at all.[^pro-ordinary-history]

History has a preference for ordinary things. It lingers beneath the fingertips that reach automatically for a QWERTY keyboard designed for machines that no longer exist. It lives inside the sixty seconds of every minute, a gift from ancient astronomers who counted differently than we do. It waits inside the thirty-year mortgage, inside the quarterly earnings report, inside the icon shaped like a floppy disk that still promises to save work for people who have never touched one.

The original constraint disappears. The structure remains.

Most of the time this is a gift. Imagine rebuilding the calendar from scratch every generation. Imagine renegotiating the length of an hour, the layout of a keyboard, or the rhythm of the workweek whenever circumstances changed. Civilization depends upon inheritance. Coordination requires continuity. We move forward because we do not have to begin again every morning.

The trouble comes when continuity disguises contingency. Structures that once answered particular questions slowly begin to resemble nature itself. They no longer appear designed. They simply appear true. The reasons fade first. The routines remain. Eventually, it becomes difficult to imagine that the world might ever have been arranged differently.

This book is about those arrangements—not because they are mistakes, not because they should all be replaced, but because seeing them changes something.

To notice that September still means seven is a small act of archaeological attention.[^pro-archaeological-attention] It reminds us that the ground beneath ordinary life is layered. Every institution, every measurement, every custom carries traces of the problems that produced it. Some of those traces stabilize us. Some quietly shape us in ways we no longer recognize. Some deserve preservation. Others deserve reconsideration.

None can be understood if they remain invisible.

We do not live on bare ground. We live on accumulated ground.

The question is not whether there is sediment beneath our feet. The question is whether we remember that it is sediment.

[^pro-ordinary-history]: See Neil MacGregor, *A History of the World in 100 Objects* (New York: Viking, 2011); Fernand Braudel, *The Structures of Everyday Life*, vol. 1 of *Civilization and Capitalism, 15th–18th Century*, trans. Siân Reynolds (New York: Harper & Row, 1981).
