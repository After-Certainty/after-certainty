# **Chapter 3**

## **Releasing the Need for Heroes and Villains**

### **The Teacher Everyone Trusted**

For years, nobody had a bad word to say about him.

Students remembered him decades later. Parents requested him by name. Former students returned to visit long after graduation. When people described him, they rarely described specific actions. Instead they described what it felt like to know he was there: dependable, caring, one of the good ones—the sort of person who restored faith in people.

Then a complaint surfaced. Nothing dramatic at first—a concern, an uncomfortable interaction, a detail that did not fit the story.

Most people did not dismiss it outright. Something more complicated happened. They translated it. The concern became a misunderstanding. The uncomfortable interaction became an exception. The detail became incomplete context. Nobody had to coordinate this response. The story adjusted itself. The teacher remained who everyone already knew him to be.

Human beings do this constantly—not because we are irrational or dishonest, but because trust is expensive to build. A community spends years constructing confidence in someone. The story becomes shared property. A trusted person helps people navigate uncertainty. They become a landmark.

When new information threatens that trust, the community experiences more than an informational problem. It experiences a disruption of orientation.

People often assume trust and scrutiny pull in opposite directions. The more we trust someone, the less scrutiny seems necessary. The more scrutiny appears, the more trust seems endangered. This feels intuitive. It is also one of the most dangerous mistakes communities make.

Because trust does not become fragile when scrutiny appears. Trust becomes fragile when scrutiny disappears. A bridge is not made safer by refusing inspections. A friendship is not strengthened by avoiding difficult conversations. An institution is not protected by becoming unquestionable. The things we value most require the most scrutiny, not the least.

Yet admiration often moves in the opposite direction. The more a person symbolizes hope, competence, sacrifice, or goodness, the more criticism begins sounding like betrayal. People become protective. Questions feel disloyal. Context expands around mistakes. Exceptions multiply. The story bends before it breaks.

What begins as gratitude gradually becomes insulation.

### **What Heroes Quietly Protect**

We need heroes for reasons that are not childish. A hero concentrates hope. They make sacrifice legible. They tell a frightened room that good intention still exists somewhere in the mess. When harm is visible and causes remain mixed, a group still has to decide how to act, whom to follow, and how much trust to extend. A hero reduces those coordination costs. They absorb ambiguity long enough for people to move together.

Heroes make trust portable. You do not have to re-derive seriousness from first principles every morning. You can lean on the person already cast as competent, courageous, or committed. That is not nothing. It is one of the ways frightened people endure difficulty together.

None of this requires the hero to be false. It requires only that simplification work—and that groups under pressure often cannot function without it.

The problem is not admiration. The problem begins when admiration becomes protection from proportionate inquiry—when criticism of the person starts sounding like criticism of the cause, when scrutiny starts sounding like betrayal, when trust becomes something that must be defended instead of examined.

Admiration also offers coherence. Once a person becomes heroic, the story simplifies. Difficult tradeoffs can be narrated as courage. Harm nearby can be reframed as the cost of necessary work. Loyalty has a face. Criticism, by contrast, threatens to reopen a story others have already used to decide where they stand.

Public life rewards this coherence. Institutions elevate people who embody resolve. Groups defend their champions because champions make the group's moral seriousness visible. To question a hero can sound like questioning the cause itself—not only the person.

Something shifts when admiration starts asking more than proportion can give. Criticism begins sounding like ingratitude. Dissent starts feeling like sabotage. The person who raises an inconvenient fact risks being read not as careful, but as disloyal to the cause the hero represents. Information surrounding heroes begins changing shape. Harm becomes reframed as error, tradeoff, or necessity. Context expands around them. Motives receive generous interpretation.

This is rarely conscious corruption. It is usually an attempt to preserve coherence. Once a heroic narrative stabilizes, revising it becomes emotionally expensive. Information that threatens the story begins to feel destabilizing—not necessarily because it is false, but because accepting it would require moral re-sorting.

What begins as care can slowly become protection of the story itself.

**Admiration Becomes Insulation.**[^c3-motivated-reasoning]

The teacher's community had not needed a conspiracy to protect him. Gratitude had already done the work.

### **What Villains Make Possible**

The same dynamic appears from the opposite direction with villains.

Villains simplify the moral landscape. They make harm easier to understand. They gather confusion into a single figure. If heroes make trust portable, villains make blame portable. Both provide orientation. Both reduce complexity. Both tell us where our moral attention should land. And both can become distortions.

Condemnation often feels like clarity. It can seem like taking harm seriously—if wrongdoing has a face, then outrage is not excess. It is recognition. But villains do more than locate wrongdoing. They offer belonging.

To share outrage is to join a side. To name a villain is to make harm portable—something that can be condemned quickly, circulated widely, and felt together before the full complexity has been weighed. Condemnation also offers relief. If harm has a face, then the rest of us can feel clean by comparison. We are not the villain. We are the people who noticed. Outrage becomes a way of remaining morally visible without remaining morally entangled.

You can see the same relief in smaller rooms. A family conflict that finally names one person as the problem—and everyone else feels, for a moment, that the confusion has been understood. The naming had not resolved the harm. It had given it a single address—and let everyone else step back from what they were still participating in.

The hero absorbs too much grace. The villain absorbs too much blame. The surrounding conditions disappear. The systems disappear. The incentives disappear. Ordinary participation disappears. Reality becomes easier to narrate and harder to understand.

If harm is caused primarily by bad people, then ordinary people do not have to examine their own participation. If wrongdoing is exceptional, then the surrounding systems remain largely unquestioned. Villains create moral distance. They help us avoid asking how similar dynamics might operate closer to home—or through us.

**Blame Compresses Complexity.**[^c3-villain-narratives]

What makes heroes and villains attractive is not merely simplicity. It is relief. A hero tells us where trust belongs. A villain tells us where blame belongs. Both spare us the discomfort of remaining uncertain. Both spare us the burden of continued scrutiny.

But scrutiny is exactly what trust requires—not because people are bad, but because people are human.

### **Releasing Archetypes Is Not Moral Relativism**

The trusted person will eventually disappoint. The admired leader will eventually make a poor decision. The respected expert will eventually overlook something. The beloved teacher will eventually reveal a flaw. The question is not whether this will happen. The question is whether trust can survive the discovery.

Communities organized around heroes often cannot. The story collapses. Admiration becomes cynicism. Certainty flips directions. Yesterday's hero becomes today's villain. The same sorting continues. Only the labels change.

What does it feel like to let go of heroes and villains? Usually not like moral sophistication at first. It feels like losing footing. The first time you stop defending someone you admire, it can feel like betrayal—not because the criticism is unfair, but because the story everyone was using no longer holds. The first time you refuse to condemn someone everyone else has already sorted, it can feel like disloyalty—to the harm, to the group, to your own need for the moral world to stay legible.

If no one is simply the villain, then outrage has nowhere clean to land. If no one is simply the hero, then loyalty has no obvious object. The story stops offering the emotional relief of total condemnation or total innocence. Harm remains visible, but less shareable. Responsibility spreads outward—toward systems, incentives, participation, proximity—and with it the fear that everything may become excusable.

That fear is understandable. Releasing archetypes does not eliminate judgment. It makes judgment harder. It requires holding mixed motives, partial responsibility, structural incentives, and uneven consequences in view at the same time. It refuses the shortcuts sorting provides.

Mature trust works differently. It does not require innocence. It does not require perfection. It does not require exemption. It remains willing to ask questions, to investigate, to revise—not because trust is weak, but because trust is strong enough to survive examination.

**Scrutiny Preserves Trust.**

The opposite is rarely true. Insulation preserves stories. Scrutiny preserves trust. The difference matters. Stories are about certainty. Trust is about reality. One eventually breaks. The other adapts.

### **What Replaces Heroes and Villains**

Nothing dramatic replaces them. No better mythology arrives.

Releasing heroes and villains is not abandoning judgment. It is abandoning the belief that judgment must arrive already sorted—choosing scrutiny over worship, inquiry over allegiance, proportion over narrative.

That is slower work. It offers fewer moral rewards in the short term: less belonging, less clarity of allegiance, less of the feeling that moral life has finally found its shape. But it preserves something sorting often sacrifices—the ability to respond proportionally instead of narratively.

Without heroes and villains, several things quietly become possible again: admiration without exemption, condemnation without erasure, responsibility without omniscience. You can recognize good without surrendering scrutiny. You can name harm without pretending it explains everything. You can judge without pretending judgment finishes the moral account.

Trust does not survive through insulation. It survives when admiration remains open to proportionate inquiry—when legitimacy can be questioned without the story collapsing into cynicism.

The teacher's story had not needed a villain to feel complete. It had needed a community willing to let trust remain answerable to what it could not yet explain.

The goal is not to trust less. The goal is to trust well.

This is less emotionally satisfying than narrative certainty. But it is more durable.
