# Chapter 1 - Why Leadership Evaluation Fails

Leadership is most often judged by outcomes. When things improve, the
leader is praised. When they deteriorate, the leader is blamed. The
logic feels intuitive, even responsible. But it quietly collapses two
different questions into one: Did this leader produce results, and were
those results achieved in a morally permissible way? When those
questions are treated as equivalent, success becomes virtue and failure
is mistaken for moral innocence. Harm is excused by effectiveness;
restraint is dismissed as weakness—even when the systems being praised
are efficient precisely because they suppress dissent, concentrate
power, or externalize suffering.

This collapse is not merely cultural; it is cognitive. Research in moral
psychology has demonstrated that people consistently judge decisions
based on outcomes rather than decision quality—treating a lucky result
as evidence of wisdom, and an unlucky result as evidence of
recklessness. Once results are known, the uncertainty present at the
moment of choice disappears from judgment. The path that succeeded feels
obvious; the path that failed feels reckless. Leadership judgment
inherits this bias wholesale.

A project manager who delivers ahead of schedule is celebrated. What is
less visible are the late nights, the burnout, the quiet attrition that
follows months later. A principal who raises test scores is praised,
even if the gains required narrowing which students were allowed to
succeed. Results are legible; harm distribution is not. Outcome-based
evaluation rewards what can be counted and ignores what was prevented.

Crisis intensifies the distortion. In emergencies, decisiveness often
becomes a moral proxy. Leaders who act quickly, consolidate authority,
and impose compliance are described as strong. Leaders who pause to
consult, preserve dissent, or share risk are described as hesitant. Once
outcomes are known, hindsight completes the error: success feels
inevitable and failure feels culpable.

This distortion is easiest to see in the following case.

## The Problem with Outcomes

Consider two leaders faced with the same looming threat.

Both are presented with incomplete information. Both know action will
cause harm. Both know inaction may cause harm of a different kind.
Neither can know the full shape of the future they are about to create.

The first leader acts early. They impose restrictions that disrupt daily
life. Businesses close. Routines fracture. The costs are immediate and
visible. People are angry. The harm is undeniable, even if its purpose
is contested. Later, fewer deaths occur than might have been
expected—but there is no control group. The harm that did not occur
leaves no evidence behind.

The second leader waits. They emphasize calm. They avoid disruption.
Life continues as normal for a time. The immediate costs are low, and
approval remains high. When the harm finally arrives, it arrives
diffusely and unevenly. Responsibility is distributed across time,
institutions, and individuals. By then, the narrative has shifted: no
one could have known; the information was unclear; the situation was
unprecedented.[<u>³</u>](#notes)

In hindsight, one leader is often judged harshly for the harm they
visibly caused. The other is forgiven—or even praised—for the harm that
appeared later, diluted by distance and uncertainty.

This is outcome bias at work.¹

We reward leaders not for the moral posture they adopted toward harm,
but for how cleanly the harm they allowed can be obscured by time,
complexity, or plausible deniability. Visible harm is treated as
evidence of failure. Invisible harm is treated as inevitability.⁴

Leadership, however, does not occur in hindsight. Moral judgment that
relies on outcomes alone quietly erases the conditions under which
decisions were made. It confuses luck with virtue and substitutes
narrative coherence for ethical evaluation.²

Another failure follows close behind: judging leaders by character
rather than conduct. Leaders are labeled good or bad, principled or
corrupt, as though morality were a stable personality trait rather than
a pattern revealed under pressure. Social psychology has long shown that
we over-attribute behavior to disposition and under-attribute it to
context, especially when power is involved. Hero narratives and villain
narratives spare us the harder work of examining incentives,
constraints, and authority.⁵

Time compounds the confusion. As consequences unfold long after a
leader’s involvement, legitimacy travels forward through institutions
while accountability remains fixed at the origin. Founders are
sanctified to excuse institutions they would not recognize, or condemned
for harms they never authorized. Authority inherits; responsibility does
not.⁶

One signal that outcome-based judgment often overlooks is whether
authority was visible enough to be predictable without being threatening
enough to require enforcement.

Authority that must be concealed to appear kind, or asserted
aggressively to remain intact, is already unstable. In both cases, power
exceeds the trust supporting it. What looks like strength or humility on
the surface often masks the same failure underneath: authority that
provokes fear when asserted and resistance when left implicit.

Here, authority does not mean the ability to compel obedience. It refers
to legitimacy that is accepted without force—and becomes unstable
precisely when it must rely on threat or concealment to function.

This failure of evaluation is not confined to politics or religion. It
appears in organizations, movements, and families—any where authority
outlives the conditions that once constrained it. Wherever power
persists without proximity to consequence, moral clarity erodes.

A more honest evaluation of leadership begins by separating what we have
learned to conflate. This lens draws attention to three questions:

- How did this leader relate to harm when they had the power to choose
  otherwise?

- How effective was their influence in shaping behavior or systems?

- How was their authority allowed to propagate after their control
  ended?

These questions interact, but they are not interchangeable.

> **“Leadership is often judged by its outcomes because outcomes are
> easy to see, not because they are sufficient for moral judgment.”**
