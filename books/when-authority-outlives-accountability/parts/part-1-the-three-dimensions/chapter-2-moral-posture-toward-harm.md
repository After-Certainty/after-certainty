# Chapter 2 - Moral Posture Toward Harm

When people speak about morality in leadership, they usually speak about
character. Leaders are described as good or bad, compassionate or cruel.
This language feels natural, but it misleads. Research on moral injury
and situational ethics shows that harm is rarely produced by monstrous
intent alone. It is produced by ordinary people operating within systems
that authorize, excuse, or conceal damage.[^c2-shay-achilles-in-vietnam]<sup>,</sup>[^c2-milgram-obedience-to-authority]<sup>,</sup>[^c2-zimbardo-the-lucifer-effect]

Leadership morality is often better understood not as who a leader is,
but as how a leader handles harm when they have the power to choose
otherwise. This focus does not reduce morality to harm alone; it treats
harm as the point at which leadership ethics become unavoidable,
observable, and testable.

Harm is not an anomaly in leadership; it is its constant companion.
Every decision allocates cost, even when that cost is delayed or hidden.
Someone absorbs risk so that others do not. Some losses are visible and
immediate; others are delayed, abstracted, or hidden entirely. The moral
question is not whether harm exists, but where it is placed, how it is
justified, and whether it is treated as unavoidable, regrettable, or
useful.[^c2-weber-politics-as-a]

This distinction becomes clearer in the following cases.

## Managing Harm

A large organization faces a problem it did not create but now must
address.

The harm is real. People are being hurt—not uniformly, not all at once,
but predictably enough that leadership cannot claim surprise. Internal
reports describe the pattern. External complaints confirm it. Legal
counsel is consulted. The risk is acknowledged.

Leadership responds.

Policies are revised. New procedures are introduced. Reporting channels
are formalized. Training sessions are scheduled. Statements are issued
emphasizing concern, seriousness, and commitment to improvement. The
organization does not deny the harm. It does not minimize it outright.
It recognizes the problem and takes steps to address it.

And yet, the harm continues.

Not because leadership is unaware of it, but because the response has
been carefully shaped to manage exposure rather than prevent recurrence.
The measures are sufficient to demonstrate responsibility, but
insufficient to disrupt the structures that make the harm possible. The
response is sufficient to contain exposure, but insufficient to
eliminate the conditions that allow harm to recur.

Those closest to the harm notice the difference immediately. Complaints
are processed, but rarely acted upon. Escalation is permitted, but
discouraged. Responsibility is diffused across departments, committees,
and timeframes. Each individual decision appears reasonable. No single
actor can be said to have chosen harm.

From the outside, leadership appears conscientious. From the inside, the
posture is clear: harm is acceptable so long as it remains
administratively manageable.

This posture is often mistaken for moral seriousness. After all, the
organization acknowledges harm. It invests resources. It follows
procedures. Acknowledgment does not equal restraint, and compliance does
not equal responsibility.[^c2-shay-achilles-in-vietnam]

The decisive question is not whether leaders recognized harm, but how
they understood their relationship to it. Was harm treated as something
to be prevented, even at cost to authority, reputation, or efficiency?
Or was it treated as an operational risk to be controlled?

In this case, leadership did not intend harm. But intention is not the
relevant measure. What matters is that harm became a known byproduct of
maintaining stability. It was absorbed into the system as an acceptable
loss.[^c2-arendt-eichmann-in-jerusalem]

This is not cruelty. It is something more ordinary—and more dangerous.

It is harm that has been normalized by authority.

## Absorbing Harm

A leader inherits a system under strain.

Budgets are tight. Expectations are high. The organization is asked to
deliver more with less, and the pressure to pass costs downward is real.
There are options available—none of them clean.

One option would preserve performance by externalizing harm. Targets
could be raised. Staffing could be reduced. Risk could be transferred to
those with the least leverage to refuse it. The losses would be
distributed thinly enough to appear tolerable, even if they were not.[^c2-weber-politics-as-a]

Another option would limit harm by accepting it.

The leader chooses the latter.

They slow the pace of work, knowing it will disappoint stakeholders.
They reduce scope rather than stretch capacity. They refuse incentives
that would require pushing costs onto those already near exhaustion.
When cuts are unavoidable, they cut upward first—removing privileges,
absorbing political damage, accepting personal loss of standing.

The consequences are immediate.

Progress slows. Metrics soften. The leader is described as ineffective,
overly cautious, insufficiently ambitious. Some leave anyway. Others
question whether the organization is still competitive. There are no
dramatic victories to point to, no obvious proof that the sacrifice was
necessary.

But something else happens quietly.

Burnout stabilizes. Trust consolidates. People begin to believe that
harm will not be casually offloaded onto them. When future strain
arrives—as it inevitably does—those closest to the work stay engaged
rather than withdrawing. The system bends without breaking.

From the outside, this leadership looks unremarkable. From a distance,
it may even appear weak.

But the moral posture is unmistakable. Harm was not denied. It was not
instrumentalized. It was not displaced. It was taken on deliberately,
where power was concentrated and choice still existed.[^c2-weber-politics-as-a]

This posture does not eliminate harm. It redistributes it.

And in doing so, it preserves something that cannot be measured easily
but is costly to lose: the credibility of care.

This lens makes four recurring postures toward harm visible. These are
not rankings or identities. Leaders may move between them under
pressure. Naming these postures is not a way of judging leaders, but a
way of making tradeoffs visible.

**Harm-absorbing** leadership describes a posture that internalizes
cost. A fire chief who enters danger with their crew redistributes risk.
A manager who accepts personal loss to protect a team from layoffs
absorbs harm inward. This posture often appears weak, but it builds
trust that cannot be coerced.[^c2-edmondson-the-fearless-organization]

**Harm-tolerant** leadership describes a posture that acknowledges harm
while displacing it. A hospital administrator who knowingly understaffs
units may sincerely regret burnout while continuing the practice. Regret
does not negate displacement. This posture is common and often
well-intentioned, but it normalizes harm as governance.[^c2-shay-achilles-in-vietnam]<sup>,</sup>[^c2-arendt-eichmann-in-jerusalem]

**Harm-instrumental** leadership describes a posture that uses suffering
as a tool. Fear, punishment, or sacrifice become mechanisms rather than
side effects. Once harm is employed rather than endured, moral limits
collapse.[^c2-milgram-obedience-to-authority]<sup>,</sup>[^c2-zimbardo-the-lucifer-effect]

**Harm-blind** leadership describes a posture that obscures damage
through distance. A policymaker who optimizes metrics while remaining
insulated from downstream consequences may sincerely believe themselves
moral. Scale makes blindness easier.[^c2-arendt-eichmann-in-jerusalem]

Harm-absorbing leadership is morally serious only when it preserves
agency rather than replacing it, and when it prevents harm without
becoming a substitute for responsibility.

When harm absorption prevents others from confronting consequences they
must bear, or allows harm to become normalized, inherited, or automatic,
it ceases to be restraint and becomes distortion.

Absorbing harm is not morally serious by itself. It becomes serious only
when it prevents harm without removing another person’s responsibility
to act.

Scale matters. Small systems make harm visible; large systems make it
abstract. Crisis pushes leaders toward instrumentalization; stability
allows restraint. Moral posture is shaped by structure as much as
intent.[^c2-weber-politics-as-a]

> **“Leadership morality is often less about being good or bad than
> about how power handles harm.”**
