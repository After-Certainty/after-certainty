# Chapter 5 - The Integrated Model

Up to this point, this lens has separated leadership into three
dimensions: moral posture toward harm, effectiveness, and legitimacy
over time. Each dimension clarifies something that outcome-based
judgment obscures. But the lens only comes into focus when these
dimensions are held together. Leadership does not occur along a single
axis. It unfolds at the intersection of permission, impact, and
inheritance over time.

**Morality** determines what a leader is ethically permitted to do when
harm is unavoidable. **Effectiveness** determines how widely and durably
those actions shape the world. **Legitimacy** determines whether the
authority behind those actions persists beyond the leader’s direct
control. None of these dimensions can substitute for the others.
Together, they help clarify why leadership that looks similar on the
surface can diverge so dramatically in its consequences.¹

To see how these dimensions interact over time, consider the following
case.

## The Long Arc

A leader inherits a system already under strain.

Expectations are high, trust is fragile, and resources are finite. The
leader is not reckless. They are thoughtful, competent, and widely
regarded as decent. Early decisions reflect caution. Harm is
acknowledged. Tradeoffs are explained. People feel heard, even when
outcomes disappoint.

The leader emphasizes stability. They consolidate authority just enough
to act, but not so much as to silence dissent. Early results are mixed,
but acceptable. The system holds.

Then conditions change.

External pressure intensifies. Oversight bodies demand clarity.
Stakeholders want reassurance. Time horizons compress. What once felt
like prudence now feels like delay. The leader adapts.

Effectiveness becomes the organizing principle.

Metrics are clarified. Incentives are aligned. Processes are
standardized. Ambiguity is reduced. Results improve. The leader is
praised for focus and decisiveness. Harm has not disappeared, but it is
now legible, manageable, and increasingly abstract.²

Some costs are displaced. Not maliciously, but systematically. The
people who bear them are distant enough to be represented statistically
rather than personally. Regret is expressed. Mitigations are announced.
The system moves forward.

From the outside, this looks like responsible leadership.

From the inside, moral posture begins to shift. Harm is no longer
something to be absorbed or resisted; it becomes something administered
carefully through systems. The distinction is subtle, but decisive.

Over time, the leader steps away.

The system remains.

Procedures persist. Metrics endure. The leader’s language is codified
into training materials and performance frameworks. Successors inherit
not only authority, but justification. Decisions are defended by
precedent rather than judgment.

When harm appears, it is explained as necessity. When objections arise,
they are met with references to what “worked.” Accountability points
backward to precedent; power points forward through authority.

Eventually, critics trace present damage to choices made long ago.
Defenders respond by pointing to early restraint and good intent. Both
are correct—and both are incomplete.

The leader did not intend harm.

The leader was effective.

The leader acted within norms that rewarded visible success and deferred
cost.

And yet, the system now produces harm without requiring anyone to choose
it.

This is how moral failure often enters leadership—not through cruelty or
corruption, but through accumulation. Each step is defensible. Each
transition feels reasonable. Responsibility thins as authority spreads
across people, roles, and time.³

By the time harm becomes undeniable, it no longer belongs to any one
person.

This case illustrates how the three dimensions interact when left
unconstrained. Moral seriousness gives way under pressure. Effectiveness
amplifies displaced harm. Legitimacy travels forward without renewing
accountability. None of these failures appear dramatic in isolation.
Together, they produce durable damage.

But this arc is not inevitable in every case.

## The Bounded Arc

A leader inherits a system already under strain.

Expectations are high, trust is fragile, and resources are finite. The
leader is competent and experienced, but cautious about what competence
can justify. Early decisions are slow. Tradeoffs are explained
repeatedly. Some stakeholders grow impatient. Others leave.

The leader accepts the consequences of this choice.

Rather than consolidating authority to accelerate change, they
distribute it. Rather than clarifying metrics immediately, they resist
premature simplification. They ask which harms are reversible and which
are not. Where harm cannot be avoided, they choose to absorb it
upward—through delayed recognition, constrained scope, and personal
political cost.⁴

Progress is uneven.

Some indicators improve. Others stagnate. The leader is described as
principled, but not transformative. There are no clean turnarounds to
celebrate.

Still, something stabilizes.

The system learns that harm will not be casually displaced. Decisions
remain contestable. Exceptions are permitted. Judgment is valued
alongside consistency. When people disagree, disagreement is recorded
rather than erased.

Effectiveness arrives slowly.

Not through enforcement, but through uptake. Practices spread because
they make sense, not because they are mandated. Norms form around
fairness, clarity, and shared responsibility. The system becomes harder
to summarize—and harder to break.¹

When external pressure intensifies, the leader narrows ambition rather
than widening authority. They reduce scope before increasing coercion.
Some opportunities are declined. Some expansions are refused. The system
remains smaller than it could have been.

Eventually, the leader steps away.

The system remains.

But what persists is not merely procedure. It is restraint.

Successors inherit authority that is deliberately incomplete. Processes
include expiration dates. Roles contain mechanisms for dissent and
revision. Precedent is treated as guidance, not permission.

When harm appears, it is debated rather than justified. When appeals are
made to the past, they are interpreted rather than enforced. The
leader’s example is cited—but not as a shield.

The system still causes harm. No institution avoids this.

But harm requires choice.

It cannot proceed on autopilot.

This is not moral perfection. It is moral friction.

Authority continues, but it does not escape responsibility. Legitimacy
travels forward carrying limits, not exemptions.

What separates these two arcs is not who the leader was, but how their
authority was allowed to travel through time.

A leader who absorbs harm but lacks effectiveness may protect a small
circle while failing to change broader conditions. Their leadership is
morally serious but limited in reach. A leader who is effective but
harm-tolerant may produce stability or growth while quietly
externalizing cost onto others. Their leadership works—until the damage
accumulates. A leader who is both effective and harm-instrumental may
achieve rapid transformation at the cost of fear, suffering, and
long-term brittleness. Their influence is unmistakable, but so is the
risk it carries.²<sup>,</sup>³

Legitimacy complicates the picture further. A morally serious leader may
act with restraint, yet leave behind an institution that treats their
example as permission rather than constraint. Conversely, a leader whose
actions were morally compromised may preside over systems that later
reform and limit harm. The moral character of a leader and the moral
trajectory of their legacy are related, but not identical.

This helps explain why leadership evaluation so often feels
unsatisfying. We want a single verdict—good or bad, success or
failure—but leadership does not resolve cleanly into categories. A
leader may score well on one dimension and poorly on another. Moral
seriousness may trade off against immediate effectiveness. Effectiveness
may amplify harm. Legitimacy may outlive accountability. The lens does
not eliminate these tensions; it makes them visible.

The integrated model also explains why it is difficult—though not
impossible—to be both morally serious and effective. Absorbing harm
slows action. Limiting power reduces leverage. Preserving dissent
introduces friction. These choices often make leadership appear weak or
indecisive in the short term. Effectiveness, by contrast, is easiest to
achieve by displacing cost, narrowing options, or enforcing compliance.
The lens does not romanticize this difficulty; it names it.³

At the same time, the model guards against cynicism. The model rejects
the idea that effectiveness requires cruelty or that moral seriousness
must be futile. It allows for leadership that is both restrained and
influential, especially when effectiveness is measured over longer time
horizons and legitimacy is deliberately bounded. Such leadership is rare
not because it is incoherent, but because it resists the incentives that
reward speed, certainty, and control.

Importantly, the model also reframes failure. A leader who refuses to
instrumentalize harm and is removed from power may still succeed
morally, even if their influence is limited. A leader who achieves
sweeping change but leaves behind a culture of fear may succeed
instrumentally while failing ethically. The model allows these truths to
coexist without collapsing one into the other.

What becomes visible when these dimensions are held together is not a
scoring system, but a diagnostic lens. It helps us ask better questions:

*Where was harm placed?*

*\*
*How far did its effects travel?*

*\*
*Who was allowed to wield the resulting authority—and for how long?*

These questions shift leadership evaluation away from personality and
toward structure, away from outcomes alone and toward responsibility
across time.

> **“Morality governs what may be done.\
> Effectiveness governs how far it reaches.\
> Legitimacy governs whether it becomes dangerous after it is done.”**
