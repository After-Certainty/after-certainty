# Chapter 8 - Using the Lens

## A Guide for Evaluation, Not Instruction

Some readers may come to this lens hoping it will persuade
others—leaders, institutions, or systems—to act differently. That is not
its primary work. More often, this lens resonates with people who have
already chosen restraint, who have limited their own power, or who have
stepped back rather than participate in harm they could not justify. For
such readers, the lens does not instruct. It clarifies. It provides
language for choices that are often misread as weakness, indecision, or
failure—and helps distinguish moral seriousness from ineffectiveness
when those choices are judged by outcome alone.

This lens is not a method for producing better leaders. It is a way of
seeing leadership more clearly—especially when judgment is distorted by
outcomes, loyalty, fear, or admiration. Its purpose is not to tell you
what to do, but to help you recognize what is happening.

What follows is not a checklist or a decision tree. It is a set of
evaluative moves—ways of slowing judgment, separating dimensions that
are often collapsed, and resisting narratives that excuse harm or
dismiss restraint. The lens is most useful when something feels
confusing, unfair, or morally unresolved.

## Separate What Is Often Collapsed

When evaluating leadership—your own or others’—it can help to resist the
instinct to issue a single verdict. Instead, hold three distinct
questions at the same time, even if they pull in different directions:

**Moral posture toward harm**

- Where did harm land, and how was it treated?

- Was harm absorbed, displaced, instrumentalized, or obscured?

**Effectiveness**

- What actually changed as a result of this leadership?

- How wide was the influence, and how long did it last?

**Legitimacy over time**

- What authority was created, transferred, or normalized?

- Who was empowered to act afterward, and with what constraints?

Do not rush to reconcile the answers. The lens works by allowing tension
to remain visible rather than resolving it prematurely.

## Resist Outcome Gravity

Outcomes exert a powerful pull on judgment. When results are positive,
harm fades from view. When results are negative, restraint is
reinterpreted as failure. The lens counters this gravity by asking you
to reconstruct the moral landscape *before* outcomes were known.

Useful questions include:

- What uncertainties existed at the moment of decision?

- What alternatives were available, and who would have borne their
  costs?

- Which risks were accepted by the leader, and which were transferred to
  others?

This is not an exercise in excusing mistakes. It is an exercise in
distinguishing moral seriousness from luck, and recklessness from
misfortune.

## Trace Harm, Not Intent

The lens deliberately deprioritizes intent. Leaders may act with sincere
concern and still authorize harm. Others may act cynically and yet limit
damage through structure or constraint. Moral evaluation begins not with
motives, but with trajectories.

Ask:

- Who absorbed the cost of this decision in practice?

- Did harm remain localized, or did it spread across people, time, or
  systems?

- Was harm treated as regrettable, inevitable, or useful?

Tracing harm often reveals moral posture more clearly than statements of
principle ever could.

## Watch What Becomes Permission

One of the most important uses of this lens is recognizing when
leadership stops acting and starts authorizing.

Pay attention to moments when:

- “What this leader did” becomes “what this role allows”

- Example hardens into rule

- Moral authority converts into operational power

The question is not whether influence persists—that is unavoidable—but
whether it remains contestable. Leadership becomes dangerous when
legitimacy travels farther than responsibility, and when successors
inherit permission without inheriting restraint.

## Seeing Leadership at Human Scale First

Before applying the lens to institutions or historical figures, apply it
where consequences are immediate: families, teams, caregiving
relationships, and your own roles.

At human scale:

- Harm is harder to deny

- Effectiveness is harder to exaggerate

- Legitimacy must be renewed rather than assumed

If the lens clarifies leadership here, it will scale outward. If it
fails here, abstraction will not save it.

## Accept That Clarity May Not Produce Comfort

Seeing leadership clearly does not guarantee satisfaction. The lens
explains why morally serious leadership is often unrewarded,
misrecognized, or displaced—and why harmful leadership so often appears
effective, decisive, or inevitable.

One use of the lens is learning to tolerate this discomfort without
resolving it through cynicism or hero worship. Clear judgment may not
change outcomes, but it changes what you consent to, what you excuse,
and what you allow to be inherited.

## What This Lens Is Not

This lens:

- does not tell you whom to follow

- does not promise that moral restraint will prevail

- does not convert goodness into success

What it offers instead is restraint in judgment itself—a refusal to let
outcomes excuse harm, and a refusal to let failure erase moral
seriousness.

Used well, the lens does not make leadership easier. It makes it harder
to lie to ourselves about what leadership is doing, who it is
protecting, and what it is leaving behind.

> **“If this lens changes what you admire, what you tolerate, or what
> you allow to persist unexamined, then it has already altered the moral
> terrain—whether or not it changes who holds power.”**
