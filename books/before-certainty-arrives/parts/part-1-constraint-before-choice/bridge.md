# **Part I**

## **Constraint Before Choice**

Before morality could be debated at length, it had to keep people alive.

The first three chapters stay close to that problem—not as philosophy,
but as pressure. Small bands under scarcity deciding whether everyone
who expected to be alive at dawn would still choose to stay. Bronze Age
cities where harvest counts were pressed into clay before anyone asked
whether the arrangement was just. Empires where bronze vessels and
temple grain held power and piety inside the same ceremony.

Each case is different. The question rhymes: what will still hold when
explanation is too slow, disagreement too costly, and the season will
not wait?

Part I follows that movement from constraint to order to legitimacy.
Moral systems emerge here less as choices than as adaptations—roles
before reasons, law before argument, sacred authority when personal
trust could no longer stretch across distance.

What held groups together gradually begins to feel like the shape of the
good itself.

Iron would soon test whether that shape could travel farther than the
hand that enforced it.
