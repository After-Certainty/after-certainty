# Romance

Two people had been married long enough that their arguments had developed weather patterns.

Not dramatic weather. The ordinary kind—tone before topic, the pause that meant someone was tired rather than cruel, the way a dishwasher loaded wrong could stand in for a harder conversation neither of them wanted after nine in the evening.

One night she said, "You're not listening."

He said, "I am listening. I just don't know what you want me to do with what I'm hearing."

That might have been the whole fight, if he had stopped there.

Instead he stayed in the kitchen.

He put the dish towel down.

He said, "Tell me again. Slower."

That was romance, or something close enough that most of us recognize it when it appears—not only desire, not only ceremony, but the ongoing choice to remain reachable inside a bond that has stopped being new.

## What the beginning teaches

There is something wonderful about the beginning. You notice everything. A favorite song feels like discovery. A story from childhood feels like being trusted with treasure. You replay conversations on the drive home because every sentence seems to reveal another piece of who this person might become in your life.

We should not laugh at that version of ourselves. That curiosity was real.

Early romance already holds the attention long love still needs—curiosity, pursuit, play, surprise, the sense that someone is still unfolding. What often fades in a bond that lasts is not those things. It is the fear beneath them: whether you are wanted, whether the bond will hold, whether a wrong move might end everything. What matures is often the curiosity staying put while the fear softens into something closer to safety.

On the third date, you were paying attention because every answer opened another question. After thirty years, the same movement is still available—not because you might lose them, but because you have not finished meeting them.

## Building a life while still changing

In many marriages, two people are trying to do something that is harder than it looks on a wedding program: build a shared future while both continue becoming—without pretending they have become one person.

Careers shift. Bodies age. Tastes change. A person who hated travel at thirty may long for it at fifty. A person who once wanted children may discover a different dream. A practical partner may surprise you with a poem; a quiet one may become bold in a season you did not see coming. The future two people imagine together keeps changing because they keep changing.

Romance is not only maintenance—the dishes, the schedules, the thousand small repairs of ordinary life. Somewhere inside them, two people are still paying attention.

## When you think you are finished learning

One of the quiet dangers in long relationships is believing you have finished learning the other person.

You know how they take their coffee, which stories they tell at parties, how they load the dishwasher, which topics will make them go quiet. You have a private joke that still makes you laugh after twenty years—a reference so small an outsider would miss it entirely. Familiarity is a gift. It is also a temptation. It can make us mistake our picture of a person for the person themselves.

Romance does not end when surprise ends. It begins to go quiet when we stop expecting the other person to surprise us.

## Staying curious

A couple in their fifties were driving home from a funeral when one of them said, almost casually, that they had been thinking about going back to school. The other nearly missed it—the sentence was so ordinary, so unlike anything they had talked about for years. For a moment the car felt strange, as if a door had opened in a house they thought they knew room by room.

Letting conversation become easier over the years without letting it become automatic.

Early years are often full of questions you are afraid to ask. Later years can become full of questions you are finally safe enough to ask—and still interested enough to mean.

## Play on an ordinary Tuesday

Romance does not require constant excitement. It requires room for two people who are still alive to one another.

Sometimes that is a joke told out of character at the end of a long day, or a note left on the counter for no reason except that someone was thought of. Sometimes it is a walk that turns into a conversation neither person planned, and one of them says, "I didn't know you still cared about that," and the other says, "I didn't know either."

## When romance goes thin

Not every marriage that lasts is romantically alive. Two people can share a house, a history, and a schedule and still treat each other as solved problems—known quantities, managed roles, familiar weather that no one bothers to report anymore. The toast can look identical. The story underneath may not.

That does not mean romance is shallow. It means romance is a practice, not a trophy. It can deepen. It can also go quiet while life remains orderly on the outside.

## This person, this kitchen

None of this explains why you fell in love with this person rather than someone else. Romance lives inside particularity—this person, this kitchen, this Tuesday. There are cold seasons in every long bond; people still find each other on ordinary Tuesdays, still listening, still asking, still leaving room for the person beside them to surprise them.
