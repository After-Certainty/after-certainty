# Chapter 23 - Following

By the fourth day out of Kestrel Gate, Cael had stopped counting miles. He counted what held.

The Seeker did not speak.

Rock. Staff. Riven still behind him. That was the list now. Everything else had become negotiable.

The trail narrowed between tilted slabs of stone. Wind pushed through them and the rock groaned like timber under load. Riven walked a few paces behind, testing each slab with his staff before committing his weight. Cael had stopped telling him to hurry. That was new too.

"You going to test every rock between here and wherever we're going?" Cael asked.

"If they need testing," Riven said.

"Christ."

Cael stepped onto the next slab. It held. That was enough.

The night before had been the worst yet. No fire. Hard bread soaked with dew. Riven had woken twice with Cael's nightmare still in his chest: the square, the count, Halverin's voice calling numbers into a crowd that wouldn't stop moving. The second time Cael had woken with Riven's memory of the canal stairs, cold water, somebody else's hands on the rail. Neither of them spoke about it at breakfast. There was no breakfast. They ate while walking.

At a bend in the trail Riven stopped and vomited nothing into the grass. Cael waited, jaw tight, feeling the dry heave in his own throat. When Riven straightened, he said, "Walking anyway." Cael nodded. The rule held.

"Should've skipped breakfast," Riven said. "If we'd had breakfast."

"We didn't," Cael said.

"Exactly my point."

Through the bond, Cael felt Riven stop calculating the walk back. Not all at once. Just a door closing somewhere behind them.

The Seeker's boots left prints that sometimes pointed the wrong direction. Cael had noticed on the second day and stopped mentioning it. Riven had started checking the direction against the sun without being asked. They didn't discuss it. They just both looked.

The trail forked around a split boulder. The Seeker tapped the left path with the staff and kept walking. No explanation. No warning. Just a direction.

Cael hesitated. Old habits wanted evidence, a reason, a list. The Seeker disappeared around the bend. Cael followed. Behind him, Riven muttered, "Fucking hell," and came too.

The left path dropped through a slot barely wide enough for shoulders. Wind squeezed through after them, cold and metallic.

The left path held. Rock. Staff. Riven still behind him.

By evening they stopped in a windbreak between two ridges. No fire. No conversation from the Seeker. Just bread, water, and the sound of cooling stone. Cael cut the ration loaf in half. Riven took his share. Across the bond, the familiar ache settled into place, not comfortable, not painful, familiar.

The windbreak was a crack between two tilted slabs, barely deep enough to hide from the wind. Stone still radiated the day's cold. Cael sat with his back against one wall. Riven sat against the other. The Seeker sat in the open, hood up, staff across their knees, facing the dark.

Cael watched Riven eat while Riven watched the Seeker not eat, hunger crossing the bond from both directions until food lost taste.

Riven set his bread down. "Can't."

"Same," Cael said.

They ate anyway. Wrong-tasting calories were still calories. Cael counted the bites Riven took without meaning to. Ledger habit. Some habits didn't die. They just changed what they counted.

The water skin tasted wrong again. Cael drank anyway. Riven made a face and drank too. Out here wrong was still water.

"Why us?" Cael asked.

The Seeker stared into the dark. No answer.

Riven rolled onto his back. "Still asking for sentences."

Cael looked out toward the hills. Maybe he was still asking for sentences. He didn't like any of the ones he had.

"You ever sleep through the night anymore?" Riven asked.

"No," Cael said.

"Me neither." The bond carried the admission before the words finished leaving his mouth.

For a while neither of them spoke. The wind shifted.

At a seep that tasted like tin, a Gate patrol passed going south—spears vertical, bread sacks half empty. One guard complained that demonstration prep had cut the ration issue again. Nobody looked at Cael or Riven. They weren't the problem today. They were just in the way.

They passed a maintenance post an hour later—tool rack empty, nails gone, roof beam patched with green wood and no crew. A slate nailed to the door read *crew reassigned, wall demonstration, return when posted.* Riven read it twice. Cael copied the date into the map margin. The north road was already forgetting how to hold itself up.

On the fifth day the trail dropped into a valley of bent trees whose trunks leaned against winds that had already passed. Riven stared up at them.

"Fractured," Riven said.

A branch moved overhead, and the leaves answered a moment later.

Cael kept walking.

They passed a dead cart overturned beside the trail. No horse. No driver. Cargo spilled: cracked pottery, a coil of rope grey with weather, a child's shoe. A woman's shawl was caught under the wheel hub, mud-stiff, still pinned as though she'd dropped it reaching for the reins. Nobody had come back for any of it. Cael didn't stop. Stopping invited questions.

Riven slowed anyway. Cael felt it through the bond before Riven's boots changed rhythm. "Don't," Cael said.

"Not touching it," Riven said.

"Good," Cael said.

"Just looking." Riven kept walking. "She left the shawl."

"Or couldn't reach it," Cael said.

Cael adjusted his grip on the staff. "Keep walking."

They passed a line of stones someone had set along the trail edge, then abandoned halfway. Cael counted eleven. Riven counted twelve. They didn't argue. Out here even counts lied.

The valley floor changed every few yards. Soft ground became hard. Hard ground gave way without warning. Twice his staff punched through crust into cold mud. The third time he stepped wide before testing it. His ankle rolled on hidden stone. Pain shot up his leg. Ahead, Riven stumbled and grabbed a tree.

"Son of a bitch," Riven said.

"You all right?" Cael asked.

"No." Riven straightened. "Walking anyway."

The answer made Cael smile despite himself. The smile didn't last.

Cael tested the mud with his staff before stepping. The staff sank to the ferrule. He tested again six inches left. Solid. He marked the spot with a stone and stepped. Riven followed the stone marker without comment. Timber thinking on broken ground. It was all he had left.

The Seeker waited on the far side. Before Cael reached them, they kicked a flat slab into the worst of the crossing—stepping stone, nothing more—and walked on without looking back.

Half a mile later Riven caught a toe on nothing and went down hard. Pain flashed through Cael's knee before he saw the fall. Mud streaked Riven's trousers when he climbed back to his feet.

Cael reached for him. Riven waved him off. "Don't. I'm—" He stopped. They both knew what he wasn't.

"You all right?"

Riven spat blood into the grass. "No." A beat. "Walking anyway."

Cael nodded. That was the rule now.

They rested at a seep. Cael filled both skins. Riven checked the map while Cael checked his knee. The swelling had started. By evening it would be both their knees. The bond didn't schedule injuries politely.

Riven folded the map with hands that shook. Cael took the case without asking and held it while Riven drank. Small work. Real work.

"How much farther to that thing?" Riven asked.

"Far enough that arguing won't help," Cael said.

"Christ." Riven stuffed the map away. "Fine."

Cael almost smiled. Almost. The smile died when Riven's pain crossed the bond again, sharp, honest, shared.

The rain started sometime after midday—cold mist, no clouds, soaking through by the second hour. The oatcake in Cael's pack had gone green at one corner from the damp. He'd deal with it at camp. Or not. Out here some problems waited.

"Halverin was right about one thing," Riven said.

That got his attention. "Which thing?"

"We'll die out here if we start treating this like a demonstration."

Cael adjusted his grip on the staff. "We're not demonstrating," Cael said.

"Maybe." Riven kicked at the trail. "Still feels like some bastard's counting."

A hour later they found chalk on a flat stone, arrows pointing north, child-sized. Someone had drawn them and someone else had scraped half away. Riven stared at the smear. "Kids from the square?"

"Kids from somewhere." Cael didn't touch the stone. "If we wash it, they'll draw again."

"So we leave it," Riven said.

"So we leave it." Cael walked on. The arrows pointed the same direction the Seeker did. That didn't make them true. It only made them hard to ignore.

That stayed with Cael for the rest of the climb.

He had spent years believing load could be measured, assigned, carried. Out here load moved without ledgers. The bond was the ledger now. Every step added a line neither of them had written.

The valley eventually gave way to open stone. The bent trees fell behind them. The ground behaved a little better—not enough, just enough to notice. By the time they reached the ridge, both of their legs were burning. The Seeker never slowed. Neither did they.

At the crest the country opened beneath them: broken hills, a distant storm, a pale track winding north, and beyond it, on the highest rise either of them could see, something built from stone. Not natural. Not close. Real.

Riven stood beside him. "That it?"

"Could be," Cael said.

"Christ, I fucking hope so." Riven stared at the distant shape. "Still looks built."

The descent off the crest took until midafternoon. Twice the trail vanished and reappeared ten paces left. Cael stopped testing every slab and started trusting the Seeker's prints instead. Trust by exhaustion. It was all he had.

The Seeker had already started down the far side. Cael felt something settle in his chest—not certainty, direction. Beside him, Riven went quiet. Through the bond, the same conclusion arrived from the other side, flat, heavy, inescapable.

"We're not going back," Cael said.

"No," Riven said.

They descended in silence. The stone shape ahead grew slowly: not a tower, not a fort. A ring. Standing stones on a rise, pale against the storm line. Drawings in Halverin's archive had lied. This looked smaller and heavier than any drawing.

The sixth day was worse. Storm on the horizon. Hail the size of knuckles. Riven turned his shoulder. Cael turned his. Bruises formed in both bodies before either saw the marks.

When it passed, Cael's map case was soaked through. Ink ran off the margin notes into grey smear. Merrow's woodcut blurred until the joined pair looked like one body with two heads. Riven tried to wipe it dry with his sleeve and made it worse. Cael took the case, wrung what he could from the leather, and handed it back without a word. They walked the rest of the afternoon with only the Seeker's prints and memory to trust.

Riven walked ahead for a while. Not leading. Just needing space the bond wouldn't grant. Cael felt the need and gave him three paces anyway. It wasn't privacy. It was courtesy.

They reached the ring's foothills by dusk.
