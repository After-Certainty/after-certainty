# Chapter 17 - The Demonstration

First bell rang over a square packed shoulder to shoulder. The sound bounced off stone and came back thin.

Families had camped on the side streets since before dawn while a bread seller moved through the press with a tray on his head calling prices nobody haggled over, two boys fought over a good view spot until a guard separated them with a spear shaft flat side and no blood, and a priest at the north rope burned a cheap incense stick that did nothing for the paste smell.

A magistrate aide counted heads at the east rope and wrote the tally on a pocket slate. Cael saw the number and looked away. Numbers would not keep the frame from dipping when fear hit.

At the west lane Merrow's medic wagon stood open with six cots made up and injury slates blank. Lyra checked the south tent flap from inside—cot placement, lane width, no front row—and did not look at the platform.

Cael had read the crowd rules from the steps ten minutes before first bell. His voice had held. Merrow had nodded once from the west lane, approval or warning, he could not tell. Now his mouth was dry and the sheet was folded in his belt anyway, useless once the square filled.

He stood on the east staging mark with his boots set on chalk and his hands loose at his sides because Halverin had told him clenched fists read as fear. The crowd filled every rope line and spilled into the side streets. Faces turned up.

Halverin had already spoken the civic preamble while Cael and Riven took their marks: rules, sightlines, mercy for the frightened if they stayed behind the rope. Now he raised the speaking horn again. His voice carried clean across stone.

"Today is not punishment," he said. "Today is proof what started in Ashfen can hold in public—shared burden, not chaos, when somebody steers it."

A magistrate clerk at the platform rail read the same lines from a sheet in a quieter voice for the side streets where the horn did not carry. Cael heard the echo arrive late from the north wall and filed it again. Halverin had said the crowd would absorb it. The wall did not absorb. It returned.

Riven stood three paces to Cael's left, jaw tight, eyes on the guards at the margin. Through the bond Cael felt Riven's dread in his own chest, steady and fast. He pushed it down and reached for counts instead.

"Signal one," Halverin called.

They stepped forward on the count. The bond caught and settled. For six steps they matched the road: one rhythm, one load. Cael's boots found chalk. Riven's found chalk. The square went quiet.

Halverin's clerk at the platform rail timed the steps on a pocket slate. Cael heard the stylus scratch between counts. The scratch arrived late from the north wall echo. Cael filed it and kept moving.

A cooper's nephew near the north rope held up a charm on a string—the same woodcut pair from the wall, painted crude in red. His uncle shoved his arm down. The charm swung once and stilled. Cael saw it and kept his eyes on Halverin's hands.

"Hold," Halverin said.

They held. Cael felt the load settle into his legs and bleed into Riven's without a visible shudder. The crowd could not see how much of holding was simply not flinching when another man's fear arrived in your chest.

The square was quiet enough that Cael heard a baby fussing near the north rope and a wagon wheel squeak at the west lane Merrow had kept open. He saw her there with two medics and an empty bed in the wagon. He saw the south tent flap and Lyra's arms folded, not in the front row.

Cael did not look at Lyra's tent. He looked at Halverin's hands on the horn and told himself the next minute could still be held.

"Signal two. Pressure."

Guards at the margins moved in a slow drill, shields up, boots stamping a pattern that was supposed to simulate stress without real blades. It looked stupid from the platform. It still worked on the crowd. People leaned in. A boy on his father's shoulders pointed at the shields and shouted.

Halverin counted breath. "In on my voice. Out on my voice. You do not carry alone."

Cael breathed. Riven breathed half a beat behind, then caught up. The lag scraped through the bond. Cael filed it. Adjusted. Tried not to hate Riven for the lag or himself for noticing.

Halverin let the imperfection show and called it correction. "See," he told the crowd. "Even a slip can be folded back into line."

A woman in the third row shouted back, "Fold my son's ankle back, then." Her boy sat on the curb with a wrap already on his foot from yesterday's rehearsal stamp. Merrow's medic had seen to him at dusk. The mother had brought him anyway because hope was cheaper than firewood.

Riven's anger spiked. *Folded.* Cael felt it hot through the bond. Riven did not say it loud enough for the crowd. His jaw worked.

Halverin raised the horn before the crowd could answer the mother. "Again. Tighter."

They ran the footwork sequence without the frame: step, set, turn, hold. Cael counted in his head. Chalk scuffed under his boots. On the turn Riven's heel caught a smear of blue chalk from yesterday's run and slid half an inch. Cael compensated and felt the bond pull, rope taking strain.

At the margin a guard's shield rim flashed wet paste from a broadside and threw it into Cael's eyes. He blinked and missed a half-beat. Halverin stopped the run.

"Eyes on me," Halverin said. "Not on the crowd. Not on each other. On me."

Riven muttered, "Eyes on the load. Goddamnit."

Halverin heard enough to frown. "Again."

They ran the sequence again. Cael focused on the chalk marks and the counts and the feel of Riven's weight moving when it should. Not trust. Mechanics. On the west berm mud had taught them the same lesson: fight the load and you lose. Move with it and you might live.

Between sequences a magistrate aide passed out folded programs with the day's counts printed inside. Riven tore his in half without reading. Cael kept his because throwing it would be a story.

Halverin walked down two steps from the platform. "Now we add load."

Two laborers rolled a timber frame into the center space, a mock breach frame like the one at the west berm. Cael and Riven had lifted real timber under smoke. This was cleaner and worse because everyone was watching.

A clerk at the platform edge checked rope tension with a pocket gauge and wrote numbers Cael could not read from the mark. Halverin called the laborers back. "Citizens will see the frame move. They will not see you compensate. Keep your eyes on the load."

Riven's jaw tightened. Cael felt the tightening in his own teeth. Merrow at the west lane watched without nodding. Lyra's tent stayed closed at the south. The paste smell mixed with incense and river wind until Cael could not separate them.

"Lift on three," Halverin said.

The laborers rolled the frame into position. Rope handles slapped stone. Cael flexed his fingers once, open, and set his grip where ash and mud had taught him. Riven mirrored without looking. For a breath their timing matched.

They lifted. The bond took the strain and spread it. Cael's shoulders burned. Riven's breath hitched. The crowd made a low rush of sound that hit the bond hard.

"Good," Halverin said. "Set. Again."

They set. They lifted again. On the second lift the frame caught wrong on the wheel and Cael felt splinters bite through gloves he did not remember wearing. Riven cursed under his breath. "Fuck." The bond spread the sting. They set anyway, reset their grip, lifted on Halverin's count. The crowd cheered as if pain were proof.

Halverin called a pause. Two laborers reset the wheel pin while a clerk noted the time on a slate. Cael flexed his fingers. Riven did not look at the crowd. The bond carried Riven's want to look and Cael's want to stop him.

"Lift on three," Halverin said again.

A merchant near the north rope started taking bets on whether they would drop the frame on the next lift. Merrow's runner slapped the slate out of his hand. The merchant cursed and melted back into the press. Cael heard the exchange and kept his eyes on the chalk.

On the third lift a boy in the front row shouted Cael's name like it was a song. The shout spiked Riven's attention. Riven missed half a count.

Riven had known that voice would belong to a stranger before he turned. On the road only caravan drivers and angry mothers used their names. Now children in the front row sang *Cael* like a rule they had memorized from a broadside. Riven knew the set of Cael's shoulders when he heard his own name called in Greyhaven—duck, deflect, keep moving. He did not know this version of his friend at all.

The frame dipped. The bond jerked. Pain lanced across Cael's palms and landed in Riven's at the same time.

"Focus," Halverin said, louder. "You're not two men. You're one pair."

On the fourth lift Halverin called margin increase without warning. Shields advanced. Boots stamped. Riven's attention snapped to a guard's face—split eyebrow, west berm—and the frame dipped again. Pain crossed palms. The crowd cheered wrong.

"Again," Halverin said. "Discipline."

Riven's anger hit the bond hot. Cael's shame answered it. The frame dipped again. "Fuck this," Riven muttered.

"Again," Halverin shouted. "Discipline."

They tried. Cael kept his eyes on the chalk marks and Halverin's hands. Tam the fishmonger stood at the north rope with his arms folded and his empty basket at his feet—the striped awning pole leaned against the rope post like he had camped there since first light. He did not cheer. He did not look away. He watched the platform the way people watched a body pulled from a river: not hoping for life, refusing to miss the moment the city named death.

When the frame dipped the first time, Tam's nephew covered his mouth. Tam did not move. When Halverin called correction, Tam's mouth tightened. Cael felt Riven notice him through the bond and file him under *grief that will not become a story on command*. Cael filed the same face under *name for the wrist list*—one more line he could carry until the sequence held.

A tanner's apprentice in the second row fainted from heat and paste and no breakfast. Lyra's orderly caught him before he hit stone. Halverin did not stop the run. Merrow's medic took the boy to the west lane without breaking formation.

Cael could feel the rhythm underneath the noise, the same rhythm that had worked on the road when nobody was selling hope from a platform. It was still there. It was thin. Riven's anger had not cooled; it ran under the rhythm and kept throwing them off. Cael did not try to soothe it. He tried to use it, the way you used weight instead of fighting it. The turn came clean. The hold came clean. For four counts the square believed them.

Then Halverin called margin increase again and the belief thinned.

Halverin climbed back up. "Citizens, you will see correction in real time. Watch."

Halverin signaled the guards to increase the margin drill. Boots stamped harder. A horn blew once, off rhythm. Someone in the crowd pushed against the rope and a guard shoved back. A woman swore. A child laughed, then was hushed.

A paste boy dropped his bucket and ran when the front row surged. Paste smeared the stones in a long wet stripe. Merrow's runner cursed and called for chalk to mark a new rope line six paces back.

Two guards at the margin argued about whether to fix the cracked rope post or leave it as evidence. Merrow said leave it. Halverin said fix it. Merrow won without raising her voice.

A paste crew at the north wall scraped the old broadside in wet strips so fresh paste would stick if Halverin ordered a correction sheet mid-run. They worked without looking at the platform.

At the south lane a baker's wife pulled her husband back from the rope line. "We're closing at noon whether he finishes or not." The husband looked at the platform and shook his head. They left through the side street with their tray half full.

The square tightened. Cael smelled sweat and paste and the iron edge of fear. Motion over the fountain basin looked wrong by a finger width, sound arriving late.

Cael thought of the riverside entrance. He thought of the girl's breath. He thought of Halverin's margin note on Merrow's sheet: *Hesitation at landing.* All of it arrived at once through the bond and Riven's answer was anger, hot and useless.

"Step," Halverin called.

Cael stepped. Riven stepped late. The bond pulled wrong. Cael's knee buckled. The crowd gasped.

"Hold the line," Halverin said. "Don't improvise."

Riven looked at him. Cael saw the refusal in Riven's face before he heard it in words.

"We're improvising every fucking second," Riven said, too low for the crowd, loud enough for Cael. "His goddamn counts are killing us."

"Not now," Cael said. "Jesus Christ. Not now."

"Now is all there is," Riven said. "Shit."

Halverin raised the horn again. "Again. From the top. You're not leaving this square until you hold."

The bond strained, not broken yet, but close. The air over the fountain basin looked wrong, a faint lag between motion and sound. Cael had seen distortion in Ashfen. This was different. This was a packed square with nowhere for fear to go.

He looked at the chalk marks. He looked at Merrow's open wagon. He looked at Halverin's face and saw the first crack in the magistrate's certainty, quickly plastered over with volume.

"One more sequence," Cael said to Riven. "Match me. Shit."

"I've been matching you," Riven said. "The whole fucking time."

"Then match harder," Cael said.

"Harder." Riven laughed once, raw. "Fuck almighty. Goddamnit."

Through the bond Riven felt how much Cael meant it—not cruelty, hope dressed as instruction. That was worse. On the Heartbridge path Cael had said *match me* when a cart was listing and meant *trust the weight*. Here he meant *trust Halverin until the weight proves him wrong*. Riven did not know how to say the difference without breaking the next count.

They turned toward the center mark. The crowd pressed in. The horn blew. Halverin called the count.

Cael stepped on one. Riven stepped on one. For one breath the square might hold. The frame rose. The bond spread the weight. Cael heard himself breathe out and felt Riven breathe with him.

Halverin called hold. They held. The guards stamped. The horn blew on rhythm for once. Cael allowed himself one thought: *maybe.* It was thin and he knew it was thin and the bond knew he knew—and some shameful part of him was relieved anyway, because if it held, the curse would not have been for nothing.

"Margin pressure increase," Halverin called.

Shields advanced one pace. Boots stamped harder. A woman near the front row fainted and was passed hand to hand over the rope to the medics. Merrow's people moved to meet her at the west lane without breaking formation. Cael saw Lyra step out of the south tent with a kit he recognized. She did not look at the platform. She looked at bodies.

Two guards at the north rope swapped places mid-drill and threw the echo off by half a beat. Cael felt Riven adjust late. Halverin called correction without stopping the drill. The crowd leaned in anyway.

The fainted woman's son followed the hand chain until Merrow's medic blocked him with a palm. "She's breathing. Stay back." The boy sat on the curb and cried quietly. Cael heard it through the bond as Riven's chest tightened—not pity, memory of stairs.

A magistrate aide tried to write the fainting into the sequence tally as *successful correction.* Merrow's medic blocked the slate with her elbow. "Injury lane. Not your column." The aide left with ink on his cuff and no number filed.

They lifted. Set. Lifted. The rhythm was there, underneath, the road rhythm, the berm rhythm. Cael clung to it. Riven clung to it. For nine counts they were one pair Halverin could name.

On the tenth count Halverin shortened the breath call—*in, out, in*—and Riven missed the switch. The frame dipped an inch. The crowd gasped. Halverin did not stop. "Correction," he told them. "Watch."

Between counts Cael heard fragments: a merchant betting on whether they would drop the frame; a priest muttering that Velorum tested the proud; a child asking if the bond hurt. Each fragment landed in the bond as static. Riven's jaw never loosened.

A paste crew at the magistrate wall stopped mid-stroke when the frame dipped. They watched without cheering. Halverin called correction and they went back to scraping the old sheet so fresh paste would stick.

Then a drunk man in the east street shouted that the raiders were back. Two people believed him. Ten did. The ripple started before guards could swallow it.

Then someone in the north rope screamed about a collapsing awning two streets over, real or rumor, and the crowd surged.

Cael saw the awning—or didn't. A strip of canvas tore free three streets north and snapped taut. Whether it was falling or flapping didn't matter once the front row pushed. A rope post cracked. Splinters from yesterday's rehearsal frame still littered the basin lip and cut someone's shin when the surge reversed.

Over the north roofline smoke still rose thin from a gutter fire no one had time to douse. Below, bodies packed until the rope lines bowed. A rope post tilted. A guard struck a man's shoulder to push him back and the man struck the guard in return. The front row became a hinge. The back rows pushed because they could not see and thought pushing was escape.

Merrow's medic at the west lane wrote the first injury name on her slate while the surge was still building—*faint, north rope*—and blocked Halverin's aide when he tried to copy it to a sequence tally.

Fear hit Cael and Riven at once through the bond, too fast to sort whose was whose. Halverin kept calling counts into noise that no longer obeyed counts.

A merchant's betting slate lay in the gutter where Merrow's runner had slapped it from his hand. A cooper's nephew dropped his charm string and did not reach for it. Cael kept his grip on the frame because letting go would be a story too.

"Hold!" Halverin shouted. "Hold the line!"

Merrow's voice cut under his from the west lane. "Medic to north rope. Clear the basin mouth." Two of her people moved against the surge while guards still tried to push the crowd back into a shape that no longer fit.

Cael tried. Riven tried. The bond did not settle. It amplified. A woman's panic. A guard's anger. Cael's old image of the girl at the stairs. Riven's heat at Lyra's name. It stacked.

The air over the fountain basin thickened. Sound lagged. Cael saw his own hand move and felt Riven's panic a beat later, then earlier, then both at once.

Halverin kept calling counts. Cael heard himself think: one more clean sequence and the stairs would stop being his fault; one more and Tam's brother might mean something; one more and the woman with the boy's ankle might forgive the stamp. He knew the thought was poison. He stepped toward the center mark anyway.

Halverin lowered the horn. For the first time in Cael's hearing, his voice shook.

"Follow my instruction," he said. "Do not—"

Cael stopped listening. He looked at Riven. Riven looked back. Not Halverin's rhythm. Theirs, hard-earned on roads and berms and the riverside entrance where failure had already taught them the cost.

"On me," Cael said.

"On you," Riven said. "Fuck. On you. Hell."

They moved together, not for the crowd, not for the magistrate, for the load that was trying to tear through both of them at once. The square was still screaming. The air was still wrong. Halverin was still talking.

For one breath the old rhythm caught—the road, the berm, mud and smoke. Their grips matched.

The rhythm tore.

The frame jerked. Cael's palms burned. Riven missed the count—not rebellion, lag—and the load came back wrong. Not shared. Scraping. The square roared once, unified, then split into a thousand separate sounds.

A woman in the front row screamed that the pair were bleeding. A man near the north rope shouted that they were fine, the magistrate would fix it, stand still. A boy cried because his father had let go of his hand. Parents pulled children back from the rope in clusters—some toward the square as if closer meant safer, some away as if distance could undo what they had watched.

Guards argued at the margin before the surge fully broke. "Hold the line," one said. "Open the west lane," another said. "Injury cord first," Merrow said from somewhere Cael could not see, and two guards peeled off toward her voice without saluting Halverin.

Tam stood at the north rope with his empty basket at his feet. He did not run. He watched the platform with the flat attention of a man who had already lost one person to missing and would not miss the moment the city chose a second story.

A magistrate runner pushed through the press toward the platform with a slate. "Witness statements for the correction broadside—"

Merrow's medic blocked him with a shoulder. "Injury slate first."

"Injury can wait—"

"Your sequence can wait." She wrote on Merrow's column without looking up. *Faint, north rope. Crush, basin lip.*

Two more runners crossed the square with different messages. One carried Halverin's crest; one carried Merrow's chalk. They collided at the east rope and argued in whispers too fast for Cael to follow. The Halverin runner said *crowd interference*. The Merrow runner said *names first*. Neither looked at the pair on the stone.

Merchants at the side ropes debated with their whole bodies. "It almost worked," a baker said. "Did you see them hold before the push?" His wife said, "I saw my tray half full and my shop closed for a show." A spice seller Cael recognized from rehearsal days—table legs still in memory—spat in the dust and said, "I saw paperwork win again."

"It worked until panic started," a guard told his sergeant. The sergeant said, "Panic is part of the test." The guard looked at Cael's split palms visible above the frame edge and did not sound convinced.

"We need more pairs on the walls," someone shouted from the third row. Someone else shouted, "Never again," and a third voice said, "Velorum cursed them," and none of the three looked at each other when they spoke, as if the square could hold every answer at once if everyone yelled loud enough.

Halverin stepped down one stair, hands out. "Citizens—stand back. This is a correction—"

No one stood back.

A clerk at the magistrate overhang wrote *change the run* on a fresh sheet. Merrow's guard took the sheet, read the header, and pointed at the injury slate. "Wrong desk." The clerk sat on the steps and began a second sheet without tearing the first.

Halverin's senior aide appeared at the platform rail with three broadside drafts facedown. "Which version do you want, magistrate? *Crowd broke the count* or *the pair mismatched on the hard lift*—"

Halverin did not answer. He looked at Cael and Riven on the frame and for the first time since the stairs briefing Cael saw fear without a chart underneath it.

Horror came first—the bodies, the splinters, Lyra's cots filling. Grief came second, and it was worse because it was not for the injured. It was for the story breaking on the stone between them while the crowd still tried to name what it had seen.

Through the bond he felt Riven—not triumph, not *I told you so*, just pain and a sick relief that hurt almost as much as the splinters in Cael's palms.

The frame dipped. Cael's knees buckled. Riven's followed an instant later, pulled by load or bond or both. They went down on the stone with the mock timber between them and the crowd's noise pressing from every side.

Lyra reached the south lane cord before Cael could find her face in the blur. She knelt at a woman who had gone down in the press—not at the platform, not at them. Cloth. Water. Two fingers on a wrist. She did not look up when the frame splintered.

"Back," she said to the press without raising her voice. "Give her air." The crowd did not give air. It leaned harder anyway.

Halverin raised the horn with a cracked mouth. "This is not—citizens, stay calm—"

"Shut the hell up," someone shouted from the north rope.

The shout spread—not cheer, not jeer, a low furious noise of people who had been sold steadiness and watched it turn into visible harm.

Cael looked up from the stone. The demonstration was over. Everyone in the square knew it before Halverin did. What they did not agree on yet was why.

At the east rope a cooper told his nephew the bond had failed. The nephew said Halverin had failed. At the north rope Tam said nothing and picked up his empty basket. A priest crossed himself. A paste boy copied *LOSS* on a scraped broadside strip and held it up until a guard tore it down and another guard pinned it to Merrow's injury crate.

Runners left the square in three directions with three different stories. Clerks gathered witness names from whoever would still talk to them. Merrow's people cleared the basin mouth one body at a time. Halverin stood on the platform with the horn lowered and no sequence left that the square would obey.

Cael held Riven's eyes across the splintered frame. The bond was still loud—pain, shame, anger, relief braided too tight to sort.

The square had come to watch steadiness.

It was already talking about something else.
