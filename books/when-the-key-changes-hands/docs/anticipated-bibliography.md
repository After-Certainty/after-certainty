# Anticipated Bibliography

## Purpose

This is a working research map, not a final bibliography. It identifies thinkers, books, and articles that may sharpen the manuscript's claims, provide counterarguments, or anchor specific chapters. Sources should appear only where they clarify a distinction already made visible by an ordinary scene. The book should not become a survey of moral philosophy, personality psychology, trust theory, or leadership studies.

## Research principle

Use the smallest number of sources needed to make the argument intellectually responsible. Prefer original works when practical, use secondary sources to orient or complicate, and keep the prose centered on the book's own causal chain:

costly choice → repeated pattern → inferred character → warranted expectation → trust → social signal → symbolic attachment → inherited expectation → succession mismatch.

## Core Sources For The Whole Book

1. Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, especially Books II, III, and VI.

Use for: habit, practical wisdom, voluntary action, choice, and the formation of character through repeated practice.

Likely chapters: 3, 5, 7, 8, 12.

Caution: Do not let Aristotle become the book's governing vocabulary. The manuscript's distinctive contribution is epistemic and social, not merely a restatement of virtue ethics.

2. Julia Annas, Intelligent Virtue (Oxford University Press, 2011).

Use for: virtue as learned practical intelligence rather than automatic habit; the relation between practice, reflection, and growth.

Likely chapters: 5, 7, 8.

3. Alasdair MacIntyre, After Virtue, 3rd ed. (University of Notre Dame Press, 2007; originally 1981).

Use for: practices, traditions, narrative unity, and the social settings in which virtues become intelligible.

Likely chapters: 7, 11, 15.

Caution: Use selectively; the book does not need MacIntyre's full diagnosis of modern morality.

4. Iris Murdoch, The Sovereignty of Good (Routledge, 1970).

Use for: moral attention, the difficulty of seeing another person clearly, and the danger of fantasy replacing reality.

Likely chapters: 4, 6, 8, 15.

5. John Doris, Lack of Character: Personality and Moral Behavior (Cambridge University Press, 2002).

Use for: the situationist challenge to stable global character traits.

Likely chapters: 1, 3, 4, 12.

Role: primary counterweight. The book must acknowledge that conduct varies across situations and that observers overgeneralize from limited evidence.

6. Gilbert Harman, "Moral Philosophy Meets Social Psychology: Virtue Ethics and the Fundamental Attribution Error," Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society 99 (1999): 315--331.

Use for: skepticism about robust character inference and the tendency to attribute behavior to disposition rather than context.

Likely chapters: 1, 4, 12.

7. Walter Mischel, Personality and Assessment (Wiley, 1968), and later work on personality signatures.

Use for: cross-situational variability and the idea that stable patterns may take an "if--then" form rather than appearing identically everywhere.

Likely chapters: 3, 4, 9.

## Part I — How Character Becomes Visible

Chapter 1 — The Question That Wouldn't Stay Simple

8. G. E. M. Anscombe, "Modern Moral Philosophy," Philosophy 33, no. 124 (1958): 1--19.

Use for: why moral inquiry cannot be reduced to a ledger of outcomes and prohibitions without an account of the person and practical life.

Role: light framing only.

9. Bernard Williams, Moral Luck (Cambridge University Press, 1981), especially "Moral Luck."

Use for: the unstable relation among intention, outcome, circumstance, and moral judgment.

Likely chapters: 1, 2, 3.

Chapter 2 — Good Outcomes Are Not Character

10. Robert K. Merton, "The Unanticipated Consequences of Purposive Social Action," American Sociological Review 1, no. 6 (1936): 894--904.

Use for: beneficial intention, successful action, and system effects coming apart.

11. James C. Scott, Seeing Like a State (Yale University Press, 1998).

Use for: visible success metrics obscuring local knowledge, process damage, and institutional cost.

Role: optional systems counterpoint; avoid turning the chapter into a governance book.

12. Thomas Nagel, "Moral Luck," in Mortal Questions (Cambridge University Press, 1979).

Use for: how outcome judgments can exceed what conduct alone reveals about character.

Chapter 3 — A Line Must Be Tested

13. Harry G. Frankfurt, The Importance of What We Care About (Cambridge University Press, 1988).

Use for: volition, what a person identifies with, and why resisting a desire can reveal something about the will.

14. Philip Zimbardo, The Lucifer Effect (Random House, 2007), used cautiously alongside critiques.

Use for: situational pressure and why refusal under real institutional pressure may be diagnostically meaningful.

Caution: Do not overstate contested experimental evidence.

15. Stanley Milgram, Obedience to Authority (Harper & Row, 1974), used with later methodological criticism.

Use for: pressure, compliance, and the moral significance of refusal.

Caution: historical and methodological context is essential.

Chapter 4 — Patterns, Not Souls

16. Lee Ross and Richard E. Nisbett, The Person and the Situation (McGraw-Hill, 1991).

Use for: attribution error, situational variation, and humility in character judgment.

17. Susan Wolf, "Moral Saints," Journal of Philosophy 79, no. 8 (1982): 419--439.

Use for: resisting totalizing pictures of the morally good person and preserving human plurality.

18. Cheshire Calhoun, "Standing for Something," Journal of Philosophy 92, no. 5 (1995): 235--260.

Use for: integrity as a publicly intelligible pattern of commitment, not merely private consistency.

## Part Ii — How Character Forms

Chapter 5 — The Cost of a Choice

19. Immanuel Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, especially the discussion of acting from duty.

Use for: the intuition that doing what inclination already favors may reveal something different from acting against inclination for a reason.

Caution: Present as one lens, not the book's final theory. The manuscript should not equate reluctance with virtue or pleasure with moral shallowness.

20. Angela Duckworth, Grit (Scribner, 2016), used critically.

Use for: sustained effort and disciplined repetition.

Caution: distinguish perseverance from moral character and acknowledge structural limits.

Chapter 6 — Love as Voluntary Inconvenience

21. Virginia Held, The Ethics of Care: Personal, Political, and Global (Oxford University Press, 2006).

Use for: care as morally serious and socially consequential rather than private sentiment.

22. Joan C. Tronto, Moral Boundaries: A Political Argument for an Ethic of Care (Routledge, 1993).

Use for: care as practice, responsibility, competence, responsiveness, and the unequal distribution of caring labor.

Likely chapters: 6, 8, 12.

23. Nel Noddings, Caring: A Relational Approach to Ethics and Moral Education, 2nd ed. (University of California Press, 2013; originally 1984).

Use for: relational attention, receptivity, and care as enacted response.

24. Harry G. Frankfurt, The Reasons of Love (Princeton University Press, 2004).

Use for: how caring changes what counts as a reason inside a person's choices.

25. bell hooks, All About Love: New Visions (William Morrow, 2000).

Use for: love as practice involving care, commitment, knowledge, responsibility, respect, and trust.

Caution: connect to the existing Everyone Knows Love argument rather than re-defining love from scratch.

Chapter 7 — What Repetition Leaves Behind

26. William James, "Habit," in The Principles of Psychology (1890).

Use for: how repeated action creates channels that make later action easier.

27. Pierre Bourdieu, Outline of a Theory of Practice (Cambridge University Press, 1977).

Use for: habitus and the way dispositions are socially formed and embodied.

Caution: optional; avoid importing a full sociological apparatus.

28. Wendy Wood, Good Habits, Bad Habits (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2019).

Use for: contemporary habit research and the role of cues, environments, and repetition.

Chapter 8 — Failure, Repair, and Return

29. Margaret Urban Walker, Moral Repair: Reconstructing Moral Relations after Wrongdoing (Cambridge University Press, 2006).

Use for: repair as relational work after wrongdoing, not merely private remorse.

30. Charles L. Griswold, Forgiveness: A Philosophical Exploration (Cambridge University Press, 2007).

Use for: apology, accountability, resentment, forgiveness, and the limits of restoration.

31. Nick Smith, I Was Wrong: The Meanings of Apologies (Cambridge University Press, 2008).

Use for: what makes acknowledgment and apology substantive rather than strategic.

32. Judith Herman, Truth and Repair: How Trauma Survivors Envision Justice (Basic Books, 2023).

Use for: repair centered on harmed people, truth, acknowledgment, and limits of reconciliation.

Caution: use carefully and proportionately; the chapter is broader than trauma justice.

## Part Iii — Why Character Matters

Chapter 9 — The Future We Cannot Specify

33. Herbert A. Simon, "A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice," Quarterly Journal of Economics 69, no. 1 (1955): 99--118.

Use for: bounded rationality and decision-making under incomplete information.

34. Friedrich A. Hayek, "The Use of Knowledge in Society," American Economic Review 35, no. 4 (1945): 519--530.

Use for: dispersed knowledge and why rules cannot centralize every relevant future fact.

Caution: use the epistemic point without importing the full political program.

35. Michael Polanyi, The Tacit Dimension (University of Chicago Press, 1966).

Use for: knowledge that cannot be fully specified in rules or contracts.

Chapter 10 — Trust Is a Bet on a Pattern

36. Annette Baier, "Trust and Antitrust," Ethics 96, no. 2 (1986): 231--260.

Use for: trust as accepted vulnerability within relationships of dependence and power.

37. Russell Hardin, Trust and Trustworthiness (Russell Sage Foundation, 2002).

Use for: trust as grounded expectation, especially encapsulated interest, and the distinction between trust and trustworthiness.

38. Karen Jones, "Trust as an Affective Attitude," Ethics 107, no. 1 (1996): 4--25.

Use for: trust as an emotionally inflected stance toward another's goodwill, not merely probability calculation.

39. Onora O'Neill, A Question of Trust: The BBC Reith Lectures 2002 (Cambridge University Press, 2002).

Use for: the distinction between increasing trust and becoming trustworthy; transparency, accountability, and institutional reliance.

40. Niklas Luhmann, Trust and Power (Wiley, 1979).

Use for: trust as a way of reducing social complexity.

Caution: the existing trust book already owns this broader argument; here it should remain background.

Chapter 11 — When Symbols Borrow Character

41. Erving Goffman, The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (Anchor, 1959).

Use for: public role, performance, interpretation, and the gap between person and presented self.

42. Peter L. Berger and Thomas Luckmann, The Social Construction of Reality (Anchor, 1966).

Use for: institutionalization, typification, and meanings that persist beyond the original interaction.

43. Paul Ricoeur, Oneself as Another (University of Chicago Press, 1992).

Use for: identity, narrative, attribution, and the difference between sameness and selfhood.

Caution: likely a secondary or footnote source rather than a major exposition.

44. Max Weber, Economy and Society, especially the types of legitimate domination.

Use for: authority attached to office, tradition, law, and charisma.

Likely chapters: 11, 13, 15, 16.

Chapter 12 — The Limits of Character

45. James Madison, Federalist No. 51.

Use for: the classic claim that institutions must account for human fallibility rather than depend on virtue alone.

46. Karl Popper, The Open Society and Its Enemies, Vol. 1 (Routledge, 1945), especially the question of controlling rulers rather than merely selecting good ones.

Use for: why systems of correction matter even when leaders appear admirable.

47. Elinor Ostrom, Governing the Commons (Cambridge University Press, 1990).

Use for: institutional design, monitoring, graduated sanctions, participation, and context-sensitive governance.

48. Philip Pettit, Republicanism: A Theory of Freedom and Government (Oxford University Press, 1997).

Use for: non-domination and why unchecked discretionary power remains dangerous even under a benevolent holder.

## Part Iv — When Character Becomes Social

Chapter 13 — When Others Look to You

Use for: observational learning, modeling, and why conduct becomes instruction when others watch.

50. Ronald A. Heifetz, Leadership Without Easy Answers (Harvard University Press, 1994).

Use for: leadership under uncertainty, adaptive work, and the temptation to supply false certainty.

Caution: use as a dialogue partner, not a competing definition of leadership.

51. James MacGregor Burns, Leadership (Harper & Row, 1978).

Use for: the moral and relational dimensions of leadership.

Caution: distinguish the manuscript's descriptive definition from Burns's normative categories.

Chapter 14 — Character Becomes Permission

52. Edgar H. Schein, Organizational Culture and Leadership, 5th ed. (Wiley, 2016).

Use for: how leaders teach groups what matters through attention, reaction, reward, and selection.

53. Albert Bandura, "Moral Disengagement in the Perpetration of Inhumanities," Personality and Social Psychology Review 3, no. 3 (1999): 193--209.

Use for: how authority, language, diffusion of responsibility, and normalization permit harmful conduct.

54. Diane Vaughan, The Challenger Launch Decision (University of Chicago Press, 1996).

Use for: normalization of deviance and how repeated tolerated exceptions become institutional expectations.

55. Amy C. Edmondson, The Fearless Organization (Wiley, 2018).

Use for: leader behavior, psychological safety, speaking up, and the social consequences of admitting uncertainty and error.

Chapter 15 — The Symbol and the Person

56. Murray Edelman, The Symbolic Uses of Politics (University of Illinois Press, 1964).

Use for: political roles and events as symbols that organize perception and expectation.

57. Clifford Geertz, "Centers, Kings, and Charisma: Reflections on the Symbolics of Power," in Local Knowledge (Basic Books, 1983).

Use for: how authority and symbolic centrality become fused.

58. Shmuel N. Eisenstadt, ed., Max Weber on Charisma and Institution Building (University of Chicago Press, 1968).

Use for: charisma becoming routinized and attached to institutions.

59. David A. Snow and Robert D. Benford, work on framing processes.

Use for: how complex persons and events become compressed into portable interpretive frames.

Caution: likely optional unless Chapter 15 needs stronger social-movement language.

Chapter 16 — When the Key Changes Hands

60. Philip Selznick, Leadership in Administration (University of California Press, 1957).

Use for: institutional embodiment of values and the leader's role in giving an organization distinctive character.

61. Oscar Grusky, "Managerial Succession and Organizational Effectiveness," American Journal of Sociology 69, no. 1 (1963): 21--31.

Use for: succession as disruption and the organizational consequences of changing leaders.

62. Jeffrey Pfeffer, "The Ambiguity of Leadership," Academy of Management Review 2, no. 1 (1977): 104--112.

Use for: how observers attribute outcomes and meaning to leaders beyond what evidence supports.

63. James G. March and Johan P. Olsen, Rediscovering Institutions (Free Press, 1989).

Use for: roles, rules, identities, and institutional expectations that shape successors before they act.

64. John W. Gardner, On Leadership (Free Press, 1990).

Use for: renewal, continuity, and the challenge of sustaining institutions beyond one person.

65. Ronald Heifetz, Alexander Grashow, and Marty Linsky, The Practice of Adaptive Leadership (Harvard Business Press, 2009).

Use for: the successor's third path—remaining present, distinguishing technical from adaptive responsibility, and resisting both rescue and abandonment.

## Epilogue — The Lock Does Not Know

66. Reinhold Niebuhr, The Irony of American History (University of Chicago Press, 1952), used lightly.

Use for: moral humility, the limits of self-knowledge, and the danger of mistaking virtue claims for guarantees.

67. Hannah Arendt, Responsibility and Judgment (Schocken, 2003).

Use for: judgment under conditions where rules do not settle the case, personal responsibility, and the refusal to surrender thought.

68. John Dewey, Human Nature and Conduct (Henry Holt, 1922).

Use for: habit, moral inquiry, consequence, and character as something formed in ongoing interaction with environments.

Possible use: epilogue or throughout as a quiet pragmatist foundation.

## Possible Empirical And Historical Case Sources

These should be selected only after the scenes are chosen.

- Longitudinal studies of conscientiousness, honesty-humility, and trait stability, used to test how predictive recurring conduct really is.

- Research on the person--situation debate and behavioral signatures.

- Organizational succession cases in family firms, nonprofits, religious communities, corporations, and civic institutions.

- Historical cases where a successor inherited symbolic expectations formed by a predecessor's restraint, decisiveness, warmth, rescue behavior, or certainty.

- Cases of apology and institutional repair where later conduct either changed the pattern or revealed strategic compliance.

- Studies of leader modeling, ethical climate, psychological safety, normalization of deviance, and moral disengagement.

## Drafting Rules For Citation

1. Cite thinkers after the ordinary scene has made the problem visible.

2. Do not stack multiple philosophers merely to demonstrate breadth.

3. Give counterarguments real force, especially the situationist critique of stable character.

4. Distinguish empirical claims from philosophical interpretation.

5. Prefer paraphrase over quotation; reserve direct quotations for unusually precise language.

6. Recheck editions, page numbers, article metadata, and disputed empirical findings during drafting.

7. Let sources constrain overclaiming rather than replace the book's own reasoning.

Final test

Every citation should answer at least one of these questions:

- What distinction does this source sharpen?

- What assumption does it challenge?

- What evidence does it add?

- What overclaim does it prevent?

- Why does this source belong in this chapter rather than in one of the neighboring books?

The bibliography should make the manuscript more careful, not make it sound more certain than the evidence allows.
