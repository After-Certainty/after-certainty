# Chapter 04 - The Highlands

Cold found the places fire never reached: the seam of Cael’s collar, the gap behind his belt buckle, the hinge of each finger where rope had worn the skin shiny. He woke to grey light and the sound of wind worrying the hollow’s mouth, a steady scrape like someone trying to lever a board loose.

Riven was already sitting, knees drawn up, blanket around his shoulders, staring at dead coals like they owed him an answer.

“We move,” Cael said.

Riven nodded once, no fight in it yet.

They ate what was left, hard and small, and stamped the fire dead until the ash went flat and cold under Cael’s boot. Packing was the same work it always was: rope, blanket, buckle, strap, check the knife, check the water skin, check that Riven’s latch wasn’t about to spit the pack off halfway up a scree slope.

The road out of the hollow wasn’t a road anymore. It was a suggestion scratched into rock, wide enough for goats and bad ideas. Wind hit them sideways the moment they cleared the lee, carrying grit that stung Cael’s eyes and found the wet rim of his mouth.

He set the pace the way he set a ladder: weight where it belonged, no drama, no show. If Riven wanted to turn every climb into an argument, fine, but Cael wasn’t going to give him the opening by sprinting.

Gravel slid under Cael’s boots on the first pitch. He listened for the sound behind him, the scrape-halt-scrape that meant Riven was still on his feet. It came, half a beat late.

Cael slowed without turning his head. “Don’t rush the loose stuff.”

“I’m not rushing.”

“You’re rushing.”

Riven’s breath went rough. “You’re counting my steps.”

Cael let that sit. Accurate enough to sting.

The climb tightened into a cut where stone rose on one side and air opened on the other. Below, trees looked like bristles, too far down to catch you if you leaned wrong. Water threaded the rock in a thin line, frozen in the shade, slick where sun hadn’t touched yet.

Cael tested each placement before he shifted his weight. His palms went cold against stone until friction warmed them. Riven’s shadow crossed his boots, then fell back again, the way it did when Riven’s pride was losing to his knees.

They stopped in a notch where the wind dropped for a few heartbeats. Cael drank, passed the skin without looking, and watched Riven’s throat work when he swallowed.

“You hear that?” Riven asked, eyes on the empty air above the cut.

“Hear what.”

Riven tilted his head. “Like… an echo. But wrong.”

Cael listened. Wind. His own pulse loud in his ears. Far off, a hawk’s cry, thin and clean.

“I don’t hear wrong,” Cael said.

Riven’s mouth tightened. “Fine.”

They went on.

By midday the sky stayed the color of old iron, no sun to speak of, just brightness behind cloud that hurt to look at for too long. The trail switchbacked until Cael’s thighs burned and his pack strap dug a line he could map with his eyes closed.

A scatter of loose stone shifted under him without warning, not a slide, just a handful of pebbles deciding to go somewhere else. Cael’s foot skidded half an inch. His hand snapped to rock, fingers finding a crack that bit back.

Behind him, Riven swore, sharp and involuntary, like the slip had happened to him too.

Cael steadied. “You good?”

“Yeah.” Riven’s voice came too fast. “Yeah. Christ.”

Cael glanced back. Riven’s face was pale under wind-burn, eyes too wide, fixed on Cael’s boots like they were the only honest thing left on the mountain.

“What,” Cael said.

“Nothing.”

“That wasn’t nothing.”

Riven swallowed. “My stomach dropped like I missed a step on the dock.”

Cael felt his own gut answer, a stupid echo, and hated it. “We’re high,” he said, because explanation was a tool he trusted. “Air’s thin. Your body lies.”

“If my body lies, yours does too.”

Cael turned forward again before he could snap. “Then don’t listen to it.”

They climbed.

The wrongness didn’t announce itself with a drum. It crept in as details Cael’s carpenter brain didn’t want to catalog because once you started, you couldn’t pretend you hadn’t.

A patch of moss grew against the grain of shade, bright where it should’ve been dry.

A gust hit his left ear first, then his right, like sound arrived late to the second side of his head.

Once, his own footfall came back to him a half second after his boot hit, as if the mountain withheld the noise until it checked his work.

Cael told himself fatigue. Told himself wind funnels. Told himself the pamphlet had filled their heads with junk and now every oddity wanted a name.

Then the water did it.

They reached a seep in a crease of rock, clean enough to refill the skins if you didn’t mind cold teeth. Cael knelt and cupped his hand. The surface held too still for moving water. When he dipped his fingers, ripples spread slower than they should have, like the water was thinking about each ring before it agreed to let it go.

Riven knelt beside him without being asked, eyes on the same wrong stillness.

“Don’t,” Cael said before Riven could say it.

“Don’t what.”

“Don’t make it a sermon.”

Riven’s laugh came out broken. “I’m not the one who treats weird like a personal insult.”

Cael filled his skin anyway, because thirst was real even if the seep felt off. The first swallow tasted metallic, not enough to spit, enough to notice. He capped it and stood, wiping his wet hand on his trousers.

“We keep going,” he said.

“Cael.”

“We keep going.”

Riven stood too, pack shifting, jaw set. “Say it.”

“Say what.”

“That you see it.”

Cael looked at the seep, at the slow rings finally dying, at the rock above them where nothing grew except a hairline crack that ran too straight for comfort.

“I see we’re not on a harbor road,” Cael said carefully. “I see loose stone and bad wind. I see two men who chose to stop treating a statue like a bedtime story.”

Riven stared at him. “That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the answer I’ve got.” Cael adjusted the strap across his chest until the buckle dug in a familiar way, pain that behaved. “If you want to turn around, say it now while we can still lie to ourselves about the grade.”

Riven’s eyes flashed. “I’m not turning around.”

“Then quit asking me to name ghosts.”

They walked.

Afternoon brought sleet that wasn’t quite rain, needles that stung cheeks and found every seam. Cael pulled his collar higher and listened to Riven’s breathing behind him, ragged, stubborn, still keeping pace.

The trail opened onto a bench of bare rock, wide enough to breathe. Below, the land fell away in layers of pine and shale until the horizon blurred into cloud.

Cael stopped because stopping was part of work: check the body, check the route, check the other man before pride got him killed.

Riven halted a step later, hands on his thighs, head down. Water dripped from his hairline.

“You need rest,” Cael said.

“I need you to stop talking like I’m a horse.”

“You need rest,” Cael repeated, flat. “We take ten minutes. We eat. We don’t stand on the edge like idiots.”

Riven moved back from the drop without arguing, which meant he was more tired than he looked.

They sat with their backs to a windbreak of stacked stone that might’ve been natural or might’ve been old work, too weathered to tell. Cael broke bread with his knife, divided it exact, and passed Riven’s share.

Riven chewed slow, eyes on the cloud line.

“You still think it’s just thin air?” Riven asked.

Cael watched a gust move through the pines below, a visible wave, delayed, like the trees noticed late and then all agreed to sway together.

“No,” he said, because lying to Riven had never been free anyway.

Riven’s shoulders dropped a fraction. “Okay.”

“Okay doesn’t fix it,” Cael said.

“I didn’t say it fixes it. I said okay.”

Cael nodded once. “We’re close.”

“Close to what.”

Cael tipped his chin toward the next rise, where the rock looked paler, almost bleached, and the wind seemed to avoid it in a way that didn’t make sense until you watched long enough to feel uneasy.

“Pamphlet calls it the first circle,” Cael said, the words tasting like swallowing metal too. “Could be garbage. Could be a ring of stones and a story.”

“And if it’s not garbage.”

Cael met Riven’s eyes. “Then we find out what ‘not garbage’ feels like.”

Riven looked away first, jaw working. “You’re still glad you came.”

Cael flexed his cold fingers until the joints ached. “I’m not glad,” he said. “I’m here.”

They packed up and moved before the cold could settle into their bones and talk them into staying.

The pale rock rose like a scar. As they climbed toward it, Cael’s skin prickled, not from sleet, not from fear he’d admit out loud, but from pressure, the kind that came before thunder, except the sky never broke open, it just held.

Sound dampened. Even their boots seemed quieter, as if the mountain took each step and didn’t give all of it back.

Riven fell in beside him instead of behind, which Cael hated and needed at the same time.

“You feel that,” Riven muttered.

“Yes.”

“What is it.”

Cael swallowed. “Weight.”

“In the air.”

“In the air,” Cael agreed, because naming it narrow kept it from spreading.

They crested the last lip and the world opened: not a temple, not a door, just a wide dish of stone with old marks cut into it, circles within circles, worn shallow by wind until they looked like accidents unless you knew what you were looking for.

The hair on Cael’s arms lifted.

His heart beat once, hard, then steadied into a rhythm that wasn’t quite his own panic and wasn’t quite calm.

Riven whispered, “Christ.”

Cael stepped onto the first ring before he could talk himself out of it, because hesitation was how you lost a roofline in a storm. The stone felt warmer than it should’ve been, like sun had been stored in it through days of iron sky.

“Cael,” Riven said, voice tight.

“I know.”

“You don’t know.”

Cael looked back. Riven stood on the rim like a man testing ice.

“We came for this,” Cael said, and heard how thin it sounded, how much like the pamphlet it was, and said the next part anyway because it was true in the only way that mattered now. “We don’t get answers from turning around.”

Riven’s eyes were too bright. “Answers wasn’t the word I’d use.”

Cael held out his hand, palm up, an offer he hadn’t planned and couldn’t take back.

Riven stared at it a long beat, then took it, grip hot and shaking, and stepped in.

The pressure tightened, not on skin, on the inside of his ribs, like something had found the shape of him and pressed a template down.

Cael swallowed metal again and kept his feet flat on the stone.

Whatever came next, they were already inside it.
