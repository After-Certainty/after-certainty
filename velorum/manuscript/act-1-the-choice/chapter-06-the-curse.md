# Chapter 06 - The Curse

Sound came back wrong.

Cael heard his own breath and Riven’s breath overlaid, same inhale, same exhale, not matched on purpose, matched like someone had tied two bellows to one handle.

He tried to let go of Riven’s hand.

His fingers wouldn’t uncurl all the way, then did too fast, spasmed, skin hot and cold in the same second.

Riven made a noise in his throat, not a word, and Cael heard it twice: once in his ears, once behind his eyes.

Cael turned his head. “You all right?”

“No,” Riven said, and the syllable arrived with an echo Cael felt in his own mouth, copper taste and all.

Cael’s stomach lurched. He planted his boots wider on the stone. The rings below him looked shallow now, almost silly, like any groove could matter compared to what was happening inside his skull.

“Sit,” Cael said, because sitting was what you did when someone went pale, when knees shook, when the world did something you couldn’t brace with a beam.

“I’m not going to faint,” Riven snapped.

“You’re going to sit,” Cael said, and hated how much it sounded like an order, and didn’t take it back.

Riven sat anyway, sudden, like his strings cut. Cael dropped beside him, close enough to catch a shoulder if Riven tipped, close enough to feel heat rolling off him in a wave that didn’t match the cold air.

Cael’s own hands shook.

He clenched them once, hard, nails biting palms, trying to return sensation to ownership.

It didn’t work.

Riven’s fear slid into him anyway, slick and immediate, not “I think Riven is scared,” not observation, intrusion, like someone had poured Riven’s panic into Cael’s chest through a funnel.

Cael swallowed bile. “Okay,” he said, voice too steady, the steadiness a lie he reached for because lies could be scaffolding. “Okay. We breathe.”

“I am breathing,” Riven said, and Cael felt the irritation under the panic like a second tongue.

“Then we breathe slower.”

“You sound like Mara.”

“Good,” Cael said. “Mara keeps people alive.”

Riven laughed once, high and wrong, and Cael felt the laugh like a cut on his own lip.

He stared at the stone between his boots until the grain lines sharpened. Focus. Name objects. Nail. rope. knife hilt. buckle.

His mind said, not in his voice, stop listing, and Cael flinched so hard his teeth clicked.

“What,” Riven said instantly.

“Nothing.”

“That wasn’t nothing.”

Cael looked up. Riven’s eyes were wide, pupils blown, fixed on Cael’s face like he was reading something written there in ink only he could see.

“Don’t,” Cael started, not sure what he was forbidding.

Riven whispered, “You thought ‘nail.’”

Cael’s throat went dry. “I said nail.”

“You thought it first,” Riven said, voice shaking. “I heard it. Christ, Cael, I heard it inside.”

Cael’s first instinct was denial, clean and familiar as a square cut board.

The second instinct was worse: relief, sharp and shameful, because if Riven heard him, maybe he wasn’t alone with whatever had moved into his head.

Cael clamped that relief down hard.

“We’re wound up,” he said.

“We’re not wound up,” Riven said. “We’re—this is—”

“Don’t spiral.”

“Don’t tell me not to spiral when you’re scared too,” Riven snapped, and Cael felt the snap land in his own ribs, deserved.

He inhaled slow through his nose, exhaled slower, the way he did before lifting a heavy door.

Riven’s shoulders mirrored the breath without looking at him.

Cael noticed.

Riven noticed that Cael noticed.

They both flinched.

“Okay,” Riven breathed. “Okay. That’s… that’s messed up.”

“Yeah,” Cael said.

For a few seconds neither spoke, and the silence still wasn’t quiet. It was full of almost-words, half-shaped, scraping behind Cael’s thoughts like rats in a wall.

He tried to think of nothing.

Riven’s mind brushed his anyway, quick images: dock dusk, chalk dust, Mara’s coin rhythm, the monument’s fused hands.

Cael jerked his head sideways like he could shake the images loose.

“Sorry,” Riven said automatically.

“You didn’t do it on purpose,” Cael said, because it was true and because it was the kind of sentence that kept rooms from exploding.

“Did you—” Riven swallowed. “Did you hear that?”

“Saw it,” Cael said, then corrected, because lying was pointless now. “Felt it. Same thing.”

Riven’s face went greyer. “That’s not possible.”

“It’s happening.”

“That’s worse.”

Cael couldn’t argue.

He stood because motion sometimes fixed confusion, and because sitting on the stone felt too much like agreeing to stay pinned in the circle forever.

His knees almost betrayed him. Riven stood a half beat later without being told, body snapping upright like someone had pulled the same string.

They stared at each other.

Cael lifted his hand slowly, palm toward Riven, not touching, a test.

Riven lifted his to match without looking down.

Their palms aligned an inch apart, heat between them like a stove left on.

Cael lowered his hand. Riven lowered his.

Same speed.

Same arc.

Cael’s mouth went bitter. “Christ.”

“Yeah,” Riven whispered.

Cael turned deliberately the other way, toward the trail lip, toward the idea of down, toward anything that wasn’t the empty center and its attention.

“Pack,” he said.

“Pack,” Riven echoed, then winced. “I didn’t mean to echo.”

“I know.”

They moved toward their packs on the rim, and the movement should’ve been ordinary: bend, lift, strap, check.

Instead Cael reached for the strap at the exact instant Riven reached for his own, elbows brushing, a near-collision that didn’t happen because both stopped at the same inch, like they’d rehearsed it.

Cael felt Riven’s surprise as his own.

He steadied himself on a knee that wasn’t supposed to be shaking.

“This is bad,” Riven said.

“Maybe,” Cael said.

“Maybe?”

Cael swallowed. His mind raced for structure, for a frame that could hold what was happening without letting it spill into panic.

The pamphlet had called the bond a bridge.

Bridges had rules. Weight limits. Centerlines.

If something crossed that wasn’t supposed to, you didn’t stand under it and admire the problem. You checked joints. You braced. You decided what could bear load.

Cael’s thoughts lined up too fast, too clean, and halfway through he realized Riven was staring at him with his mouth slightly open.

“You felt that whole thing,” Riven said.

Cael’s neck heated. “I was thinking.”

“I heard it,” Riven said, almost wonder, almost horror. “Like… like you were explaining it to me without speaking.”

Cael wanted to deny it.

Denial felt like a lie that would cost more than truth.

“Okay,” Cael said, voice rough. “So we’re… aligned.”

“That’s not a word I want right now.”

“It’s the word we’ve got.”

Riven laughed again, broken. “You’re glad.”

“I’m not glad,” Cael said automatically, then stopped, because the reflex lie wouldn’t hold.

He was scared, yes.

Under the fear, something else sat bright and wrong: exhilaration, the kind he’d felt on a good roofline when everything measured true, when the world clicked into place.

He felt Riven recoil from that feeling like it burned.

“Sorry,” Cael said, too late.

“You’re not sorry,” Riven said, and Cael felt the twist of Riven’s anger and his own shame at the same time, layered until he couldn’t separate them.

He pressed his palm flat against his thigh until the muscle ached, grounding by pain.

“We walk,” Cael said.

“Where.”

“Down,” Cael said. “Not because running fixes it. Because we can’t sleep on consecrated stone like we’re camping in a kitchen.”

“Is that what this is,” Riven muttered. “Consecrated.”

“It’s what the pamphlet called it,” Cael said. “If you’ve got a better label, say it.”

Riven didn’t.

They shouldered packs. Cael led because someone had to, and every time he chose a foot placement he felt Riven’s body adjust behind him before gravel shifted, before wind gusted, like Riven’s nerves had been strung through Cael’s own.

It should’ve been clumsy.

It wasn’t.

They descended the first steep stretch faster than they should have, too synchronized, too clean, and Cael hated how efficient it felt, how much like proof.

“Stop,” Riven said suddenly.

Cael stopped on the same foot.

Riven swayed, caught himself on a knob of rock, breathing hard. “We have to stop doing that.”

“Doing what.”

“Moving like one person.”

Cael’s jaw tightened. “Then you move different on purpose.”

“I tried,” Riven said, voice rising. “I tried and my body didn’t listen.”

Cael felt the spike of Riven’s panic try to climb into his throat.

He turned, slower than his instincts wanted, and set a hand on Riven’s shoulder, heavy, deliberate.

“Feel that,” Cael said.

“What.”

“My hand,” Cael said. “Skin. Pressure. Mine.”

Riven blinked fast. “It’s your hand.”

“Keep saying true things,” Cael said. “Even if they’re small.”

Riven nodded, shaky. “Your hand.”

“Your shoulder.”

“My shoulder.”

“My breath.”

“Your breath,” Riven corrected, then made an awful sound halfway between laugh and sob. “This is insane.”

“Yeah,” Cael said.

They stood until Riven’s shoulder stopped trembling under Cael’s palm, or until Cael’s hand went numb from cold, or both, time slippery.

When they moved again, it was slower, uglier, on purpose.

It still wasn’t natural.

Every few steps one of them would hesitate and the other would hesitate in the same beat, a stutter-stop that looked like comedy from outside and felt like drowning inside.

Halfway down the bench trail, Cael tried to think a nonsense word on purpose, loud in his head: cabbage.

Riven snorted, miserable. “Why cabbage.”

“To see if you’d hear.”

“I heard,” Riven said. “God. I heard.”

Cael exhaled, fogged the air, watched it dissolve too fast.

“This is the curse,” Riven whispered, like naming it might shrink it.

Cael flinched at the word curse, old pamphlet glamour and old songs, heroes never forgotten, ribs and all.

Under the flinch, another thought rose, unwanted and clear: if this was a curse, it was also a seam. A joint. A way two men could hold what broke.

Cael didn’t say that out loud.

Riven heard it anyway.

Riven’s eyes met his, wet, furious. “Don’t romanticize it.”

“I wasn’t—”

“You were,” Riven said. “I felt it.”

Cael looked away first, jaw aching from clenching. “Fine. I won’t.”

“You will,” Riven said. “You’ll call it proof.”

Cael couldn’t stop the next thought either, clean as a plumb line: it is proof.

Riven made a sound like he’d been struck.

Cael forced himself to speak aloud, slow, separate words, as if spoken language could rebuild a wall inner silence used to be.

“I’m not saying it’s good,” Cael said. “I’m saying it’s… consistent.”

“Consistent,” Riven repeated, hollow.

“We asked for something real,” Cael said. “This is real.”

“We asked to matter,” Riven snapped. “Not to be… hollowed out together.”

The fear under that sentence hit Cael so hard his vision narrowed.

He leaned forward, hands on his knees, and counted stone colors until he trusted his voice again.

“We’re not hollow,” he said.

“You don’t know that.”

“I know we’re standing,” Cael said. “I know we’re talking. I know you heard cabbage.”

Riven stared at him, then laughed, ugly again, softer. “Fuck you.”

“Okay,” Cael said.

They kept walking.

By the time they reached a lower shelf where pines returned and wind sounded almost normal, the sun had begun to fail behind cloud, grey on grey.

Cael picked a camp spot without discussing it, and Riven dropped his pack where Cael pointed, too tired to argue, and that should’ve felt like victory.

It felt like habit.

Like something training them.

Cael built a fire with hands that steadied too quickly, each motion arriving before doubt did.

Riven watched him, arms wrapped around his own ribs, and Cael felt the ache there as if it were his own spine.

“Don’t look at me like that,” Cael said, quieter than he meant.

“Like what.”

“Like I’m happy.”

Riven swallowed. “Are you.”

Cael sat back on his heels and watched flame catch, hungry and ordinary.

“I’m scared,” he said, because it was true and because truth was the only currency left that didn’t multiply in transit.

“But,” Riven said, hearing the rest before Cael could decide whether to say it.

Cael met his eyes. “But I’m not wrong,” he said, and heard how cruel it sounded, and said it anyway because pulling punches felt like another kind of lie now. “We asked. Something answered. We’re still here. That means we chose something that… fits.”

Riven looked away, jaw tight. “Fits.”

“For now,” Cael added, because preservation mattered, because leaving Riven without an exit in the sentence was its own violence.

“For now,” Riven echoed, bitterly obedient.

They ate because bodies demanded it. The food tasted like ash and salt, same as the seep.

When they lay down, back to back for warmth like they always had, Cael felt Riven’s heartbeat through his coat like a second pulse pressed to his spine.

He stared into the dark until his eyes burned.

Somewhere inside him, Riven’s thoughts still moved, restless, angry, grieving something neither of them could name yet.

Cael didn’t try to stop the noise.

He listened the way he listened to a roof in wind: for the first crack, for the telltale shift, for the moment before failure.

Nothing failed.

That was almost worse.

“Cael,” Riven whispered at last, voice small.

“Yeah.”

“I’m still me,” Riven said, like asking permission.

Cael swallowed. “Me too.”

He felt Riven’s doubt curl through him anyway, hot and cold.

He closed his eyes and pretended sleep until sleep stopped being pretend.

In the last moment before he dropped under, one clear thought rose, not cabbage, not proof, not curse:

We hold.

And Riven, half-gone already in exhaustion, answered without speaking, heard anyway:

Yeah.

The fire popped once, loud in the false quiet.

Cael didn’t know anymore whose fear kept him awake, and stopped trying to separate it.

He lay still until morning, two hearts, one rhythm, one seam.
