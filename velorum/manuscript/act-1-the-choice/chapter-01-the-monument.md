# Chapter 01 - The Monument

Greyhaven clung to a crook of coast where the hills slumped into the reach—grey water hammering sand where it ran shallow near the harbor mouth, sky the color of cooled ash, and the town itself a tight clutter of timber lanes, tarred rope, stone foundations patched with whatever wouldn’t wash away in the next bad winter. Fishermen hauled herring up from the docks in splintering crates; highland shepherds drove sheep down pastures that looked greener from a distance than they felt underfoot; travelers paid Mara for a bed and a bowl of stew and counted their coins twice before sleep, in case what they’d tucked under the pillow wasn’t still there come morning. Smoke rose from chimneys too low for pride and too honest for beauty. That was Greyhaven in practical terms: a place that survived by rope, salt, wool, and gossip, and called the mix “peace” because nobody wanted to admit how little slack there was.

From a ridge above the harbor road the monument rose—pale stone against weather, visible from half the waterfront on a clear day like a snag in the horizon. Pilgrims climbed when they could afford decent boots; couples went up after weddings because someone’s grandmother said it brought luck; kids dared each other toward dusk until their fathers caught them and dragged them home.

Riven climbed it the way he climbed anything that gave him height—lean through the chest and hips, sleeves shoved up past rope-burned wrists, dark hair sticking out where other men tucked theirs under caps. People called his eyes sharp; he thought they were just tired of pretending not to notice. He went when the market noise pressed too tight against his ribs—when the lanes stacked close enough that every shoulder brushed wool he hadn’t chosen.

The path was loose rock and stubborn grass, cut into the slope in old switchbacks too narrow for carts. Every step spilled grit down behind him—a thin rattle he hated because it followed him like gossip. Above, two carved figures leaned into the gust off the water—the same air that tasted of brine and cold iron. Down in the market square, barely a smear from this height, vendors called out onions and salted fish and the town’s favorite three phrases—heroes, curse, never forgotten—until the words went dull as a coin rubbed smooth.

He leaned hard into wind straight in off the water—damp, relentless, sharp enough to feel like a shove. Salt dried at the corners of his mouth; cold slid under his collar and bit the hollow of his throat until he swallowed and tasted metal. His jaw ached; his knuckles went red gripping nothing—that was real, this high up, where it didn’t give a damn if he still told himself he was meant for more than rent chalk and crate splinters.

Grit under his boots; Greyhaven lay small below the ridge—roofs and alleys and the dull glint of tidal mud at low water. That should have been enough to look at. It wasn’t—his thoughts drifted to the inn anyway: Mara behind the scarred counter, coin slid across wood under lamp smoke. After that came the ending he kept trying not to picture: him folding his last coin flat on Mara’s counter anyway, rent squared, boots scraped clean by the door—and up on the cliff, only his mother’s gravestone, rain eating the letters while nobody chiseled anything for him.

That hurt worse than cold.

From up here the statue looked older than the town’s brag—older than he could tell from the street vendors and their cheap printed stories. Below, New Greyhaven patched itself to the hillside in fresh timber and borrowed time: goat paths, stakes driven where the soil held, smoke that smeared the air and then thinned into nothing. Up here, Old Greyhaven had only this: two weathered people on a stone base, leaning into each other in the pose every cheap booklet called *the last hold*—two bodies bracing each other because they were about to go down together. One figure still had a jaw cut sharp by a chisel that had worked slow and mean. The other had worn until the face was nearly gone—just a suggestion of cheek, a shadow where a mouth should be. Their hands had fused into a single blurred knot where neither carver nor weather had left a clean answer—who had hold of whom when they went down.

Riven walked the base edge the way he always did, boot soles scuffing grit in a circuit he didn’t need to measure anymore, eyes on the seam where the pale rock—if it was marble; it looked too worn to be precious—met the raw hillside underneath as if two centuries had been jammed into one bad joint. Grey-green crust grew in the folds of carved cloth like mold on old bread. Birds had left pale shit streaks down the heroic chests. Where the stone base cracked, last year’s leaves had piled; beneath them, darker stains like old smoke, or rain that had pooled in the crack and never drained right. Finger-smudged wax and coin-rub brightened the rim where pilgrims stopped themselves from touching—Riven could see the hesitation polished into the lip, shiny and shared. In the crack: dead pine needles, black against new green, and a strip of rotted ribbon wedged deep like an offering nobody would claim.

He stopped in front of the fused hands. The pamphlet he’d stolen from the inn left smudges on his thumb—cheap ink that flaked once his thumb went slick. It said the bond had turned the first pair into a living bridge, that the god’s power had flowed through them like water through a clean ditch, that the land “chose alignment” when it needed it most. The words made a neat picture until he tried to pin it down: a ditch flooding anyway, banks crumbling, somebody’s boot stuck in sucking mud. The pamphlet never said whose field got eaten when the ditch broke.

Riven reached, then didn’t touch. Even now, with neither of them sworn to anything but their own hunger, his fingertips ached with the almost-touch—cold radiating off the fused knuckles, a pull that felt like leaning toward a stove he hadn’t earned yet.

“That’s gross,” Cael said behind him.

Riven knew that voice—low, level, built to carry across wind and quarreling drunks—and the body that usually came with it: Cael filled a doorway without trying, broad-shouldered and taller by a hand, dark hair clipped practical, skin weathered pale where Riven burned. Everything about him looked squared and decided, like a carpenter had measured twice and cut once.

Riven didn’t jump. Disappointment tightened in his ribs anyway—because he hadn’t heard Cael coming, and because some childish part of him still wanted wind and gulls long enough for a loose flake of stone to ring off near his boot—a real chip fallen from the carving, not another hour wasted on his temper.

“Everything’s gross,” Riven said without turning around. “Whole town’s rotting—it’s all shit in slow motion. That doesn’t make us clean.”

Cael stepped in beside him at that steady pace strangers always read as calm. Riven knew better—knew the difference between calm and held still. Cael’s eyes tracked movement the way a carpenter tracked grain—looking for the grain’s weak line before the board split. When Cael stopped beside him, less wind hit Riven’s face—Cael’s bulk blocked part of it.

“You’ve been up here since dawn,” Cael said.

“No.”

“You smell like dawn.”

“I smell like cheap soap and bad tea.” Riven scrubbed grit off his palms on his trousers. “If that’s dawn, dawn’s thin enough to choke on.”

From where Riven stood, Cael studied the statue like evidence: straight on, shoulders squared, wind in his eyes and no flinching. His eyes moved over what lettering survived—the last lines half-eaten—and didn’t hurry. He treated the monument like orders carved where nobody got to argue—and from Riven’s angle the stone was law that didn’t bend for sweet talk.

“You’re sulking,” Cael said.

“I’m studying.”

“You’re sulking while you study. Impressive.”

Riven almost smiled. The smile died when he looked back at the fused hands. Two heroes. One knot. In the pamphlet’s woodcut they leaned into each other glorious, swords up, mouths open like singers. In the cramped line under the ink, somebody had admitted the bodies were found fused—hands locked, ribs touching—no air left between them at the end.

“I keep trying to decide which one is which,” Riven said.

Cael didn’t ask *which what*. He’d done this before with Riven—let a half-sentence stand and answered it anyway, like he’d already heard the rest of Riven’s sentence in his own head.

“In the stories,” Riven went on, “you can swap them and nothing changes. The Anchor and the Flame. Saint-and-flame, anchor-and-spark—pick your cheap pair. Anyone selling the printed legend wants the romance to outsell whichever poor bastards actually died with their teeth locked.” He flicked dust off the stone base and watched it skitter into the notch. “If the tale’s enough, nobody owes two dead men a scrap of marble.”

Cael stepped closer to the fused hands but didn’t touch them either. Riven noticed the restraint like a bruise.

“Somebody still lived it,” Cael said.

“Tell that to the whole damn town.”

“I’m telling you.”

Riven swallowed and let his eyes travel out past the monument’s ridge—easier than holding Cael’s stare after *I’m telling you*—to where the reach opened wide, grey water rolling over pale sand where it stayed shallow long enough to wade, gulls wheeling low, skimming the shallows. The harbor below was a scribble of masts and nets and men shouting distances at each other; beyond it the far line flattened into a grey seam where sky met water. Downhill, sheep paths crossed the flank in crooked lines. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin threads that tore apart before they climbed—nothing that hooked Riven’s eye. Fine by everyone else. People called that peace. Riven heard it as *eyes off—nobody counting who limped home*.

“You’re afraid nobody will remember you,” Cael said.

The straight words stung. Shame climbed his throat first, then anger—then the pull to joke his way out and fail.

“That’s not—” He stopped. There was no honest denial available. Fear pressed under his ribs like a stone too heavy to cough up. “God damn,” he muttered. “I’m afraid I’ll die with my boots by the fucking door and nobody able to swear what I did besides eat and sleep and pay what I owed,” he said finally. “I’m afraid I’ll get good at lasting and nobody will know why my hands shook when I said I was fine.”

Cael nodded once. No pity act—heard him, didn’t look away, didn’t pretend otherwise.

“You think being remembered fixes that?” Cael asked.

“Being forgotten guarantees it. Every time.”

Below them, past the slope of brown grass and outcroppings of darker stone, a dog barked twice and stopped as if someone had hushed it. A gust shoved salt, wet wool, iron at him—the inn’s laundry line working loose somewhere out of sight.

Riven watched Cael’s gaze rake the roofs of the town they both knew by memory—the same sweep as always, like he could spot every loose pin in Greyhaven before it squeaked. “Most people aren’t remembered,” Cael said. “Most people feed children and mend fences until they stop. That isn’t failure.”

“It is if you needed more.”

“Needed—or wanted?”

Riven laughed once, sharp. “Goddamnit, Cael.”

“What?”

“You sound like my mother.”

Riven muttered “Christ” and scraped his sleeve across his lip.

“I sound like somebody who doesn’t want you bleeding where the crowd can bless it and toss coins.” Cael tipped his chin toward the statue. “That rock isn’t a sermon on forgotten souls. It’s proof somebody once split stone for two men you could’ve passed in the lane—see the chisel strikes. They’re still there.”

“Something,” Riven echoed. “Not someone.”

“Both.”

“Easy for you to say.”

Cael’s jaw tightened—not anger, restraint. “Why?”

Riven had a petty tally—things he hated knowing he’d catalogued: how Cael squared his shoulders before he spoke; how Mara broke off mid-count when Cael crossed her threshold; how the chained dog outside the inn quit growling once it picked out his shape against the light.

What Riven said was: “Because you’re not half as fucking scared as I am.”

Cael stayed quiet until noise off the water filled the gap between them. Then: “That’s bullshit.”

Riven blinked. “Say that louder. I don’t think the sheep heard.”

“You think I’m not scared?” Cael’s voice stayed level—level enough that Riven heard every word twice. “I’m terrified. I’m terrified of hearing timber crack and knowing my hands moved too slow. I’m terrified of being the reason somebody dies tired.” His eyes flicked to the fused hands again. “I’m terrified people only keep me close because I’m easy.” Cael’s breath shook once. “Fuck.”

The admission landed too raw on Riven’s skin. Heat shot behind his sternum—too hot, too fast for Cael’s confession alone—then faded; his mouth tasted copper. He looked away fast at the reach until the horizon steadied.

“What the hell am I supposed to do with that?” he muttered.

“Nothing.” Cael exhaled through his nose. “You asked.”

Footsteps scraped on the loose-rock path—lighter than Cael’s, quicker. Bran, one of the innkeeper’s messenger boys, came up over the hill—all knees and elbows, hay-colored hair sticking out around a knit cap too thin for the season, freckles and a fresh scab on his jaw where he’d probably lost an argument with a crate. His boots were two sizes too big; his ears were red from cold; his breath puffed white. From below, the square’s noise had thickened—somebody shouting prices, somebody laughing too loud—noise climbing the hill toward them.

“Oi,” Bran called, overly cheerful in the way kids learned when adults paid them for cheer. “Mara says if you two are skipping chores again she’s charging your mothers.”

*You two.* Same shorthand Mara used; Bran didn’t mean anything by it. Riven still heard himself sorted next to Cael whether he liked it—two names, one chore—and let it go. Not worth picking at with a messenger boy.

“Tell Mara my mother’s dead,” Riven said without thinking.

Bran faltered a half-step. “Uh.”

“Don’t tell Mara that,” Cael said mildly. “Tell Mara we’ll sweep the yard after.”

“What he said,” Riven added, softer. “Sorry. Tell her… tell her we’ll sweep.”

Bran nodded like he’d been handed broken glass and didn’t want to bleed. He caught his breath and added, “Also that singer’s back. The one with the songs. They’re doing the thing in the square.”

Riven’s stomach tightened at the thought of the square packed tight between timber walls—cheap paper passed hand to hand, ink too bright, kids with their mouths open while somebody sold them a life in four verses and called it history.

“Fucking hell,” Riven breathed.

“Probably not,” Cael said. “Probably just men.”

Bran laughed, uncertain. Cael thanked him with a coin—pulled from somewhere Riven couldn’t spot, same as always, same quiet readiness that gnawed at Riven when he noticed it—and the boy vanished back down the rocky trail in a scatter of loose stone.

Stillness dropped between them again—heavier this time. Riven could already see the ribbon on the hat, the bowl at his feet filling with coins, the tavern drunk trying the chorus—same story until the words felt like his own name misspelled.

“They’ll sing about the bond,” Riven said. “They’ll make it sound beautiful.”

“It was beautiful,” Cael said.

Riven stared at him. “Beautiful my ass. You weren’t there.”

“I mean the picture it makes.” Cael’s gaze held steady on the carved knot. “Two people holding a line until the line stopped moving.”

“That’s not a picture—that’s rope burn and somebody else’s hand on your wrist, and it hurts down to the bone.”

“Could be.” Cael didn’t bite back. That unsettled Riven more than disagreement would have. “Either way,” Cael went on, “that bond story’s more grip than two dry palms and cheap paper.”

Riven wanted to spit. His mouth was too dry.

“Say it,” he pushed.

“Say what?”

“What you’re really thinking when you look at them.” Riven jerked his chin at the statue. “Not the poetry. The plan.”

Cael let the pause hang. When he spoke, his voice came out bare and blunt, thin against the gusts. “Greyhaven shouldn’t rattle like a door missing its pin. The north road eats cart wheels after rain. Last spring wasn’t weather that threw two planks off the bridge.” He looked at Riven. “Those pages aren’t only ink—the old tale says people begged once and the ground answered. Two men could walk out with shaking hands and find out if that answer still comes—or if this is rock and a good lie.”

Riven’s throat tightened. “Walk where? What the fuck does that mean?”

Cael tipped his chin past the water, toward the pale cut where the hills rose bare. “The highlands. Broken ground—until this stops being a tune Mara hums while she counts coins.”

They stood there while the sky thickened toward weather and the reach turned the color of old steel. Below them—too far to pick out faces, close enough to hear temper—a fiddle scraped its first thin note, then another; someone whooped; a drum found the shallow beat street singers always used because it made the crowd lean in before they passed the hat. Noise crawled up the cliff until the hair on Riven’s neck lifted.

“They’re going to sing that romantic shit about dying together,” Riven said.

“I know.”

“It’s romantic until you picture ribs touching—no gap left for air.”

Cael’s mouth tightened. “Then don’t make it pretty in your head. Learn what it costs.”

“And then what—choose it anyway?” Riven asked, hearing how ugly the question sounded once it left him.

“Fuck.”

Cael didn’t answer immediately. That was answer enough.

Riven stepped back from the carved knot. His boots scraped stone.

Another cheer lifted from the lanes, thin at this height; he breathed and got grit in his teeth.

Riven feared dying nameless—fish still vanishing off the crates below, nobody left to swear whose hands had stacked them; his name only in chalk where Mara tallied rent. He feared dying loud—printed, sung, passed mouth to mouth until his own name sounded borrowed and he couldn’t swear whose throat made the cheer when he joined in.

Carved stone bulked above them, faces worn smooth; below, Greyhaven kept shouting anyway. The chorus below thickened—voices stumbling on the high notes, the crowd trying to catch the singer’s rhythm and failing in the same places every time—and somebody laughed too loud on purpose. Smoke climbed from cookfires; onions fried in fat; iron rang from a smith’s hammer down near the docks, and those smells mixed on the wind until Riven could taste the town without looking.

His foot shifted toward the trail before he chose it.

When Cael turned downslope, Riven followed—no bag, no oath yet—because stillness while the crowd below sang felt like letting the singer’s verses finish him before his boots had earned another ridge.

Boots by the god damn door again, he thought. Nobody to swear he’d ever moved.
