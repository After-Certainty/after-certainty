# TONE AND STYLE

- Grounded and emotionally precise; big stakes, **plainspoken** delivery  
- **Contemporary English** in narration and dialogue—contractions, varied sentence length, idioms that fit the invented world without sounding medieval cosplay  
- Clean prose; **say what happens before you compare it to something else.** Similes and analogies are fine when one image does real work—especially in dialogue, pamphlet critique, or a single ritual beat—not when they stack for atmosphere. Default narration: **literal sense first** (who moved, what hit, what it cost), then at most **one** earned figure if the POV would think that way—see **Plain sense default** and **No ornate varnish** under Readability.  
- Emotion shown through behavior, silence, physical detail, and dialogue  
- Dialogue appears **often and at length** in every chapter—conversations carry conflict, revelation, and relationship; subtext-driven, **natural**, not sparse for effect  
- **Reader hook:** the story is set in a mythical land with oath and Velorum, but the **minds and mouths read contemporary**—so the frame feels familiar even when the stakes are not.  
- Plot and character pressure should read as **overdetermined** (choices foreclose cleanly) without relying on lofty abstract narration to say so.

## Readability *(~7th grade; profanity retained)*

- **Target:** prose that **most adult readers** can move through quickly—roughly **U.S. 7th-grade** readability (short words where they work, familiar syntax, concrete images). This is **not** “simple topics”—the ideas stay adult; the **delivery** stays clear.  
- **Dense language:** avoid rare or literary vocabulary when a common word does the same job; cut **stacked abstractions** and **noun piles** in favor of people doing things on the page. One clear picture beats three clever synonyms—prefer **direct verbs and concrete nouns** over figurative stacking (*postcard perfection*, *noise crawled along his ribs*) unless the line is deliberately a character’s spoken judgment.  
- **Profanity:** swearing is **not** the problem for readability—keep the **heavy-profanity** character and scene rules elsewhere in this file. Readability passes focus on **sentence shape** and **word choice**, not toning down voice.  
- **Sentences:** fix **true run-ons** (too many chained clauses, unclear referents, grammar that needs three reads). **Long sentences are fine** when each unit is deliberate (parallel rhythm, clear subjects), not when the line is one accidental bag of “and… and… which…” Use **em dashes rarely** in narration and dialogue; see **Em dashes** below.  
- **Paragraphs:** length is **not** the enemy; **opaque wording** is. Prefer **longer, flowing paragraphs** where the voice spec already calls for woven narration—do not break paragraphs just to “simplify” if the fix is really vocabulary and sentence boundaries.

### Em dashes *(manuscript: use sparingly)*

Stacked em dashes read like template prose and tire the eye. Default to **commas, semicolons, colons, parentheses, or a clean split into two sentences** for appositives, elaboration, and turns in thought.

**Reserve em dashes mainly for:**

- **Broken or interrupted dialogue** (*“That’s not—”* before a stop or switch of speaker)  
- **Rare** sharp asides where no other punctuation reads right without rewriting the whole line  

**Do not** lean on em dashes as the primary way to tack one clause onto another across the chapter.

**Revision cue:** Search the chapter for em dashes; if the count feels high on a skim, convert most. **`chapter-01-the-monument.md`** after its punctuation pass is the reference density (essentially **one** em dash kept for interrupted speech).

**Shape reference:** until the manuscript has a settled “gold” chapter, use **Calibration samples** below (same section of this file) and **`chapter-01-the-monument.md` (opening)** for place-first woven narration—compare new work to **density and flow**, not exact sentences.

### Plain sense default *(matches Chapter 1 revision standard)*

- **Narration:** State **cause, body, place** in plain terms (*wind pushed hard*, *gravel slid*, *rent came due*). When you use a simile or analogy, the reader should still know **what literally occurred** if they strip the comparison away.  
- **Reserve extended metaphor** for beats where it is the subject—cheap legend copy tearing under scrutiny, oath/ground language at the summit, a character performing rhetoric—not for ordinary scene-setting.  
- **Revision cue:** If a spouse or cold reader has to pause over *what happened*, swap the figure for **sequence + sensation** (*noise and stink piled into a headache*) or **one** sharp comparison tied to an object already on the page.

### Plain dialogue *(Chapter 1 “second pass” standard)*

Speech should read **sayable at stress**—what a tired person would actually spit out—not a layered literary caption.

- **State stakes before ornament.** Lead with damage, fear, refusal, demand (*hurt in public so the square can watch*, *Say the plan*, *Know what it costs*) before optional metaphor. Prefer **direct insult, threat, concession** over riddles unless the speaker is dodging on purpose.  
- **Trim stacked metaphors in quoted lines.** One image per beat unless the voice is a performer (tavern singer, magistrate, ritual). When **Cael** names craft or risk, favor **timber, nails, joints, wheel, plank** over mixed conceits; when **Riven** fires back, favor **blunt consequence** over thesis-shaped comparisons.  
- **Belief and lore stay in mouths.** Phrasing like *the ground answered* belongs to **quoted tale**, **pamphlet**, or a character stating what people claim—not to narrator omniscience (see **Clear agency → Lore and theology**).  
- **Keep mythic oath language where in-world:** Velorum / bond / alignment terms stay; do not replace them with clever secular metaphors that obscure the mechanic.  
- **Profanity stays on polish:** readability and “plain dialogue” passes **do not** replace approved swearing with softer synonyms (*darn*, *heck*, toned-down substitutes) unless a specific scene or audience constraint is documented elsewhere. Flatten **metaphor and thesis-shaped lines**, not **heat**. See **Voice guardrails** for approved registers.  
- **Exemplar:** **`chapter-01-the-monument.md`** argument blocks on the ridge (Riven/Cael): sharp exchanges, plain nouns, theology only when attributed; revision target for **density of straight talk per page** of dialogue.

### Camera-observable narration *(sight-line standard; calibrated on Chapter 1)*

Use this for **default narration** (not quoted song, pamphlet, or deliberate performance dialogue).

- **Sight line first:** describe **what would read on footage**—where things are, what they’re made of, how bodies and objects move—before thematic gloss. Prefer **Greyhaven filled the bend**, **noise came up thin**, **stone felt cold** over **town sat**, **truth peeled**, **silence waited** unless the POV frames those as *felt like* judgments.  
- **No narrator-as-oracle:** swap abstract nouns (*peace*, *beauty*, *meaning*, *romance*, *failure*) for **visible facts** (*patched roofs*, *blood left off the pamphlet*, *no name on stone*) unless a **character** says the abstract word in dialogue.  
- **Interior / memory:** when thought drifts off present action, **mark the cut** with a plain trigger (*he saw the counter instead of the roofs*, *the line blurred*) so the jump reads as **attention shift**, not omniscient slide.  
- **Monuments and objects:** first establishing shot states **subject of the carving** (how many figures, posture, hands, base) in **camera-flat** wording—avoid figurative merge metaphors in narration (*cut two men into one piece*) unless dialogue performs them.  
- **Exemplar:** **`velorum/manuscript/act-1-the-choice/chapter-01-the-monument.md`** after camera + plainness passes—compare new chapters to **literal subjects**, **physical transitions**, and **density of figurative fog** (aim: low in narration, character-appropriate in dialogue).

### Tightening *(Pass F; line-level)*

After sense, agency, and plain dialogue are in place, **tighten** so every line carries a distinct job—no duplicate beats, no second pass at the same image, no explanation tacked on after the body has already shown it.

- **One strong image per beat** when the same moment is described twice in different words, merge or cut to the keeper.  
- **Anchor moment and action before sensation stacks** where a cold read would ask *what moved?* first: who did what, then (if still needed) how it landed in the body.  
- **Cut echo** (same fear, same object) unless the second pass **adds** new information or pressure.  
- **Do not** trade tight for staccato: when you cut, **re-braid** into the same **paragraph fabric** the voice spec already calls for (see **Paragraph shape**).  
- **Exemplar (post-polish density):** **`velorum/manuscript/act-1-the-choice/chapter-01-the-monument.md`** after a full pass A–E and **Pass F**—compare new chapters to its **tightness of observation** and **absence of double-tap description**, not to its exact word count.

**Revision cue:** run after concreteness and place passes when a scene *works* but still **sponges**; see **[chapter-edit-pass.md](./chapter-edit-pass.md)** → **Pass F** and final **Pass G** skim.

### Concrete language; kill office creep

- **Bodies and dirt first:** anchor emotion and power in **what readers can see**—hands, jaw, rope, dust, rope burn, eye-line—before you name the idea. Prefer one physical beat over three abstract nouns (“cost,” “weight,” “invitation”) standing in for drama.  
- **Idiomatic, not office-shaped:** when you cut workplace-adjacent wording (*performance* piled as abstract jargon, *leverage*, *optics*), replace it with **what people actually say**—*performing*, *putting on a show*, *acting like*, *playing brave*. Don’t swap a natural phrase for a stiff euphemism that merely **avoids** office vocabulary but reads like a policy memo (*facilitating*, *optimizing*) or clinical stand-ins where spoken English already has a tool (*performing injury*, not *staging injury*—unless **dialogue** is deliberately theatrical). Institutional voices (magistrate, clerks) may sound office-like **on purpose**; default narration and ordinary speech should not.  
- **No modern workplace camouflage** in narration or ordinary dialogue: avoid *leverage, optics, bandwidth, circle back, takeaway, unpack, stakeholder, pivot, granular, onboarding, actionable, synergize*. If you mean **performing for an audience**, say **show / act / play brave**—not **performance** as abstract noun-stacking in narration; **performing** as a verb in dialogue (*performing injury*, *performing calm*) stays—it’s idiomatic.  
- **“Job description,” “role,” “reporting line”:** use only if you want **intentional** cold comedy or an institutional voice (e.g. merchant power). Default narration should sound like **road talk**, not HR.  
- **Velorum terms stay:** *alignment, sync, correction, bond, oath* are **in-world**—keep them; do **not** replace them with corporate *alignment* metaphors from another sense. When you mean ordinary English “they matched,” say **matched / snapped together / forced the same step** if you’re avoiding oath jargon in a given sentence.  
- **Abstractions to pressure-test:** *invitation, debt, theater (as metaphor), choreograph, translation (unless dialogue names language outright)*—rewrite until **someone wants something on the page** or **something hits skin**.

### Contemporary lexicon *(secondary world, not historical costume)*

The story does **not** take place in modern Earth, but **default narration and ordinary dialogue still use plain, contemporary English**—the vocabulary readers already live in. Myth and invented place stay mythical; **word choice** stays legible.

- **Prefer modern clarity over period flavor.** Swap costume-default nouns (*hawkers*, *chapbooks*, stock *verily* cadence) for **straight labels** unless a beat truly needs the older word (*street vendors*, *cheap printed stories*, *pamphlets*, *ledger*)—same objects, fewer decoder rings.  
- **What stays “other”:** **Velorum / oath grammar** (*alignment*, *bond*, *correction*, *sync*, named places, in-world oaths)—those are **setting vocabulary**, not museum English. Do **not** replace them with corporate or modern-tech metaphors (see office creep above).  
- **When period words earn their keep:** quoted ritual, a character **performing** pomposity, or a term taught **in-scene** before it carries weight—otherwise choose the word that fits **today’s ear**.  
- **Revision cue:** if a reader could mistake the word for homework (*chapbook*, *hawker*, *cordwainer*), try the **specific modern thing** (*cheap booklet*, *vendor*, *cobbler*) without flattening voice.

#### No museum-catalog or opaque jargon *(default narration)*

Default narration should sound like **someone telling you what they saw from the dirt and weather**, not like a **gallery wall label**, exhibit placard, or textbook glossary.

- **Avoid rare “display case” nouns** when an ordinary label carries the image—*stone base*, *the block under the carved figures*, *steps at the bottom of the statue*—not *plinth*, *socle*, *effigy*, unless a character **performing** education or power **says** the word on purpose.  
- **Borrowed specialty registers** (fine-art history, formal architecture, heraldry, academic geography) belong in **quoted speech**, **in-scene teaching**, or an **institutional / expert mouth**—not in omniscient scenery. Prefer **what the POV would say out loud** (*loose slate*, *bad joins*, *ridge wind*) over terms most readers pause to decode.  
- **Technical jargon** (*shoal*, *cordage*, *parapet*) earns a line only when the beat **needs** precision or the same sentence **defines** it; otherwise use sight-level naming (see **Place-first narration**).  
- **Revision cue:** if only a docent or a specialist would reach for the word first, swap it unless you’ve planted an **in-world** reason for that voice.

### Place-first narration & plain diction *(Velorum manuscript standard)*

Used to keep **setting** readable on a **first pass**—especially for readers who bump on specialist vocabulary or slogan-shaped sentences.

- **Ground “where” before “why.”** When you introduce or revisit a town, road, harbor, or wild stretch, stack **things people touch and trade**: docks, crates, rope, chimneys, coin, mud, livestock—before mood-only summary. Prefer **sand where it ran shallow near the harbor mouth** over nautical jargon that stops readers cold (*shoal*, unless context teaches it in-scene).
- **Social logic in bodies, not mottoes.** Avoid standalone **aphorisms** (*the town teaches X*) unless you tether them to **one visible consequence**—someone counting coins **because** theft happens, a wall that’s thin, a bowl that costs what it costs.
- **One-sentence practical verdict (optional):** After a dense place pass, you may land a short **summarizing judgment** grounded in survival terms (**That was Greyhaven in practical terms: rope, salt, wool, gossip…**) so readers can hold the whole sketch—then move on.
- **Exemplar chapter:** Until the project names a replacement “gold” chapter, treat **`velorum/manuscript/act-1-the-choice/chapter-01-the-monument.md`**—**opening paragraphs** for place-first narration, **ridge dialogue** for **Plain dialogue**, and **Pass A3** cleanup (no town-as-oracle, attributed lore)—as the default reference for voice alongside calibration samples elsewhere in this file.

### Spatial and surface clarity *(paths, wind, sightlines)*

Terrain should read **legible on a map-reader skim**: who is **on** what, what the **ground is made of**, and which way **sound and wind** move—without sounding like a survey report.

- **Surfaces and paths:** When composition matters (*loose rock*, *grass in the cracks*, *scree*), say **what sits underfoot**—often **full of**, **under him / under his boots**, **over loose stones**, **along the base / along the trail**—instead of compressing to *the path was rock and grass* unless you deliberately want telegraph poetry. Prefer **shifted under his boots** over vague *biting* when motion is the point.  
- **Cause before stance:** If the POV **leans into wind** or **trusts footing**, nail **why** in plain physics first (*no timber high enough here to blunt the gust*) before habit or mood (*nothing to hang onto except his stance*). Clever verbs (*cut the gust*) fail if the reader doesn’t see the missing windbreak.  
- **Sightlines:** Separate **where the body looks** from **what opens beyond** when a glance carries a beat (*Beyond the ridge, the reach opened…*) instead of stacking possessives (*the monument’s ridge*) or vague **out past**.  
- **Sound and distance:** Anchor audio in space (**sound from the slope below**) when direction matters; avoid prepositions that float (*off the slope*) unless the image is already fixed.  
- **Pronouns:** If **it** could mean **wind / face / argument / view**, swap in the **noun**, **merge into one sentence**, or use a single clear beat (*Cael’s bulk took the edge off the wind against Riven’s face*) so you don’t repeat the same noun twice awkwardly.  
- **Echoes:** Don’t stack the same **weather / wind / ridge** twice in one sentence without need—swap one slot for **sky**, **crest**, **reach**, or **gust** when the sentence would otherwise rhyme with itself.  
- **Articles:** Prefer **the** or a **possessive** when the thing is **this** scene’s element—not headline telegraph: *against **the** weather*, *through **the** wind*, *off **the** stone*, ***his** skin* when it’s Riven’s body. Keep bare forms only where English normally does (*letters worn by weather*, *in wet weather*, *toward dusk*).  
- **Revision cue:** Run this alongside **Plain sense default** on any paragraph where a cold reader could draw the wrong diagram; **`chapter-01-the-monument.md`** after its spatial pass is the practical reference.

### Character introduction *(first on-page appearance)*

When a **named character** appears for the **first time in the manuscript**, give readers a **short, concrete anchor**—not a dossier. Aim for the same density as Chapter 1’s first beats for **Riven**, **Cael**, and **Bran**: enough to **see** them in space (build, height vs POV when it matters, hair, skin, clothes or gear, age band for children; optionally **one** social read—how strangers might misread them, or how the POV knows them).

- **Same rule for everyone** new on stage—supporting traders, antagonists, kids in the street—not only leads. Scale length to importance; never skip entirely unless the name is truly throwaway noise and never focused again.
- **Filter through POV** where judgment matters (*To her he looked…*, *Riven had watched him…*) instead of neutral inventory voice.
- **Later chapters:** do **not** repeat the full intro every time someone walks on. Re-anchor only when **time, disguise, injury, or crowd** makes a fresh physical beat worth it.

### Clear agency *(who acts, who feels—and what literally happens)*

Close-third gains power when readers never have to guess **whose mind** they’re in or **what noun** did the verb. Poetry that hides the subject belongs in dialogue when a character **performs**; default narration should stay **legible on a literal read**.

- **No orphan actors.** Avoid sentences where **memory**, **silence**, **distance**, **the town**, or **something** behaves like a person unless the POV frames it as projection (*To Riven it felt like…*, *Greyhaven sounded…*). Prefer **named people** + plain verbs (**Riven thought**, **his thoughts drifted**, **wind hit his face**, **stone bulked overhead**).
- **Objects don’t watch, want, or judge.** Sidestep landmarks that **watch**, **wait**, **lie**, or **know** unless you mean deliberate personification in **someone’s** perception. Prefer **stood / rose / bulked / faced** (`From the ridge the monument rose…`) over **looked down on**.
- **Name the sensation’s owner.** Bond bleed and dread stay in the **POV skull**: show **heat**, **wrong speed**, **copper taste**—avoid **Something twisted** without a referent; if the cause is ambiguous on purpose, still anchor **body + wrong-fit** (*too hot for Cael’s words alone*).
- **Lore and theology:** when the ground “answers,” gods “choose,” or tales conflict, signal **who claims it** (**the pamphlet says**, **the old tale runs**, **Mara muttered**) so omniscience doesn’t sneak in through mythic grammar.
- **Prefer physical readings over clever compression.** If a metaphor needs a seminar to decode (*water stopped swimming*), swap for **what the POV actually experiences** (*horizon steadied*, *eyes cleared*).
- **Interior turns need a scene trigger, not a hard cut.** When thought pivots to memory, fear, or tally, anchor it to what just happened on the page (a line of dialogue, a visible detail, a sound from below). Then keep the sentence idiomatic—avoid formulaic scaffolding every time (*The question pulled up...*, *The sound made him think...*) if a cleaner transition can do the same job.

### Idiomatic figures of speech *(readable on first pass)*

Figures of speech should **sound like something a tired person would actually say**—not like a clever caption. If a reader stops to puzzle **which meaning** you meant, rewrite.

- **Default:** narration favors **literal description**; figures belong in **dialogue**, **quoted cheap literature**, or **single-shot judgment** (*borrowed time* for shoddy new construction)—not layered across every paragraph of description.  
- **One vehicle, one beat.** Avoid **mixed metaphors** (*shave an hour off his temper*) unless a character’s voice is deliberately chaotic. Match verb to image (**wasted an hour on temper**, **burned temper**, **ran out of slack**).
- **Noun collision / pun reads:** Don’t reuse a concrete noun in a figure too soon in a **different sense** in the same paragraph (e.g. **bowl** of stew vs **bowl** of mercy / livelihood). Scan for accidental echoes.
- **Verbs that imply literacy:** Be careful with *read* applied to non-text (*the statue read older*)—use **looked / seemed / scanned as** unless you mean deliberate craft (*the letter read like a threat*).
- **Modern metaphor debris:** *receipts*, *inventory*, *margin* as metaphor can read office-adjacent or internet-adjacent; prefer **tally**, **count**, **slack**, **rope talk** when the modern gloss distracts.
- **Dialogue** bends grammar and uses heat; it still follows **Plain dialogue** above—default revision is **spoken clarity**, not metaphor stacking for atmosphere. Save image clusters for **deliberate performance** (mockery, public challenge, ritual wording, singing) or a **single** sharp figure.  
- **Narration** should stay **idiomatic and legible** unless POV is genuinely spiraling.

### No ornate varnish *(default narration and dialogue)*

- **What “ornate” means here:** stock wisdom dressed as lyricism; metaphors piled for mood without adding fact; nouns acting like people (*the road remembering*); **clever consonance** instead of plain sight; theatrical stage directions in narration (*truth squatted*, *surprise opened a crack before cynicism slammed*, *metal’s patience*) when a **sensory** sentence would do.  
- **Prefer:** heat, distance, sound, speed, weight, skin, rope—**one** plain image, then move. If a line sounds like a poster quote, cut or swap one concrete verb.  
- **Exceptions:** oath / Velorum language may stay elevated **when the bond or ground are the subject**—but even then **one** elevated beat per passage; don’t stack. In dialogue, ornate lines land when a character **performs** for an audience or leans on **trade-craft nouns** (timber, chisel, rope) instead of abstract conceit pileups—see **Plain dialogue**. Default **narration** stays plain.  
- **Revision cue:** replace “sound smart” phrases with **what Cael/Riven/Lyra actually see or feel first**, then optionally one short judgment.

## Voice guardrails

- Prefer clarity over ceremony.  
- Avoid faux-archaic diction unless a character performs it deliberately.  
- Avoid anachronistic **Earth brand names, tech, or internet slang** unless you intentionally establish a bridge—default is timeless-modern speech in a secondary world.  
- **Blunt register is a feature:** plain profanity and rough idioms (*shit*, *hell*, *damn*, *bullshit*, *no shit*, *bitch*, *bastard*, *asshole*, *Goddamnit*, *God damn*, and the like) belong in dialogue when stress, character, or humor earn them; keep the land and Velorum lyrical—**experience stays mythic; speech stays contemporary and human**. Use *bitch*, *bastard*, and *asshole* deliberately: often **situational** (the road, the bond, silence “between,” likelihood-as-leverage)—not as cheap filler. When they name **people or power**, they must **earn their harm** on the page (pressure, contempt, refusal of being owned).  
- **`kick ass` is approved** as everyday contemporary idiom in dialogue (promise, threat, or praise) when it fits character heat and scene stakes; use it as spoken texture, not catchphrase wallpaper.  
- **Heavy profanity belongs on the page:** *fuck*, *fucking hell*, *Jesus Fucking Christ*, and *fuck almighty* are **approved**—use them **liberally** in dialogue where heat, humor, or harm warrant (especially **Riven**; **Lyra** under witness/public-square pressure in Kestrel Gate; **Cael** only when preservation’s mask cracks). **Vary** the shape (standalone breath, opener, mid-line hammer) so it reads like lived speech, not a single repeated tic.  
- **Body-part insults (occasional):** *twat*, *cock*, *pussy*, and *cunt* are **approved** as **spoken insults**—needling, contempt, pressure-release—between characters who already share friction and history (especially **Riven ↔ Cael**); use **sparingly** and only when the scene earns the sting. They are **not** coy euphemisms for anatomy in intimate scenes—this book **does not** include sex scenes (see **Content boundaries** below). **Lyra** only when a line matches her blade (rare; she is not Riven’s echo).  
- **Goddamnit** is allowed **often** as a habitual pressure-valve word—especially **Riven** (frustration at the bond, crowds, his own mouth); **Lyra** under witness/public-pressure stress (sparingly—she doesn’t mirror his cadence); **Cael** only at cracks in his control. **Vary** usage (opener, mid-sentence, standalone breath) so it stays alive, not a verbal tic every third line without texture. **God damn** (two words) alternates with **Goddamnit** for emphasis and rhythm—**do not** use *God damn it* in this project.

## Content boundaries *(voice-adjacent)*

- **No sex scenes on the page.** Do not write explicit sexual encounters. Attraction, tension, aftermath, and emotional intimacy are fine; **graphic sex is out of scope** for this project.

## Dialogue register (*who sounds how*)

Draft and revise so **mouth matches pressure**, not a uniform “fantasy polite.” Escalate with the scene; don’t carpet-bomb every page.

| Voice | Register |
|-------|-----------|

His candor is a **pressure valve**. |
| **Lyra** | Flat affect until she isn’t: precise, unmoved, **names power without prettifying it**. She is not Riven’s echo; she **cuts**, she doesn’t rant. |
| **Cael** | Controlled, consequence-first diction; **rare** slips into blunt speech—including **fuck** / **fucking hell** / **Jesus Fucking Christ** / **fuck almighty**—when preservation cracks. **Rare** mirrored insults (*twat*, *cock*, *pussy*, *cunt*) back toward **Riven** only when the seam breaks—**never** casual verbal wallpaper. ## Paragraph shape

- Prefer **longer, sustained paragraphs** that carry a beat forward across several sentences—interior motion, sensory detail, and consequence in one block rather than staggered one-liners.  
- Avoid **staccato stacks** of isolated single-sentence paragraphs unless a line truly needs to land alone (use sparingly).  
- When speakers alternate, keep the standard rule—**new paragraph per speaker**—but **embed** action, physical detail, and POV reaction *in* those paragraphs instead of stripping beats into separate micro-lines. When **rapid banter** stacks short lines, add enough **tags or beats** that the chain stays legible—see **Rapid banter attribution** under **Dialogue quality** below.  
- Narration especially should read like continuous fabric: combine related observations, hesitation, and judgment rather than breaking after every clause.  
- **Editing passes:** tightening prose must **not** strip paragraph fabric—when cutting repetition or sharpening dialogue, **recombine** beats into sustained blocks (especially narration and POV reaction). Never replace long paragraphs with chains of one-sentence paragraphs unless the rhythm is intentionally climactic—and even then, use sparingly. Applies to **full-chapter revisions** as well as line edits: preserve or restore **woven** narration between dialogue exchanges.
- **Global revision:** when tightening the whole book for readability, pick **one strong chapter** as the **shape reference** (how long narration blocks run, where dialogue breaks) and measure the rest against it—same standards as **Calibration samples** and the readability rules above.

## Dialogue quality *(mandatory in every chapter)*

Chapters must include **plenty of dialogue**: multiple sustained exchanges, back-and-forth during and between action, voices in counterpoint—not isolated lines dropped into description.

Dialogue must be:

- Subtext-driven  
- Tension-bearing  
- Varied in rhythm (short strikes, longer runs, interruptions)  
- **Speakable under pressure:** on a late polish, run **Plain dialogue** (same section)—match the directness of **`chapter-01-the-monument.md`** Cael/Riven ridge exchange; see **[chapter-edit-pass.md](./chapter-edit-pass.md)** → **Pass C2**.

“Plenty” does not mean filler. Every block should earn its place.

### Rapid banter attribution *(who’s speaking)*

Close-third readers should not have to **infer** the speaker from tone alone when **several short lines** trade quickly—especially **one-word** replies (*No*, *Both*, *What*, *I know*, *Fuck*) where either voice might fit.

- After **two unattributed volleys**, err on the side of a **tag** (*Name said / asked*) or a **named beat** (*Riven scrubbed…* then his line) before stacking more bare quotes.  
- **Alternate** tagging rather than labeling every line—often tagging **every second or third** line clears the stack without clutter.  
- If tagging makes **“said”** repeat heavy, swap some tags for **micro-beats** that still name who moves (*Cael’s jaw tightened. “Why?”*).  
- If a line’s speaker is already obvious from the sentence right before it, **drop the extra tag**—prioritize readability over mechanical label-every-line habits.  
- If a tiny line (**Fuck**, **I know**) gets a tag and the **next** beat would contradict it (*he didn’t answer*), rewrite the narration so silence still means what you intend (*He didn’t follow up. That was answer enough.*).  
- **Exemplar:** **`chapter-01-the-monument.md`** ridge arguments after a banter pass.

### Character voices (dialogue)

- **Cael:** controlled, precise  
- **Riven:** searching, adaptive  
- **Lyra:** clear, direct, unsettling  

### Each exchange must:

- shift understanding  
- reveal misalignment  
- deepen relationship  

### Avoid

- exposition disguised as conversation  
- one character monologuing while others go silent for pages  
- **Faux-medieval filler** (“verily,” stock “hark” cadence, ornament for its own sake)  
- **jarring Earth references** that break the secondary world without intent—**except** the **named heavy interjections** in Voice guardrails (*fuck*, *fucking hell*, *Jesus Fucking Christ*, *fuck almighty*), which are **deliberate contemporary register** for this novel, not accidental anachronism.  

Orchestration and revelation can be **shared** across many lines; characters may argue, test, interrupt, and circle the same fear in different words.

## Calibration samples *(texture reference)*

*Naming:* Refer to the stabilizing force as **Velorum** without a leading article in narration and ordinary speech (e.g. *Velorum held*, *Velorum corrected*)—unless an idiom or quoted ritual clearly calls for one.

### Voice & vividness (narration)

*Texture reference—not a plot beat. Same priorities as **`chapter-01-the-monument.md`**: concrete sequence, plain verbs, bond and Velorum named without ornament stacking.*

The trail dropped until the basin widened below. First the ridge wind quit; then the smell turned from pine to wet stone and shallow water.

Cael shortened his stride. Riven matched him without deciding to.

Between them the bond tightened wrong whenever they slipped out of sync—too hot for the weather, or Cael’s irritation landing before he spoke.

Ahead, dust hung where their boots should have scattered it.

Velorum corrected.

Riven’s boot slid on slick gravel.

The wrongness caught him mid-step—joint locked, spine checked—not his muscles choosing alone.

He drew back.

The gravel stayed put underfoot. The doubled sensation in his ribs eased partway.

Not fixed. Just quieter.

“You felt it,” Cael said.

“I noticed it.”

“That is not the same.”

“It is now.”

A pause.

“Velorum does not change,” Cael said.

Riven’s gaze remained forward.

“No,” he said. “We do.”

## Anti-patterns *(voice and dialogue)*

Do **not**:

- explain emotions explicitly (show through speech, body, silence)  
- write filler dialogue  
- bury action under ornamental prose  
- **decorate plain events with figurative fog**—if the wave broke, the bridge shifted, or the bond threw heat across, say that in plain terms before layering comparison  
- **pad narration with em dashes** instead of commas, semicolons, or sentence breaks—see **Em dashes** under Readability  
- **load dialogue with thesis-shaped metaphors** when a plain threat, concession, or question would carry the beat—see **Plain dialogue**  
- **dress scenery in museum-catalog or specialist jargon** (*plinth*, *socle*, opaque nautical or architectural lecture-words) when plain labels would carry the scene—see **Contemporary lexicon → No museum-catalog or opaque jargon**
