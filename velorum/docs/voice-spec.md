# Velorum — voice specification

Companion to **[project-spec.md](./project-spec.md)**, **[synopsis.md](./synopsis.md)**, and **[act-chapter-index.md](./act-chapter-index.md)**. This file holds **tone, dialogue register, profanity guardrails, paragraph shape, and dialogue craft**—everything that keeps the novel’s **contemporary mouth** consistent while the world stays mythical.

**synopsis.md** holds the **plain-language plot spine** (Greyhaven choice, Kestrel Gate pressure, Halverin collapse, Seeker journey, Greyhaven surrender ending). **project-spec.md** holds world rules, terrain mandates, **single-POV-per-chapter** rules, POV **lenses**, structure, scene-outline beats, ending requirements, and anti-patterns for Velorum/exposition. **act-chapter-index.md** holds the chapter-by-chapter POV/order plan and should stay aligned with both docs.

---

# TONE AND STYLE

- Grounded and emotionally precise; big stakes, **plainspoken** delivery  
- **Contemporary English** in narration and dialogue—contractions, varied sentence length, idioms that fit the invented world without sounding medieval cosplay  
- Clean prose; poetic lines only where they earn their keep (avoid stacked metaphors and purple ornament). Default narration: **plain sensory beats before clever ornament**—see **No ornate varnish** under Readability.  
- Emotion shown through behavior, silence, physical detail, and dialogue  
- Dialogue appears **often and at length** in every chapter—conversations carry conflict, revelation, and relationship; subtext-driven, **natural**, not sparse for effect  
- The **twist**: readers should feel they could meet these minds in the present day; the land, Velorum, and oath remain mythical  
- Everything should feel inevitable

## Readability *(~7th grade; profanity retained)*

- **Target:** prose that **most adult readers** can move through quickly—roughly **U.S. 7th-grade** readability (short words where they work, familiar syntax, concrete images). This is **not** “simple topics”—the ideas stay adult; the **delivery** stays clear.  
- **Dense language:** avoid rare or literary vocabulary when a common word does the same job; cut **stacked abstractions** and **noun piles** in favor of people doing things on the page. One strong image beats three clever synonyms.  
- **Profanity:** swearing is **not** the problem for readability—keep the **heavy-profanity** character and scene rules elsewhere in this file. Readability passes focus on **sentence shape** and **word choice**, not toning down voice.  
- **Sentences:** fix **true run-ons** (too many chained clauses, unclear referents, grammar that needs three reads). **Long sentences are fine** when each unit is deliberate—parallel rhythm, clear subjects, justified em dashes—not when the line is one accidental bag of “and… and… which…”  
- **Paragraphs:** length is **not** the enemy; **opaque wording** is. Prefer **longer, flowing paragraphs** where the voice spec already calls for woven narration—do not break paragraphs just to “simplify” if the fix is really vocabulary and sentence boundaries.

**Shape reference:** until the manuscript has a settled “gold” chapter, use **Calibration samples** below (same section of this file) and **`chapter-01-the-monument.md` (opening)** for place-first woven narration—compare new work to **density and flow**, not exact sentences.

### Concrete language; kill office creep

- **Bodies and dirt first:** anchor emotion and power in **what readers can see**—hands, jaw, rope, dust, rope burn, eye-line—before you name the idea. Prefer one physical beat over three abstract nouns (“cost,” “weight,” “invitation”) standing in for drama.  
- **Idiomatic, not office-shaped:** when you cut workplace-adjacent wording (*performance* piled as abstract jargon, *leverage*, *optics*), replace it with **what people actually say**—*performing*, *putting on a show*, *acting like*, *playing brave*. Don’t swap a natural phrase for a stiff euphemism that merely **avoids** office vocabulary but reads like a policy memo (*facilitating*, *optimizing*) or clinical stand-ins where spoken English already has a tool (*performing injury*, not *staging injury*—unless **dialogue** is deliberately theatrical). Institutional voices (magistrate, clerks) may sound office-like **on purpose**; default narration and ordinary speech should not.  
- **No modern workplace camouflage** in narration or ordinary dialogue: avoid *leverage, optics, bandwidth, circle back, takeaway, unpack, stakeholder, pivot, granular, onboarding, actionable, synergize*. If you mean **performing for an audience**, say **show / act / play brave**—not **performance** as abstract noun-stacking in narration; **performing** as a verb in dialogue (*performing injury*, *performing calm*) stays—it’s idiomatic.  
- **“Job description,” “role,” “reporting line”:** use only if you want **intentional** cold comedy or an institutional voice (e.g. merchant power). Default narration should sound like **road talk**, not HR.  
- **Velorum terms stay:** *alignment, sync, correction, bond, oath* are **in-world**—keep them; do **not** replace them with corporate *alignment* metaphors from another sense. When you mean ordinary English “they matched,” say **matched / snapped together / forced the same step** if you’re avoiding oath jargon in a given sentence.  
- **Abstractions to pressure-test:** *invitation, debt, theater (as metaphor), choreograph, translation (unless dialogue names language outright)*—rewrite until **someone wants something on the page** or **something hits skin**.

### Contemporary lexicon *(secondary world, not historical costume)*

The story does **not** take place in modern Earth, but **default narration and ordinary dialogue still use plain, contemporary English**—the vocabulary readers already live in. Myth and invented place stay mythical; **word choice** stays legible.

- **Prefer modern clarity over period flavor.** Swap costume-default nouns (*hawkers*, *chapbooks*, stock *verily* cadence) for **straight labels** unless a beat truly needs the older word (*street vendors*, *cheap printed stories*, *pamphlets*, *ledger*)—same objects, fewer decoder rings.  
- **What stays “other”:** **Velorum / oath grammar** (*alignment*, *bond*, *correction*, *sync*, named places, in-world oaths)—those are **setting vocabulary**, not museum English. Do **not** replace them with corporate or modern-tech metaphors (see office creep above).  
- **When period words earn their keep:** quoted ritual, a character **performing** pomposity, or a term taught **in-scene** before it carries weight—otherwise choose the word that fits **today’s ear**.  
- **Revision cue:** if a reader could mistake the word for homework (*chapbook*, *hawker*, *cordwainer*), try the **specific modern thing** (*cheap booklet*, *vendor*, *cobbler*) without flattening voice.

### Place-first narration & plain diction *(Velorum manuscript standard)*

Used to keep **setting** readable on a **first pass**—especially for readers who bump on specialist vocabulary or slogan-shaped sentences.

- **Ground “where” before “why.”** When you introduce or revisit a town, road, harbor, or wild stretch, stack **things people touch and trade**: docks, crates, rope, chimneys, coin, mud, livestock—before mood-only summary. Prefer **sand where it ran shallow near the harbor mouth** over nautical jargon that stops readers cold (*shoal*, unless context teaches it in-scene).
- **Social logic in bodies, not mottoes.** Avoid standalone **aphorisms** (*the town teaches X*) unless you tether them to **one visible consequence**—someone counting coins **because** theft happens, a wall that’s thin, a bowl that costs what it costs.
- **One-sentence practical verdict (optional):** After a dense place pass, you may land a short **summarizing judgment** grounded in survival terms (**That was Greyhaven in practical terms: rope, salt, wool, gossip…**) so readers can hold the whole sketch—then move on.
- **Exemplar chapter:** Until the project names a replacement “gold” chapter, treat **`velorum/manuscript/act-1-the-choice/chapter-01-the-monument.md`**—especially its **opening paragraph**—as the default reference for **density of concrete nouns**, **woven sentences**, and **plain diction** alongside calibration samples elsewhere in this file.

### Character introduction *(first on-page appearance)*

When a **named character** appears for the **first time in the manuscript**, give readers a **short, concrete anchor**—not a dossier. Aim for the same density as Chapter 1’s first beats for **Riven**, **Cael**, and **Bran**: enough to **see** them in space (build, height vs POV when it matters, hair, skin, clothes or gear, age band for children; optionally **one** social read—how strangers might misread them, or how the POV knows them).

- **Same rule for everyone** new on stage—supporting traders, antagonists, kids in the street—not only leads. Scale length to importance; never skip entirely unless the name is truly throwaway noise and never focused again.
- **Filter through POV** where judgment matters (*To her he looked…*, *Riven had watched him…*) instead of neutral inventory voice.
- **Later chapters:** do **not** repeat the full intro every time someone walks on. Re-anchor only when **time, disguise, injury, or crowd** makes a fresh physical beat worth it.

### Clear agency *(who acts, who feels—and what literally happens)*

Close-third gains power when readers never have to guess **whose mind** they’re in or **what noun** did the verb. Poetry that hides the subject belongs in dialogue when a character **performs**; default narration should stay **legible on a literal read**.

- **No orphan actors.** Avoid sentences where **memory**, **silence**, **distance**, **the town**, or **something** behaves like a person unless the POV frames it as projection (*To Riven it felt like…*, *Greyhaven sounded…*). Prefer **named people** + plain verbs (**Riven thought**, **his thoughts drifted**, **wind hit his face**, **stone bulked overhead**).
- **Objects don’t watch, want, or judge.** Sidestep landmarks that **watch**, **wait**, **lie**, or **know** unless you mean deliberate personification in **someone’s** perception. Prefer **stood / rose / bulked / faced** (`From the ridge the monument rose…`) over **looked down on**.
- **Name the sensation’s owner.** Bond bleed and dread stay in the **POV skull**: show **heat**, **wrong speed**, **copper taste**—avoid **Something twisted** without a referent; if the cause is ambiguous on purpose, still anchor **body + wrong-fit** (*too hot for Cael’s words alone*).
- **Lore and theology:** when the ground “answers,” gods “choose,” or tales conflict, signal **who claims it** (**the pamphlet says**, **the old tale runs**, **Mara muttered**) so omniscience doesn’t sneak in through mythic grammar.
- **Prefer physical readings over clever compression.** If a metaphor needs a seminar to decode (*water stopped swimming*), swap for **what the POV actually experiences** (*horizon steadied*, *eyes cleared*).

### Idiomatic figures of speech *(readable on first pass)*

Figures of speech should **sound like something a tired person would actually say**—not like a clever caption. If a reader stops to puzzle **which meaning** you meant, rewrite.

- **One vehicle, one beat.** Avoid **mixed metaphors** (*shave an hour off his temper*) unless a character’s voice is deliberately chaotic. Match verb to image (**wasted an hour on temper**, **burned temper**, **ran out of slack**).
- **Noun collision / pun reads:** Don’t reuse a concrete noun in a figure too soon in a **different sense** in the same paragraph (e.g. **bowl** of stew vs **bowl** of mercy / livelihood). Scan for accidental echoes.
- **Verbs that imply literacy:** Be careful with *read* applied to non-text (*the statue read older*)—use **looked / seemed / scanned as** unless you mean deliberate craft (*the letter read like a threat*).
- **Modern metaphor debris:** *receipts*, *inventory*, *margin* as metaphor can read office-adjacent or internet-adjacent; prefer **tally**, **count**, **slack**, **rope talk** when the modern gloss distracts.
- **Dialogue** may stack images and bend grammar; **narration** should stay **idiomatic and legible** unless POV is genuinely spiraling.

### No ornate varnish *(default narration and dialogue)*

- **What “ornate” means here:** stock wisdom dressed as lyricism; metaphors piled for mood without adding fact; nouns acting like people (*the road remembering*); **clever consonance** instead of plain sight; theatrical stage directions in narration (*truth squatted*, *surprise opened a crack before cynicism slammed*, *metal’s patience*) when a **sensory** sentence would do.  
- **Prefer:** heat, distance, sound, speed, weight, skin, rope—**one** plain image, then move. If a line sounds like a poster quote, cut or swap one concrete verb.  
- **Exceptions:** oath / Velorum language may stay elevated **when the bond or ground are the subject**—but even then **one** elevated beat per passage; don’t stack. Dialogue can snap ornate when a character **performs** (speech, mockery, ritual)—narration still defaults plain.  
- **Revision cue:** replace “sound smart” phrases with **what Cael/Riven/Lyra actually see or feel first**, then optionally one short judgment.

## Voice guardrails

- Prefer clarity over ceremony.  
- Avoid faux-archaic diction unless a character performs it deliberately.  
- Avoid anachronistic **Earth brand names, tech, or internet slang** unless you intentionally establish a bridge—default is timeless-modern speech in a secondary world.  
- **Blunt register is a feature:** plain profanity and rough idioms (*shit*, *hell*, *damn*, *bullshit*, *no shit*, *bitch*, *bastard*, *asshole*, *Goddamnit*, *God damn*, and the like) belong in dialogue when stress, character, or humor earn them; keep the land and Velorum lyrical—**experience stays mythic; speech stays contemporary and human**. Use *bitch*, *bastard*, and *asshole* deliberately: often **situational** (the road, the bond, silence “between,” likelihood-as-leverage)—not as cheap filler. When they name **people or power**, they must **earn their harm** on the page (pressure, contempt, refusal of being owned).  
- **`kick ass` is approved** as everyday contemporary idiom in dialogue (promise, threat, or praise) when it fits character heat and scene stakes; use it as spoken texture, not catchphrase wallpaper.  
- **Heavy profanity belongs on the page:** *fuck*, *fucking hell*, *Jesus Fucking Christ*, and *fuck almighty* are **approved**—use them **liberally** in dialogue where heat, humor, or harm warrant (especially **Riven**; **Lyra** under witness/public-square pressure in Kestrel Gate; **Cael** only when preservation’s mask cracks). **Vary** the shape (standalone breath, opener, mid-line hammer) so it reads like lived speech, not a single repeated tic.  
- **Body-part insults (occasional):** *twat*, *cock*, *pussy*, and *cunt* are **approved** as **spoken insults**—needling, contempt, pressure-release—between characters who already share friction and history (especially **Riven ↔ Cael**); use **sparingly** and only when the scene earns the sting. They are **not** coy euphemisms for anatomy in intimate scenes—this book **does not** include sex scenes (see **Content boundaries** below). **Lyra** only when a line matches her blade (rare; she is not Riven’s echo).  
- **Goddamnit** is allowed **often** as a habitual pressure-valve word—especially **Riven** (frustration at the bond, crowds, his own mouth); **Lyra** under witness/public-pressure stress (sparingly—she doesn’t mirror his cadence); **Cael** only at cracks in his control. **Vary** usage (opener, mid-sentence, standalone breath) so it stays alive, not a verbal tic every third line without texture. **God damn** (two words) alternates with **Goddamnit** for emphasis and rhythm—**do not** use *God damn it* in this project.

## Content boundaries *(voice-adjacent)*

- **No sex scenes on the page.** Do not write explicit sexual encounters. Attraction, tension, aftermath, and emotional intimacy are fine; **graphic sex is out of scope** for this project.

## Dialogue register (*who sounds how*)

Draft and revise so **mouth matches pressure**, not a uniform “fantasy polite.” Escalate with the scene; don’t carpet-bomb every page.

| Voice | Register |
|-------|-----------|
| **Riven** | Highest heat by default: sharp, impatient, funny-bitter; profanity and rough idioms when the bond, crowds, or hypocrisy squeeze him; **Goddamnit**, **God damn**, **fuck**, **fucking hell**, **Jesus Fucking Christ**, and **fuck almighty** land often when alignment scrapes him raw. **Occasional** body-part insults (*twat*, *cock*, *pussy*, *cunt*) toward **Cael** when banter or rage needs an ugly edge—**not** constant. His candor is a **pressure valve**. |
| **Lyra** | Flat affect until she isn’t: precise, unmoved, **names power without prettifying it**. Under Kestrel Gate / magistrate / public-square stakes, let her go **hard**—short clauses, ugly truths, **heavy profanity** when she refuses to be an audience for theater (Halverin’s control narrative, public blame-shifting, "alignment" as leash). **Body-part insults** only when a line truly fits her cut—**rare**. She is not Riven’s echo; she **cuts**, she doesn’t rant. |
| **Cael** | Controlled, consequence-first diction; **rare** slips into blunt speech—including **fuck** / **fucking hell** / **Jesus Fucking Christ** / **fuck almighty**—when preservation cracks. **Rare** mirrored insults (*twat*, *cock*, *pussy*, *cunt*) back toward **Riven** only when the seam breaks—**never** casual verbal wallpaper. Most swearing stays in Riven/Lyra/magistrate scenes around him rather than in his mouth. |
| **Magistrate & institutional power** | **Smooth menace**: civic-legal confidence, euphemism, “help” that inventories bodies. Politeness as **blade**. When challenged (especially by Lyra), answers can turn colder, nastier, or more openly transactional—still **in character**, not cartoon villain mustache-twirling. |

**Split narration vs speech:** narration stays comparatively clean; **terrain / Velorum** favor sensory and structural language over curse words. Loading emotion into dialogue keeps the mythical layer from dissolving into snark.

## Paragraph shape

- Prefer **longer, sustained paragraphs** that carry a beat forward across several sentences—interior motion, sensory detail, and consequence in one block rather than staggered one-liners.  
- Avoid **staccato stacks** of isolated single-sentence paragraphs unless a line truly needs to land alone (use sparingly).  
- When speakers alternate, keep the standard rule—**new paragraph per speaker**—but **embed** action, physical detail, and POV reaction *in* those paragraphs instead of stripping beats into separate micro-lines.  
- Narration especially should read like continuous fabric: combine related observations, hesitation, and judgment rather than breaking after every clause.  
- **Editing passes:** tightening prose must **not** strip paragraph fabric—when cutting repetition or sharpening dialogue, **recombine** beats into sustained blocks (especially narration and POV reaction). Never replace long paragraphs with chains of one-sentence paragraphs unless the rhythm is intentionally climactic—and even then, use sparingly. Applies to **full-chapter revisions** as well as line edits: preserve or restore **woven** narration between dialogue exchanges.
- **Global revision:** when tightening the whole book for readability, pick **one strong chapter** as the **shape reference** (how long narration blocks run, where dialogue breaks) and measure the rest against it—same standards as **Calibration samples** and the readability rules above.

## Dialogue quality *(mandatory in every chapter)*

Chapters must include **plenty of dialogue**: multiple sustained exchanges, back-and-forth during and between action, voices in counterpoint—not isolated lines dropped into description.

Dialogue must be:

- Subtext-driven  
- Tension-bearing  
- Varied in rhythm (short strikes, longer runs, interruptions)  

“Plenty” does not mean filler. Every block should earn its place.

### Character voices (dialogue)

- **Cael:** controlled, precise  
- **Riven:** searching, adaptive  
- **Lyra:** clear, direct, unsettling  

### Each exchange must:

- shift understanding  
- reveal misalignment  
- deepen relationship  

### Avoid

- exposition disguised as conversation  
- one character monologuing while others go silent for pages  
- **Faux-medieval filler** (“verily,” stock “hark” cadence, ornament for its own sake)  
- **jarring Earth references** that break the secondary world without intent—**except** the **named heavy interjections** in Voice guardrails (*fuck*, *fucking hell*, *Jesus Fucking Christ*, *fuck almighty*), which are **deliberate contemporary register** for this novel, not accidental anachronism.  

Orchestration and revelation can be **shared** across many lines; characters may argue, test, interrupt, and circle the same fear in different words.

## Calibration samples *(texture reference)*

*Naming:* Refer to the stabilizing force as **Velorum** without a leading article in narration and ordinary speech (e.g. *Velorum held*, *Velorum corrected*)—unless an idiom or quoted ritual clearly calls for one.

### Voice & vividness (narration)

The valley did not open all at once. It revealed itself in layers.

The air cooled first. Then the ground softened beneath their steps. Pale roots beneath the soil pulsed faintly, not with light, but with presence.

Cael slowed. Riven adjusted.

The alignment between them held—precise, unbroken. Not visible, but felt in the exactness of motion.

Ahead, the basin distorted.

Reflections lagged. The air resisted.

Velorum corrected.

Riven reached.

The correction came faster this time.

His hand stopped.

Not by force. By alignment.

He withdrew.

The world settled.

But not completely.

### Dialogue

“You felt it,” Cael said.

“I noticed it.”

“That is not the same.”

“It is now.”

A pause.

“Velorum does not change,” Cael said.

Riven’s gaze remained forward.

“No,” he said. “We do.”

## Anti-patterns *(voice and dialogue)*

Do **not**:

- explain emotions explicitly (show through speech, body, silence)  
- write filler dialogue  
- bury action under ornamental prose
