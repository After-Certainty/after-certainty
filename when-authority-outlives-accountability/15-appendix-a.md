# Appendix A

## How Legitimacy Travels: A Contemporary Illustration

This appendix offers a contemporary illustration of how legitimacy
transfer operates over time. It is not an evaluation of presidents as
individuals, nor a ranking of moral success or failure. Its purpose is
narrower: to make visible how different forms of legitimacy expand,
contract, and travel under pressure—even within systems explicitly
designed to constrain them.

The United States presidency is a useful case for this purpose not
because it is universal, but because it is deliberately constructed to
limit legitimacy transfer. Authority is time-bound, procedurally
conferred, and subject to renewal. When legitimacy nevertheless outpaces
accountability in such a system, the mechanisms at work become easier to
see.

What follows traces how legitimacy traveled across recent
presidencies—not by intent, but by inheritance.

## The Design Assumption

The U.S. presidency is primarily a form of **procedural legitimacy**.
Authority is granted through election, constrained by law, contested
through courts and legislatures, and renewed at fixed intervals. In
principle, legitimacy belongs to the process, not the person.

At scale, however, every office accumulates **office-based legitimacy**.
Powers exercised successfully tend to persist. Precedents harden into
permission. What one officeholder is allowed to do becomes what
successors are expected to manage.

The danger is not that authority persists—that is necessary for
continuity—but that it persists without renewed accountability or
constraint.

## Crisis and the Expansion of Office-Based Legitimacy

The presidency of George W. Bush illustrates how emergency conditions
accelerate legitimacy transfer.

Following the attacks of September 11, 2001, authority shifted rapidly
from procedural constraint toward **office-based legitimacy**.
Surveillance powers increased, executive discretion widened, and
emergency justification became a durable rationale. Many actions were
framed as temporary, but several became precedents embedded in the
office itself.

The key dynamic here is not intent, but inheritance. Authority exercised
under crisis conditions proved difficult to retract once normalized.
Accountability remained attached to the moment of decision; legitimacy
traveled forward through the office.

This illustrates how harm tolerated under necessity can become harm
normalized through precedent.

## Restraint Without Structural Retraction

The presidency of Barack Obama exemplifies **procedural legitimacy**
combined with **example-based restraint**.

Norms were emphasized. Deliberation was foregrounded. Authority was
exercised carefully and often reluctantly. Yet many expanded powers
inherited from prior administrations were left intact rather than
dismantled. The office remained structurally enlarged, even as its
occupant modeled moral seriousness.

This reveals a central limitation of restraint at scale: example does
not reliably constrain inheritance. Authority that is not actively
bounded continues to travel, even when exercised with care.

Here, legitimacy did not become sacralized—but neither was it
meaningfully reduced.

## Personalization and Sacralization Risk

The presidency of Donald Trump demonstrates how legitimacy can shift
away from procedure toward personalization.

Norms were treated as optional. Criticism was framed as disloyalty.
Authority was increasingly justified as a personal mandate rather than a
procedural role. Among supporters, legitimacy took on **sacralized
legitimacy** characteristics—moral exemption, identity fusion, and
resistance to contestation.

Importantly, this shift did not require the creation of new powers. It
was amplified by permissions already embedded in the office. Authority
that had accumulated over time proved easier to press when restraint was
abandoned.

This illustrates how sacralized legitimacy does not require formal
authoritarianism. It can emerge within existing institutions when
office-based authority is personalized and insulated from challenge.

## Procedural Restoration and Structural Inertia

The presidency of Joe Biden represents an explicit attempt to restore
**procedural legitimacy** and institutional norms. Emphasis returned to
lawful process, professional administration, and rhetorical restraint.

At the same time, the structural authority of the office remained
largely unchanged. Executive powers continued to be exercised,
precedents remained in force, and the accumulated legitimacy of the
presidency was inherited rather than unwound.

This illustrates the asymmetry of legitimacy transfer: authority expands
more easily than it contracts. Norms can be restored faster than
structures can be redesigned.

## What This Illustration Shows

Across these presidencies, a pattern becomes visible:

- Crisis accelerates legitimacy transfer

- Restraint does not automatically reverse inheritance

- Office-based authority accumulates even under good faith

- Sacralization becomes possible when contestability erodes

- Restoration of norms does not guarantee restoration of limits

No single presidency “breaks” the system. Instead, each leaves behind an
office slightly easier to use, slightly harder to constrain, and more
dependent on the character of its occupant.

This is not a story of individual failure. It is a story of authority
traveling farther than accountability.

## Why This Matters Beyond the Presidency

The presidency is not unique in this respect. It is merely visible.

The same pattern appears wherever authority:

- persists beyond proximity to consequence

- accumulates through precedent

- relies on restraint rather than enforceable limits

- is inherited without reauthorization

Organizations, movements, institutions, and families all face versions
of this dynamic at different scales.

The lesson is not that authority must be distrusted, but that legitimacy
must be deliberately bounded. Where authority travels without friction,
harm no longer requires choice—it proceeds automatically.

Legitimacy failures are rarely caused by a single actor. They emerge
when authority is asserted, affirmed, and inherited without renewed
judgment.
