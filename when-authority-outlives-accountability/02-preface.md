# Preface

This book did not begin as a theory of leadership. It began as a
problem.

Over time, I noticed that conversations about leadership—whether in
politics, religion, organizations, or families—kept collapsing into the
same unsatisfying patterns. Leaders were praised or condemned based on
outcomes alone. Moral seriousness was confused with effectiveness. Harm
was either excused by success or weaponized by failure. And authority,
once established, was often treated as something that could be inherited
without question.

These patterns felt incomplete. They explained too little while
justifying too much.

I also noticed that many of the leaders I found most morally compelling
were difficult to defend in conventional terms. They were often slow,
constrained, or misrecognized in their time. Meanwhile, leaders who
caused lasting damage were frequently described as strong, decisive, or
effective. The language we were using to evaluate leadership seemed
poorly suited to the reality we were trying to describe.

This book is an attempt to build a clearer lens.

Rather than asking whether leaders are good or bad, it asks how they
relate to harm, how far their influence reaches, and what happens to
their authority after they are gone. It separates moral posture from
effectiveness and effectiveness from legitimacy—not to dissolve
responsibility, but to locate it more precisely.

The lens offered here does not promise better leaders. It promises
better questions. It is meant to help readers resist hero worship
without collapsing into cynicism, and to take moral restraint seriously
even when it fails to scale.

Most importantly, this book is not written for people in power alone.
Leadership appears wherever responsibility is unevenly
distributed—within families, teams, communities, and caregiving
relationships. The moral challenges of leadership are often most visible
at these human scales, where harm cannot be abstracted and authority
must be renewed daily.

If this lens helps you notice where harm is placed, how far it travels,
and when authority has outlived accountability, then it has done its
work.

The rest, as always, is practice.
