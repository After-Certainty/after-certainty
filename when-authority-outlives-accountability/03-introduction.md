# Introduction

Leadership is one of the most morally loaded words we use, yet one of
the least examined in consistent moral terms. We write constantly about
leaders, but rarely agree on what, exactly, we are evaluating. We argue
constantly about who succeeded, who failed, who was strong, and who was
reckless—while rarely agreeing on what, exactly, we are evaluating.
Praise and blame accumulate quickly, often without reference to clear
standards. As a result, leadership judgment tends to collapse into
ideology, loyalty, or hindsight rather than sustained moral inquiry.

This book proposes a different way of seeing.

Instead of asking whether leaders are good or bad, it separates
leadership into three dimensions that are usually treated as one: moral
posture toward harm, effectiveness, and legitimacy over time. Each
dimension answers a different question. How did this leader relate to
harm when they had the power to choose otherwise? How widely and durably
did their actions shape behavior or systems? What authority persisted
after their direct control ended—through institutions, norms, or
precedent—and with what constraints?

These dimensions are often conflated, and that conflation distorts
judgment. Moral restraint is mistaken for weakness. Effectiveness is
mistaken for virtue. Authority is treated as something that can be
inherited without renewal or scrutiny. By separating these elements, the
lens makes it possible to evaluate leadership without excusing harm,
romanticizing restraint, or demanding impossible purity.

This is not a book about ranking leaders. It does not offer a formula
for success or a checklist for moral correctness. It is a diagnostic
lens—one designed to slow judgment, clarify responsibility, and resist
narratives that justify harm through outcomes alone. The lens does not
resolve moral tension; it makes that tension visible.

The chapters that follow move from diagnosis to structure. They examine
why leadership evaluation fails, how leaders relate to harm, why
effectiveness misleads, how legitimacy travels beyond accountability,
and what happens when these dimensions interact over time. Later
chapters return leadership to human scale, where harm cannot be
abstracted and authority must be continually renewed.

If this lens succeeds, it will not tell you whom to follow. It will help
you see more clearly what leadership is authorizing, who is bearing its
costs, and what is being passed forward without examination. That
clarity may not produce comfort or consensus. But without it, leadership
judgment quietly rewards harm that succeeds and dismisses restraint that
does not. Over time, this inversion shapes what kinds of leaders rise,
what kinds of actions are justified, and what kinds of damage become
acceptable.

Chapter 1 begins where most leadership evaluation goes wrong: with our
reliance on outcomes to stand in for moral reasoning.
