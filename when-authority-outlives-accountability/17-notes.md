# Notes

## Chapter 1

1.  **Jonathan Baron & John Hershey, [<u>Outcome Bias in Decision
    Evaluation</u>](https://www.sas.upenn.edu/~baron/papers.htm/judg.html).\**
    Demonstrates how decisions are judged by outcomes rather than
    decision quality.

2.  **Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*.\**
    Explains hindsight bias and the illusion of predictability after
    outcomes are known.

3.  **Philip Tetlock, *Expert Political Judgment*.\**
    Establishes the limits of foresight and expertise in complex,
    uncertain decisions.

4.  **Albert O. Hirschman, *The Rhetoric of Reaction*.\**
    Explains how moral critique often collapses into simplified
    narratives that obscure structural responsibility.

5.  **Lee Ross, “The Intuitive Psychologist and His Shortcomings.”\**
    Shows how moral judgments overemphasize character and underweight
    situational context.

6.  **Hannah Arendt, *Responsibility and Judgment*.\**
    Addresses how accountability decays as authority persists across
    time and institutions.

## Chapter 2

1.  **Jonathan Shay, *Achilles in Vietnam*.\**
    Moral injury as harm arising from authorized or normalized
    wrongdoing, not individual depravity.

2.  **Stanley Milgram, *Obedience to Authority*.\**
    Empirical evidence that ordinary individuals commit harm under
    legitimate authority.

3.  **Philip Zimbardo, *The Lucifer Effect*.\**
    Situational and institutional drivers of harmful behavior over time.

4.  **Amy Edmondson, *The Fearless Organization*.\**
    How leadership posture suppresses or enables harm visibility through
    psychological safety.

5.  **Max Weber, “Politics as a Vocation.”\**
    The ethic of responsibility and the inevitability of moral residue
    in leadership decisions.

6.  **Hannah Arendt, *Eichmann in Jerusalem*.\**
    Harm emerging from normalization, bureaucratic distance, and
    obedience rather than malice.

## Chapter 3

1.  **Amy Edmondson, *The Fearless Organization*.\**
    Empirical research on psychological safety, learning speed, and
    long-term performance.

2.  **Christina Maslach & Michael Leiter, *The Truth About Burnout*.\**
    On productivity gains that mask delayed human cost.

3.  **Charles Goodhart, “Problems of Monetary Management.”\**
    Foundational statement of what later became known as Goodhart’s Law.

4.  **Donald Campbell, “Assessing the Impact of Planned Social
    Change.”\**
    Metric fixation and distortion under measurement pressure.

5.  **Francis Fukuyama, *Trust*.\**
    On how trust-based systems scale more slowly but endure longer.

6.  **Hannah Arendt, *On Violence*.\**
    Distinguishing coercive effectiveness from legitimate authority,
    especially in crisis contexts.

7.  **Edgar Schein, *Organizational Culture and Leadership*.\**
    On norm persistence beyond formal authority.

## Chapter 4

1.  **Max Weber**, *Economy and Society\*
    — On the routinization of charisma and the structural transformation
    of moral authority into office.

2.  **Hannah Arendt**, *Between Past and Future\*
    — On authority, responsibility, and the danger of obedience detached
    from judgment.

3.  **Robert Michels**, *Political Parties\*
    — On the tendency of institutions to concentrate power and erode
    accountability over time.

## Chapter 5

1.  **James G. March & Johan P. Olsen**, *Rediscovering Institutions\*
    — How institutional outcomes emerge from norms, incentives, and
    legitimacy rather than intent.**\**

2.  **Albert O. Hirschman**, *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty\*
    — The tradeoffs between effectiveness, dissent, and long-term
    legitimacy.**\**

3.  **Ronald Heifetz**, *Leadership Without Easy Answers\*
    — Why morally serious leadership often appears ineffective in the
    short term.**\**

4.  **Philip Selznick**, *Leadership in Administration\*
    — Leadership as stewardship across time rather than outcome
    maximization.

## Chapter 6

1.  **Murray Bowen**, *Family Therapy in Clinical Practice\*
    — Differentiation, anxiety absorption, and why leaders must not
    internalize all harm.

2.  **Trauma-Informed Care Frameworks** (SAMHSA)\
    — Harm reduction, boundary-based care, and preventing harm from
    becoming identity.

3.  **Brené Brown**, *Dare to Lead\*
    — Boundaries as conditions for trust and sustainable leadership.

4.  **Zygmunt Bauman**, *Modernity and the Holocaust\*
    — Moral distance, scale, and why harm becomes abstract in large
    systems but visible in small ones.

## Chapter 7

1.  **Max Weber**, *Economy and Society\*
    — On charismatic authority, institutional decay, and the temporal
    instability of influence.

2.  **Hannah Arendt**, *The Human Condition*; *Responsibility and
    Judgment\*
    — On action, moral responsibility, and judgment unfolding over time.

3.  **Edgar Schein**, *Organizational Culture and Leadership\*
    — On norm persistence beyond formal authority.

4.  **Trauma-Informed Leadership Frameworks** (e.g., SAMHSA)\
    — On non-coercive influence, harm reduction, and preserving agency.
