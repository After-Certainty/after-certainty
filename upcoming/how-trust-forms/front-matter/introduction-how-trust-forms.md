# **Introduction**

## **The New Neighbor**

A moving truck pulled into the driveway on a Saturday morning. By afternoon, the family had filled the garage with boxes. Children carried armfuls of blankets through the front door while two adults struggled to maneuver a couch through a narrow hallway. Neighbors glanced over from their yards, slowed their cars as they passed, and quietly took note of the newcomers.

For the first few days, everyone watched from a distance. People exchanged waves from their driveways and introduced themselves in passing, only to forget names a few minutes later. Unfamiliar cars came and went at odd hours. Packages appeared on the porch. Lights glowed in rooms that the previous owners had rarely used.

The neighborhood knew almost nothing about the family. Were they friendly? Would they become part of the community or keep to themselves? Could anyone count on them? Would they become friends, sources of conflict, or simply strangers living a few houses away? No one could answer those questions, yet relationships began to form anyway.

Someone waved from across the street. A neighbor stopped to chat while taking out the trash. Another person moved a package under cover before a storm rolled through. Someone lent a tool, and the family returned it a few days later.[^intro-baier-vulnerability]

None of these interactions resolved the larger uncertainty. The neighborhood still knew very little about the people who had moved in. But those small moments created something that did not exist before.

Months later, the family no longer felt entirely unknown—not because everyone had gathered enough information, not because every question had found an answer. The uncertainty never disappeared completely. Instead, something else emerged alongside it.

Trust.

Most people think trust follows knowledge. We gather information, observe behavior, and accumulate evidence until we feel confident enough to decide that another person deserves our trust. Much of life does not work that way.

People cooperate long before they achieve certainty.[^intro-luhmann-cooperation] They join teams before they know everyone involved. They hire employees before they know exactly how those employees will perform. They marry without knowing what twenty years together will reveal. They vote without knowing what leaders will actually do once they take office. Again and again, people choose to participate before they can fully verify what will happen next.

That creates a puzzle. If uncertainty remains, why do people participate at all? How do strangers become neighbors, neighbors become partners, and groups of individuals become families, teams, communities, and institutions capable of acting together? This book explores that question.

Trust does not eliminate uncertainty. Instead, trust makes participation possible while uncertainty remains.[^intro-luhmann-participation]

[^intro-luhmann-cooperation]: Luhmann, *Trust and Power: Two Works*, trans. Howard Davis, John Raffan, and Kathryn Rooney (Chichester, UK: John Wiley & Sons, 1979).
