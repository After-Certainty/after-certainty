# **Author’s Note**

Several contemporary scenes in this book are composites.

The parent reading a school form, the worker holding a keycard outside an office building, the patient rereading a denial letter, the administrator studying a discipline spreadsheet, and related present-day moments are constructed from documented events, public institutional records, ordinary professional workflows, and recurring patterns. They do not portray identifiable private individuals unless a person is named in a cited public source. They are written to make institutional structure visible: how rules classify, how judgment enters or disappears, and how reconsideration becomes either real or merely formal.

Historical material in later chapters is reconstructed from cited sources and will require the usual final-source review before publication. Where public records leave uncertainty, the manuscript should not be read as filling that uncertainty with dramatic invention. The aim is fidelity to the structural problem these episodes reveal, not confidence beyond the evidence.

This is not a culture-war brief. Several chapters take up disputes that are currently attached to intense political argument. The book is not written to supply a preferred partisan outcome in those disputes. It is written to examine decision tensions that would remain even if a court, agency, legislature, or later administration resolved the immediate case differently. A rule can be necessary and still overbroad. An exception can be humane and still dangerous if no one can review it. A pattern can deserve scrutiny without proving every allegation made in its name.

Current-event claims from 2026 reflect information available at the time of drafting. They should be reverified before final publication, especially where litigation, agency enforcement, contract status, program availability, or federal regulatory policy may change. The manuscript does not predict lawsuit winners, agency outcomes, or the final legality of contested policies. It treats unresolved events as occasions for examining durable institutional questions: what makes access real, what makes discretion accountable, what makes an appeal reachable, and what makes a rule capable of learning from the case it did not anticipate.

Where the book describes people affected by institutions, the purpose is not to substitute sympathy for analysis. Sympathy can reveal what a rule has missed, but it cannot by itself govern a shared system. The argument pursued here is narrower: legitimate institutions need rules strong enough to protect people from arbitrary judgment, and judgment disciplined enough to protect people when a rule has mistaken them for someone else.
