# **About the Series**

The After Certainty books examine how people and institutions act responsibly when knowledge is incomplete, authority is distributed, and the world does not always match the categories built to govern it. Rules, metrics, models, procedures, and professional norms make common life possible. They coordinate strangers. They preserve memory across time. They make decisions reviewable by giving those decisions a shared form. They also compress reality. Every useful rule decides in advance which differences will matter and which will be treated as noise.

Each book in the series takes up that tension from a different angle. Some ask how meaning hardens into systems. Some ask how incentives become a moral language of their own. Some ask how institutions learn after harm has revealed what their prior knowledge missed. The shared concern is practical rather than nostalgic: how judgment survives when certainty can no longer be assumed, and how institutions remain legitimate when they must act before they know everything.

*No Time to Think*, the companion volume to this book, asks whether institutions still possess the capacity for judgment when acceleration changes the pace of work. Its question is temporal and operational: when generation, coordination, or throughput become faster, have the places where review, attention, verification, and stop authority live been redesigned as well?

*The Case That Does Not Fit* asks a neighboring question. It is concerned with the architecture of reconsideration: whether an institution can recognize when a general rule has mistaken a person for someone else, and whether it can make room for that recognition without turning judgment into favoritism, drift, or arbitrary local power.

The distinction matters. A system may have time to think and still lack a way to reconsider. It may possess an appeal form that no one can use, a discretion standard no one can review, or a category that no one is authorized to question. It may also make room for exceptions so loosely that similar people are treated differently for reasons no one can defend. Legitimacy requires both protections at once: rules that shield people from arbitrary judgment, and judgment that shields people from rules that have failed to understand them.
