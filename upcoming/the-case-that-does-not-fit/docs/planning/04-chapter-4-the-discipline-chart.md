## Chapter title

Chapter 4 — The Discipline Chart

## Governing question

When should patterned consequences trigger reconsideration of a facially neutral rule?

## Ordinary opening image

A school spreadsheet showing suspensions and expulsions by category.

## Case / terrain

On July 23, 2026, the U.S. Education Department rescinded disparate-impact provisions used in Title VI enforcement. The department argued that the provisions could pressure schools to engage in racial balancing and that civil-rights enforcement should focus on intentional unequal treatment of individuals. Civil-rights and education groups argued that effects-based review is necessary because facially neutral local policies may repeatedly harm one group even when no official acknowledges discriminatory intent.

## Conceptual movement

This case reverses the moral direction of the earlier chapters. General civil-rights rules and statistical review can protect people from local discretion, selective enforcement, and patterns no single decision reveals. At the same time, an effects-based rule can create pressure to treat individuals through racial categories or infer wrongdoing from unequal outcomes alone.

## Questions to preserve

- When should a repeated pattern of consequences trigger review without proof of hostile intent?
- How can institutions distinguish structural distortion from statistical coincidence?
- Can effects-based review protect individuals without converting demographic outcomes into quotas?
- When does local discretion become the very arbitrariness a general rule was designed to restrain?
- What evidence should be sufficient to reopen a policy that appears neutral in its wording?

## Return image

No cell in the spreadsheet contains a motive. The pattern exists anyway. Whether the pattern proves injustice is the question the spreadsheet cannot answer by itself.
