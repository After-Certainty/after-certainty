# Chapter 2 — Salt

Salt is the kind of thing most people notice only when it is missing.

A meal can contain expensive ingredients, hours of labor, and a recipe carried through generations, yet without salt it arrives strangely incomplete. The flavors are present but distant from themselves. Add a little and the food does not taste primarily of salt. It tastes more fully of what it already was.

This is one of salt’s small mysteries. It becomes most effective by disappearing.

It dissolves into water. It enters bread and broth. It preserves food long after the harvest has ended. It leaves the body through sweat and tears, returning ordinary grief and ordinary labor to the chemistry of the sea. Civilizations found it wherever land and water met, then built roads, taxes, trades, and monopolies around something no ruler had invented.

A person can live without gold. Salt is more difficult.

Perhaps this is why power has so often been tempted by it. Authority likes to stand near necessity. When people can refuse the thing being offered, the seller must persuade them. When they cannot refuse, persuasion becomes optional.

The tax collector does not need to make salt valuable.

The body has already done that.

A government may tax luxuries without entering every household. Tax salt and the state appears at every table. It reaches the rich and poor alike, but equality of reach is not equality of burden. A small charge disappears inside one family’s abundance and enters another family as subtraction: a little less food, a little more debt, another necessity postponed.

The tax seems modest because the object is modest.

That is often how domination survives inspection. Its most durable forms rarely announce themselves as spectacular cruelty. They become part of the price of ordinary life.

People pay. Children eat. The receipt disappears. The arrangement begins to look natural.

---

Empires prefer large maps.

A map allows power to appear coherent. Borders become lines. Railways connect ports to the interior. Provinces take on orderly colors. Resources move toward ships, laws move outward from capitals, and the scattered experiences of millions of people become an administrative problem that can be summarized in columns.

Salt is too small for the map.

It sits in the palm. It sticks to damp skin. It is gathered from water, scraped from earth, measured into food. It belongs to the scale at which political economy becomes bodily.

A ruler may speak of revenue.

A family knows what must be bought.

A law may claim a monopoly.

A person standing beside the sea sees abundance.

The contradiction does not require advanced education. Water reaches the shore, the sun draws it away, and something remains. Yet the person who gathers what nature has left can become a lawbreaker because an empire has placed itself between the sea and the hand.

This was the opening Mohandas Gandhi recognized in 1930.[^s2-1]

The Indian National Congress had declared complete independence its objective at the end of 1929. Gandhi was authorized to begin a new civil-disobedience campaign, but the form it would take was not obvious. The British Empire could absorb speeches. It could prohibit assemblies, arrest prominent figures, censor publications, and describe resistance as the ambition of educated elites rather than the demand of a population.

The movement needed an act ordinary enough to be understood and consequential enough to reveal the structure surrounding it.

Gandhi chose salt.

In a letter sent to Viceroy Lord Irwin on March 2, he listed broader injuries of British rule and announced that he would begin civil disobedience by breaking the salt law. He described the tax as especially unjust from the standpoint of the poor and argued that a struggle claiming to serve the poorest should begin there.[^s2-2]

The choice compressed an empire into a handful of crystals.

Land revenue required knowledge of land. Tariffs could become arguments about industry. Constitutional reform invited technical negotiations whose vocabulary belonged to lawyers and officials. Salt required almost no explanation.

You need it.

It is here.

You are forbidden to take it.

A law becomes vulnerable when ordinary people can describe its injustice without borrowing the language of the people who wrote it.

---

Leadership books often praise clarity as a form of communication.

The leader finds the simple message hidden inside complexity. She repeats it until everyone knows what matters. But simplicity can become manipulation when it removes everything that might complicate obedience. A slogan compresses reality so that people can act without examining too much.

Salt worked differently.

It simplified the entry into the struggle without pretending the struggle itself was simple.

Breaking the salt law would not redistribute land, end religious conflict, abolish caste, create democratic institutions, or determine what an independent Indian economy should become. It could not answer every dispute within the nationalist movement. It did not eliminate the differences between a rural laborer, a merchant, an industrial worker, a landlord, a Muslim political leader, a Dalit organizer, or a woman whose participation in public life was constrained by both empire and household.

Salt did not resolve these conflicts.

It allowed people separated by them to recognize one shared intrusion.

A symbol becomes useful when it reveals more than it conceals. The best symbols do not replace reality. They create a doorway into it.

Salt opened onto taxation, poverty, extraction, self-rule, and the right of ordinary people to act without waiting for permission from a distant government. It also opened onto a question Gandhi had spent years trying to answer:

How can people resist power without becoming organized by the same logic as the power they resist?

The answer would not be contained in the destination.

It would be practiced along the road.

---

On the morning of March 12, 1930, Gandhi left Sabarmati Ashram with seventy-eight selected companions. He was sixty-one years old. Their destination was Dandi, a coastal village roughly 241 miles away. The walk would take more than three weeks, passing through towns and villages where crowds gathered, local people joined portions of the procession, and Gandhi spoke repeatedly about salt, independence, foreign cloth, alcohol, discipline, and nonviolence.[^s2-3]

A march makes politics visible through bodies.

People who might otherwise exist as petitioners begin occupying distance together. Their argument appears not only in what they say but in the road they are willing to travel. Each step imposes a small cost. Feet blister. Muscles tire. Heat becomes part of the claim.

Walking is among the least specialized forms of public action. A person does not need formal office to join a road. She does not need to understand every clause of a proposed constitution. She needs enough conviction to leave where she was and continue toward somewhere else.

This availability is one of the differences between admiration and participation.

A brilliant speech can make listeners feel that they have witnessed courage. A march asks whether they will move.

The distinction mattered to Gandhi. His influence had already accumulated to a degree almost impossible to separate from the campaign. Newspapers followed him. Crowds waited. The frail body, walking staff, plain clothing, and disciplined routine had become part of a public image recognizable far beyond India.

It would be easy to call this humility and leave the matter there.

Humility can itself become theatrical once a person knows the world is watching. Plain clothing can become a uniform. A refusal of luxury can produce another kind of status. The person who appears to possess nothing may acquire immense moral power through the appearance.

Gandhi understood symbolism too well to be innocent of this.

That does not make the symbols false.

It makes them instruments, and instruments must be judged partly by what they permit other people to do.

The walk did not merely display Gandhi’s sacrifice. It created a form others could repeat.

---

We sometimes talk about example as though it were a gentler alternative to power.

The leader does not order. He demonstrates.

Others freely imitate.

But an example can exert extraordinary pressure. The more morally revered the person becomes, the harder it is to decline the invitation without appearing selfish, cowardly, or impure. A leader who undertakes a sacrifice may claim no authority over anyone else while making ordinary hesitation feel like betrayal.

This was especially true in Gandhi’s ashrams and campaigns, where discipline entered daily life. Prayer, diet, clothing, sanitation, spinning, sexuality, labor, and political action were not separate domains. Self-rule was meant to begin within the self.

The English phrase civil disobedience can make the method sound primarily legal: a person deliberately violates an unjust law and accepts the penalty. Gandhi’s satyagraha asked more. It required truthfulness, nonviolence, self-restraint, preparation, and a willingness to suffer without retaliating. The means were not merely a route toward political victory. They were supposed to contain the moral character of the society being sought.

This is one of Gandhi’s most enduring insights.

Means do not remain behind when the goal is reached.

They train the people using them.

A movement that relies upon humiliation becomes skilled at humiliation. A party organized around unquestioning loyalty does not become democratic merely because it wins an election. A revolution that treats every opponent as disposable may discover that disposal has become its most developed institutional capacity.

The future begins taking shape inside the methods used to approach it.

Nonviolence therefore had both a moral and an architectural purpose. It limited what participants were permitted to do to opponents, but it also attempted to create people capable of self-government. A person who could endure provocation without surrendering judgment had acquired something more than obedience to Gandhi.

He had acquired the capacity to choose under pressure.

At least, that was the hope.

---

The road itself challenged the distinction between leader and follower.

Gandhi remained unmistakably at the center. Villages prepared for his arrival. Reporters recorded his words. The procession’s meaning was inseparable from his presence.

Yet a march cannot be completed by symbolic authority alone.

Someone must arrange food. Someone must carry messages. Someone must maintain discipline when the leader is resting. Local organizers must prepare gatherings and translate the larger cause into the concerns of the place. People must continue walking when the body at the front is barely visible.

Leadership begins to circulate whenever the work produces decisions the central person cannot personally make.

A village resident sees the march and decides to violate the law locally.

A merchant stops selling foreign cloth.

A student leaves school.

A woman begins picketing a liquor shop.

A local organizer explains the discipline to people who never heard Gandhi speak.

The leader’s example remains the catalyst, but the next act no longer belongs entirely to him.

This is the point at which influence becomes dangerous to the institution resisting it. An authority can arrest a leader. It cannot arrest a method as easily once thousands of people understand how to enact it.

The state needs the movement to remain dependent upon the famous person.

The healthy movement must prepare for his absence.

---

When Gandhi and the marchers reached Dandi, he did not seize a fortress or occupy a government building.

On April 6, he bent toward the shore and lifted salt-bearing material from the ground, committing the ceremonial violation he had announced. He then declared the act available to anyone willing to risk prosecution: people could manufacture salt wherever possible and refuse the monopoly themselves.[^s2-4]

The gesture worked because it was both singular and reproducible.

Only Gandhi could perform Gandhi’s act. His particular legitimacy had gathered around the journey. The newspapers had followed his body to the coast.

But no special legitimacy was required to make salt afterward.

A person did not inherit Gandhi’s authority by picking up a handful. She acquired a way to act.

This distinction is central to the kind of leadership this book is trying to see.

Legitimacy may remain personal while leadership circulates.

Gandhi could not appoint another person to possess the trust created by his history, discipline, imprisonment, and public life. He could be replaced as an officeholder, but not as an example.

What he could do was create an action whose meaning did not depend forever upon his presence.

Once the law had been broken publicly, its inevitability weakened. The salt monopoly remained legally enforceable. Police could seize salt, arrest makers, beat marchers, and close organizations. Yet the law had lost something less tangible and perhaps more important: the appearance that compliance was the only imaginable behavior.

Disobedience had become available.

Not safe.

Not victorious.

Available.

---

The first procession from Sabarmati had included no women among its seventy-eight selected marchers.

Women objected. Many joined the wider salt campaign anyway, manufacturing salt, picketing shops, organizing demonstrations, courting arrest, and moving into forms of public leadership from which both colonial arrangements and gender expectations had often excluded them. Gandhi encouraged women to participate in parts of the civil-disobedience campaign, but the record also shows that they pushed beyond the roles he initially imagined for them.[^s2-5]

This is the moment at which circulation becomes more interesting than obedience.

A movement may invite people to participate while retaining a clear image of what their participation should look like. Women may serve the cause, but in appropriately feminine ways. They may display courage, but not claim too much authorship. They may widen the movement while leaving its symbolic center untouched.

Then participation changes the participants.

The person asked to help discovers she can organize.

The person invited into a supporting role sees that she can lead.

The movement’s own moral language becomes a tool through which its boundaries can be challenged.

If the struggle concerns self-rule, who is entitled to practice it?

If courage creates public standing, why should the person demonstrating courage remain politically secondary?

A leader cannot reliably contain the meaning of the capacities he helps awaken.

This is one of the most generative and unsettling qualities of leadership circulation. The people influenced by the leader may use their new agency to exceed his design. They may expose contradictions the leader had learned to tolerate. They may become evidence against the limits of his imagination.

The healthiest response is not to treat this as disloyalty.

It is to recognize that the influence has begun living beyond its source.

---

The British authorities arrested Gandhi during the night of May 4, before he could lead a planned action against the government-controlled salt works at Dharasana. Abbas Tyabji, a retired judge chosen to continue the campaign, was subsequently arrested as well. Leadership then passed publicly to Sarojini Naidu, the poet, nationalist organizer, and former president of the Indian National Congress.

The campaign did not stop because the central body had been removed.

This was more than operational succession.

Naidu did not become Gandhi. She did not inherit his exact legitimacy or merely imitate his manner. She entered with authority already formed through her own life, political work, speech, and relationships. The movement’s structure allowed that authority to become consequential at the moment it was needed.

This is what distributed leadership looks like before organizations give it a name.

The next leader is not manufactured instantly by the absence of the first. She has been developing elsewhere, acquiring knowledge and credibility through work the central narrative may have treated as secondary.

Then the center disappears.

What had been peripheral becomes visible.

Thousands of volunteers advanced on the Dharasana Salt Works. Police struck the unresisting marchers with steel-tipped batons. The American journalist Webb Miller described rows of protesters moving forward, falling under the blows, and being carried away while others replaced them. He later counted hundreds injured at a temporary hospital, and his reporting circulated internationally despite efforts at censorship.[^s2-6]

The bodies became an argument.

Not because suffering is inherently truthful. A person can suffer for a lie, a hatred, or a fantasy. Pain does not prove moral correctness.

The argument arose from the visible relationship between action and response.

People approached without weapons.

The state answered with force.

The empire claimed to preserve order, yet the order could be maintained only by striking people who would not strike back.

Nonviolence did not remove violence from the scene.

It clarified its direction.

---

This is one reason voluntary suffering can become more powerful than force.

Force often conceals the weakness of the authority using it. A government appears strongest when everyone obeys before coercion becomes visible. Police remain at a distance. Prisons are known but not encountered. The ordinary person cooperates because resistance seems futile, and the absence of resistance is offered as evidence of legitimacy.

Civil disobedience interrupts this arrangement.

The protester refuses the expected action while denying the state the violent opponent it knows how to describe. The authority must then tolerate the disobedience, negotiate with it, or reveal the force beneath the ordinary rule.

Each choice costs something.

But suffering alone does not produce this effect. The suffering must be visible, interpretable, and connected to a claim others can understand. It also requires extraordinary discipline from the people receiving the blows.

To refuse retaliation in a meeting is difficult.

To refuse it when a person beside you has been struck unconscious asks something few people can reliably promise.

This is why Gandhi’s nonviolence cannot be reduced to gentleness. It was a demanding form of coordination. The movement attempted to train fear, anger, loyalty, and bodily instinct so that participants could preserve the contrast between their conduct and the violence of the state.

At Dharasana, that discipline allowed people far from India to see the empire differently.

Yet there is danger in admiring the scene.

The wounded body can become material in the leader’s moral strategy. The person who designed the campaign may receive historical legitimacy while another person receives the baton. Sacrifice circulates unevenly. Some people ask for it. Others physically provide it.

A movement committed to moral ends must therefore ask not only whether suffering is voluntary, but whose suffering becomes useful to whom.

The leader who accepts danger alongside others gains one kind of credibility.

The leader who repeatedly spends other people’s bodies may retain the language of sacrifice while practicing another form of domination.

Gandhi understood this danger better than many leaders, but he did not escape it completely.

---

Moral authority is often described as power acquired without coercion.

The phrase is too clean.

A person may possess no legal authority and still exert pressure that others cannot realistically resist. Love, admiration, religious reverence, public expectation, and fear of communal blame can constrain a decision as powerfully as a written order.

Gandhi’s fasts make this visible.

In 1932, while imprisoned, he began a fast unto death in opposition to a British proposal granting separate electorates to the communities then officially described as the “Depressed Classes.” B. R. Ambedkar and other Dalit leaders had supported separate electorates as a means of securing independent political representation rather than leaving Dalit candidates dependent upon the votes and approval of caste Hindus. Under the pressure created by Gandhi’s fast, negotiations produced the Poona Pact, which replaced separate electorates with reserved seats elected through joint electorates. Ambedkar later remained deeply dissatisfied with the loss of the separate vote he regarded as a crucial political safeguard.[^s2-7]

Gandhi experienced the fast as self-sacrifice.

Ambedkar experienced its political force from the other side.

The body Gandhi endangered was his own. The moral burden did not remain there. If Gandhi died, Ambedkar and the Dalit cause could be blamed by millions who revered him. A choice that appeared nonviolent in physical form placed immense coercive pressure upon a leader representing people with far less social power.

This does not mean the fast and a police baton were equivalent.

It means voluntary suffering can become a lever.

The more beloved the person undertaking it, the longer the lever becomes.

This is one of the central ambiguities of example-based legitimacy. The leader acquires trust by accepting costs others avoid. That trust allows him to move people without office or procedure. But once the moral authority becomes sufficiently large, his personal conscience can begin overriding the judgment of people who do not share it.

The person who has mastered himself may become less able to see how much power he now holds over others.

Restraint is therefore not merely the refusal to use physical force.

It includes restraint in the use of moral pressure.

---

Gandhi’s conflict with Ambedkar reveals a deeper limit in the story of moral example.

Gandhi opposed untouchability and devoted enormous public attention to its abolition. Yet Ambedkar did not primarily need a revered caste Hindu to purify Hindu conscience. He sought political power, legal rights, social equality, and the destruction of the system that made one person’s moral awakening necessary for another person’s dignity.

The difference is architectural.

One approach asks the powerful to become better.

The other asks whether the less powerful can act without waiting for that improvement.

Gandhi’s model often placed extraordinary faith in conversion: the oppressor could be confronted without being dehumanized and might eventually recover moral recognition. This faith made nonviolent resistance possible. It also risked centering the conscience of the oppressor.

Ambedkar’s challenge was more institutional.

What protections exist when conversion does not arrive?

Who represents the excluded when the majority remains generous only on its own terms?

What form of legitimacy can be transferred through constitutions, elections, and rights rather than depending upon the moral stature of exceptional people?

These are not rival questions from which one can simply be discarded.

A just society needs transformed relationships and structures that do not require transformation to occur on schedule.

Gandhi could illuminate the moral failure of caste while still misunderstanding what political independence from caste Hindus meant to the people most harmed by it.

His example was unusually powerful.

It was not sufficient.

No example is.

---

The complications begin even earlier.

Gandhi’s political development in South Africa is often told as an awakening: a young Indian lawyer encounters racial discrimination, organizes resistance, develops satyagraha, and becomes the person who will later challenge the British Empire in India.

The awakening was partial.

Scholars examining Gandhi’s South African years have documented his loyalty to the British Empire during much of that period, his efforts to improve the status of Indians by distinguishing them from Black Africans, and language and political positions shaped by the racial hierarchies he later came to oppose more broadly. They also argue that earlier narratives often centered Gandhi while minimizing the agency of indentured and working-class Indians whose resistance did not begin with him.[^s2-8]

A person can recognize injustice when it reaches him and still fail to recognize its larger architecture.

This is not a uniquely Gandhian failure. It may be among the most common beginnings of moral awareness.

The rule becomes visible because it injures us.

We object to being placed lower without yet questioning the ladder.

Gandhi’s later thought widened considerably, and his methods would influence struggles far beyond the community he initially represented. But growth does not erase the people excluded from the earlier moral circle.

The story of development should not become a machine for converting every harm into evidence of eventual greatness.

The people encountered before the leader learned better still encountered him then.

Moral leadership must be judged by its capacity for revision, but revision begins with admitting that the earlier posture was not merely incomplete in theory. It participated in the world’s distribution of dignity.

The person who later teaches humanity may first have required other people to expose the limits of his own.

---

Personal austerity carries another danger.

Gandhi treated his own life as an experiment. Food, possessions, medicine, sexuality, sleep, clothing, and physical comfort became territories in which he tested self-command. This willingness to subject himself to discipline contributed to his credibility. He did not ask people to renounce conveniences while privately preserving all of his own.

But an experiment conducted by a leader rarely remains purely personal.

In the final years of his life, Gandhi involved young women close to him in controversial “experiments” intended to test his celibacy, including sharing a bed while unclothed. The women included his young grandnieces and a close female physician. The practices troubled members of his own circle and have remained among the most disturbing aspects of his life because the spiritual test was his while the asymmetry of age, reverence, dependence, and authority was borne by everyone involved.[^s2-9]

The point is not to use one unsettling episode to cancel a life.

Cancellation is another form of simplification.

The point is to notice what can happen when moral authority becomes too concentrated around a person’s interpretation of his own purity.

Gandhi may have believed the experiments demonstrated mastery over desire. Yet self-mastery does not authorize the use of other people as instruments in its proof.

The leader can sincerely believe that everyone is participating voluntarily while occupying a position from which refusal has become nearly unimaginable.

This is the shadow of example-based legitimacy.

People trust the leader because he has accepted unusual discipline.

That trust then shields his unusual demands from ordinary scrutiny.

A leader’s willingness to suffer is not enough. He must also remain alert to the possibility that other people are suffering inside the story he tells about his sacrifice.

Moral posture toward harm begins not with the purity of intention, but with attention to where the cost lands.

---

None of these complications makes the salt march less remarkable.

They make its pattern more precise.

Gandhi was not extraordinary because he became harmless.

He was extraordinary because he repeatedly attempted to connect political effectiveness with moral means, accepted substantial personal costs, and designed forms of resistance that ordinary people could reproduce. He was also capable of allowing his personal moral certainty to become pressure upon people with less authority, and his vision widened unevenly across race, caste, gender, and intimate power.

The contradictions are not debris surrounding the useful lesson.

They define its boundaries.

A leader’s example becomes generative when it creates agency in other people.

It becomes dangerous when it demands that their agency remain subordinate to his conscience.

The salt campaign moved toward the generative form because the central act could escape Gandhi’s control.

People made salt without him.

Women entered leadership roles he had not initially offered them.

Sarojini Naidu continued after his arrest.

Local communities turned one violation into many.

The campaign did not require every participant to become morally identical to Gandhi before acting.

They needed enough shared discipline to make the next move.

That is why the campaign belongs in a book about others becoming leaders rather than merely a book about a great leader.

Its most revealing moment is not Gandhi bending toward the shore.

It is the number of hands that bent afterward.

---

The salt campaign did not end British rule.

Gandhi was released in early 1931 and entered negotiations with Irwin. The resulting agreement suspended the civil-disobedience campaign, allowed limited salt manufacture in coastal areas, and made possible Gandhi’s participation in the Round Table Conference in London. Independence remained sixteen years away.[^s2-10]

The march did not resolve the conflicts that would eventually accompany independence.

It did not prevent Partition, mass displacement, or communal killing. Gandhi’s moral vocabulary remained immense while events demonstrated the limits of his ability to direct people who invoked religion, nation, fear, and revenge more powerfully than nonviolence.

This matters because effective symbolism can be mistaken for completed transformation.

A campaign changes what people believe is possible. Institutions, interests, identities, and historical injuries remain. The leader who reveals a system’s moral weakness has not necessarily acquired the capacity to build everything that must replace it.

The salt march’s effectiveness lay elsewhere.

It widened participation.

It made imperial law appear absurd and violent in the same frame.

It shifted political action from negotiations among elites toward an act available in villages and households.

It created international attention.

It forced the British government to confront Gandhi as a political actor rather than merely a dissident it could ignore.

Most importantly for this book, it demonstrated that disciplined example could become distributed practice.

The movement did not win because everyone became Gandhi.

It became powerful because people learned they did not have to wait for him.

---

Salt dissolves.

Place it in water and its visible shape disappears. Nothing seems to remain where the crystals were. Yet the water has changed. Taste reveals what sight can no longer locate.

Influence often survives this way.

We search for the leader’s image, institution, successor, or name. We ask whether the movement remained loyal to the original teaching. We look for a line connecting the famous act to everything that followed.

But example rarely travels intact.

It enters other people’s circumstances. They borrow a method and abandon part of its philosophy. They preserve a posture and reinterpret the purpose. They discover that the leader who made action imaginable also carried assumptions they must reject.

The influence becomes less visible as it becomes more widely usable.

Gandhi’s legitimacy belonged to Gandhi. No one inherited the right to command moral attention merely by walking behind him or quoting his words. His particular mixture of discipline, symbolism, stubbornness, spiritual conviction, political intelligence, and personal contradiction ended with the person.

The capacity he helped circulate was different.

A population could discover that cooperation was not a permanent fact of nature.

A law could remain written and lose the appearance of consent.

A person without office could force power to reveal itself by refusing to imitate it.

A movement could align its methods with part of the future it claimed to seek.

An ordinary act could become political when enough people performed it together.

These possibilities moved beyond him precisely because they did not require his authority to be transferred.

They required people to recognize their own.

---

There is probably salt somewhere near you now.

In a cupboard. On a table. Dissolved inside food. Waiting in a paper packet at the bottom of a bag.

Nothing about it looks revolutionary.

That is part of the lesson.

Power becomes difficult to challenge when it persuades people that political action belongs elsewhere—to capitals, parties, famous speakers, courts, executives, armies, and people whose biographies already resemble history.

Gandhi understood that the distance between authority and ordinary life could become a weakness.

The empire stood behind statutes, police, prisons, and the accumulated confidence of rule.

The resistance began with something small enough to hold.

This was moral authority through disciplined example, but the pattern did not end with admiration for the example. Gandhi walked, suffered, broke the law, and accepted arrest. Those acts concentrated legitimacy around him.

Then he opened the action.

Make salt.

Accept the risk.

Do not wait.

The rare leader does not merely demonstrate how much he can endure. He makes visible something other people can do without becoming him.

At the edge of the sea, Gandhi bent down and closed his hand around what the empire claimed.

The more enduring gesture came next.

The hand opened.
