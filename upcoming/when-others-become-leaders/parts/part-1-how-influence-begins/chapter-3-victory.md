# Chapter 3 — Victory

The final whistle is a small mercy.

For a while, everything has been reduced to a field, a clock, and a set of rules. Someone is ahead. Someone is behind. The people watching know what counts and when the counting will stop. Then the whistle sounds, the players slow, and the contest becomes something that has happened rather than something still happening.

Afterward comes the handshake.

Children are taught to offer it even when they do not feel like it. Especially then. The gesture asks the winner not to gloat and the loser not to strike. It does not require either person to pretend the result was unimportant. One side still won. The other must still carry defeat home. But the game can end because everyone agrees that the person on the other side remains a person after the score has been settled.

Life provides no referee to enforce this transition.

A war may end on paper while continuing through memory, property, language, and grief. A government may fall while the people who sustained it remain in their houses, professions, churches, and families. The defeated do not disappear when the flag changes. They become neighbors governed by the people they once governed, citizens of a country they may no longer recognize, parents teaching children their own account of what was lost.

This is why victory is so dangerous.

Before victory, moral attention naturally gathers around what must be resisted. The oppressor clarifies the struggle. The law is unjust, the prison is real, the humiliation can be named. People know where courage points because power stands somewhere else.

Victory turns the arrow around.

The movement that demanded accountability gains the power to punish. The people who were excluded gain the authority to exclude. The person who survived humiliation can now decide what humiliation the other side deserves.

Every victory contains an invitation to make suffering symmetrical.

You made us afraid. Now you will fear us.

You removed our names. Now yours will be removed.

You taught your children that they were superior. We will teach ours that you are unworthy.

Revenge is often described as a loss of reason, but it possesses a terrible logic. It promises to restore moral balance by reversing the direction of pain. The wound remains, yet someone else finally carries it.

The difficulty is that reversed harm rarely remains contained. The child inherits a punishment for something done before she was born. The defeated organize around humiliation. The memory of injustice acquires a second memory, then a third. Each generation knows itself as the one responding rather than the one beginning.

The final whistle never quite arrives.

---

We often admire restraint most when it costs nothing.

A person refuses dessert and calls it discipline. A powerful nation pardons an enemy who can no longer threaten it. A leader speaks generously about opponents after every important decision has already been secured.

Restraint becomes morally interesting only when another action is genuinely available.

The victorious person could take more and decides not to.

The state could humiliate and instead makes room.

The leader could remain indispensable and begins preparing to leave.

This does not make restraint gentle. Sometimes mercy requires greater control than punishment because punishment carries its own momentum. The crowd demands it. Advisers can justify it. Pain supplies evidence that it has been earned.

To stop before the other side has felt everything you felt can look like betrayal.

There are always people who sacrificed more than the negotiator. They buried children, lost years, watched homes disappear, and endured indignities from officials who now request reconciliation. They may reasonably ask why the person with the power to answer history is suddenly concerned about the comfort of those who made history unbearable.

The language of unity can arrive too early.

It can ask the wounded to become calm so that everyone else can move forward. It can turn forgiveness into another burden assigned to the people who already carried the greatest one. It can preserve property, status, and institutional continuity while celebrating a change of heart.

A society can reconcile symbolically while remaining materially arranged by the injustice it claims to have overcome.

For that reason, reconciliation must not be confused with niceness. It is not forgetting. It is not the disappearance of anger, and it cannot mean that every demand for accountability is evidence of bitterness.

At its most serious, reconciliation asks a harsher question:

How do we tell the truth about what happened without allowing what happened to determine forever what we are capable of doing next?

---

In April 1994, South Africans stood in lines that seemed to contain the country’s entire past.

Some had voted before. Most Black South Africans had not. Nearly twenty million people cast ballots in the first national election based on universal adult suffrage, waiting for hours at polling places where the ordinary act of marking paper carried the accumulated weight of generations. Nelson Mandela was elected by the new Parliament and inaugurated on May 10 as the first president of a democratic South Africa.[^v1]

A ballot is another small object asked to carry an enormous meaning.

It cannot restore the dead. It cannot return the years spent in prison or the land taken from families. It does not dissolve the wealth accumulated under laws designed to protect one population from another. The person who votes in the morning may return that evening to the same house, the same joblessness, the same distance from clean water or a good school.

Yet a ballot alters the direction of permission.

A person who had been governed as a racial category enters the booth as a citizen. The government must now explain itself to people it was built to control. The transformation is procedural, but procedure can become a moral event when exclusion has been written into the machinery of ordinary life.

Mandela entered office carrying unusual personal legitimacy. He had spent twenty-seven years in prison, become an international symbol of resistance to apartheid, and emerged as both leader of the African National Congress and the central public figure in the negotiated transition. But the country’s liberation was never the work of one man. It came through decades of organizing, strikes, student movements, churches, unions, underground networks, international pressure, armed resistance, civic groups, and people whose names never acquired the simplifying force of a single symbol. The negotiations themselves depended on figures across opposing political formations, including colleagues such as Oliver Tambo, Walter Sisulu, Cyril Ramaphosa, and many others who carried the struggle while Mandela was imprisoned.

History prefers to bring one person onto the balcony; movements happen in rooms the camera never enters.

The danger of Mandela’s greatness is that it can make South Africa’s transition look like an achievement of personality: a remarkable man forgave his jailers, the country followed, and peace appeared. The story is comforting because it places reconciliation inside individual character. We need only wait for an unusually magnanimous person.

But no private virtue could have negotiated a constitution, held an election, integrated hostile institutions, restrained armed factions, or make millions of people accept an uncertain future. Mandela mattered partly because other people had already created capacities around him. He became the face of a transition that required leadership to move through negotiators, organizers, communities, political parties, security structures, and ordinary citizens deciding not to answer each provocation with the violence it appeared to deserve.

The country did not avoid catastrophe because one man lacked anger.

It avoided catastrophe because enough people found ways to act beyond it.

---

The Mandela remembered through reconciliation can become almost weightless.

He smiles. He forgives. He wears a colorful shirt and speaks about peace. The decades before the smile disappear.

Mandela was not a lifelong pacifist. After peaceful resistance had been met with state violence, political organizations were banned, and legal avenues were closed, he helped establish Umkhonto we Sizwe, the ANC’s armed wing. In his 1964 statement from the dock at the Rivonia Trial, he explained the decision to pursue sabotage, arguing that continued demands for nonviolence in the face of government force had become unrealistic. He presented sabotage as an attempt to avoid terrorism and racial war while making the costs of apartheid impossible to ignore.[^v4]

This belongs in the story.

Otherwise reconciliation becomes the natural expression of a saintly temperament rather than a political and moral choice made by someone who had already concluded that force could become necessary.

Mandela did not reconcile because he believed conflict was always avoidable. He knew the appeal of militancy from the inside. He had helped lead it. He knew that an oppressed population could not be asked indefinitely to preserve the peace of the people oppressing it.

The distinction matters because moral posture toward harm is not revealed by refusing every use of power. Sometimes refusing to resist allows harm to continue unchallenged. A leader who values peace more than justice may simply protect the conditions producing conflict.

The harder question is how power is used, what alternatives have been exhausted, which harms are deliberately constrained, and whether the methods chosen preserve any possibility of a different relationship later.

Mandela’s account of sabotage was itself an attempt to draw such a boundary. Infrastructure and symbols of state power could be attacked while loss of life was to be avoided. Whether such distinctions can remain stable once violence begins is another question. Armed movements generate their own escalations, errors, factions, and moral compromises. The ANC’s armed struggle, like the state and other forces in the conflict, became connected to actions that could not be contained inside a purified story of liberation. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission would later investigate abuses committed across the political conflict, not only by the apartheid government.[^v5]

This does not create an equivalence between apartheid and resistance to apartheid. One was a racial order enforced through the state; the other arose against it.

It does prevent the story from becoming innocent.

Mandela’s restraint after victory is more significant when we remember that he was capable of conflict, not untouched by it. Reconciliation did not emerge from an inability to imagine punishment.

It emerged after punishment had become available.

---

Prison can make time feel like something owned by someone else.

Doors open according to another person’s schedule. Food arrives when another person decides. The prisoner’s day is divided by rules designed partly to make agency disappear. Years pass beyond the walls where children grow, parents age, movements change, and the language of public life shifts without you.

When Mandela entered prison, apartheid appeared durable. When he left in February 1990, the African National Congress had been unbanned and negotiations toward a new political order were beginning, though violence and the possibility of civil war remained very real. His twenty-seven years of imprisonment had made him a symbol, but symbols do not automatically know how to govern the world that forms around them.

A prisoner can survive by preserving the moral clarity of opposition. A president cannot govern through opposition alone.

He must decide which parts of the existing state can be transformed, which must be dismantled, and which cannot be replaced quickly without producing collapse. He must work with people whose authority came from the system he fought. He must reassure those who fear losing privilege without confirming that their privilege was legitimate.

This can look like compromise because it is compromise.

The question is what the compromise protects.

Some compromises protect life and create space for future justice. Others freeze injustice at the moment the powerful become willing to negotiate.

The two can be difficult to distinguish while the agreement is being written.

South Africa’s interim constitutional arrangements created a Government of National Unity. The African National Congress governed alongside the National Party, which had administered apartheid, and the Inkatha Freedom Party. F. W. de Klerk, the last president of the apartheid era, served as one of Mandela’s deputy presidents.

There is something almost intolerable in that image.

The person imprisoned by a state shares executive power with the leader who most recently presided over it. The old order does not disappear from the room. It sits at the table, retaining an office and a voice in the government replacing it.

To some, this was prudence. To others, evidence that political freedom had been purchased by allowing too much continuity in economic and institutional power.

Both judgments contain truth.

A peaceful transition required people who feared majority rule to believe they had a future within it. It also required the majority to believe that inclusion of former rulers was not simply apartheid surviving through politeness.

Mandela’s task was not to eliminate this tension. It was to prevent either side from resolving it through force before democratic institutions had time to take root.

He had to make former enemies feel safe without making their fear the country’s highest priority.

---

A year after the election, South Africa hosted the Rugby World Cup.

Rugby had never been merely a sport there. The Springbok emblem and the national team were deeply associated with white Afrikaner identity and the apartheid state. International sporting boycotts had helped isolate South Africa, and many Black South Africans experienced the team not as a national symbol but as the uniform of a country that excluded them.

New governments are often tempted to rename.

Names, flags, uniforms, statues, and songs become arguments about who owns the past. The victorious movement inherits a landscape filled with symbols created to deny its belonging. Keeping them can feel like submission. Removing them can feel like the first visible proof that history has changed sides.

The Springbok offered such a test.

To many ANC supporters, it represented a regime they had struggled to defeat. To many white South Africans, it represented continuity with a country they feared was vanishing. The emblem could be abolished, and there were strong reasons to abolish it.

Mandela instead helped preserve it for rugby and publicly embraced the national team. At the 1995 final in Johannesburg, he appeared wearing a Springbok jersey bearing the number of captain François Pienaar.[^v2] South Africa defeated New Zealand, and the scene became one of the most enduring symbols of the country’s reconciliation project.

The gesture is now so familiar that it can look inevitable.

It was not.

Mandela was placing the symbol of the former ruling culture on the body of the man that culture had imprisoned.

He did not erase the Springbok. He interrupted its ownership.

Symbols often appear fixed because one group has been allowed to use them without challenge. A flag means this. A song belongs to us. A uniform identifies people like me. But meaning is maintained through repetition, and repetition can be altered.

Mandela’s jersey did not declare that the past no longer mattered. It suggested that a symbol could be carried into a future its original owners had not imagined.

The generosity of the act lay partly in its asymmetry. The Black majority had won the democratic struggle. Mandela did not need white approval to become president. He used the legitimacy of victory to reassure people whose political dominance had ended.

This was not submission because the direction of power had changed.

It was restraint exercised from strength.

For white South Africans, the gesture offered a difficult invitation: you may belong to the new country, but you no longer possess it alone.

For Black South Africans, it asked something equally difficult: a symbol of exclusion might be reclaimed without pretending it had never excluded.

One jersey could not accomplish this. A stadium cannot redistribute land, reform schools, integrate neighborhoods, or repair families. The 1995 team remained overwhelmingly white, and transformation within rugby and the wider society would continue to expose the limits of symbolic unity.

But symbols are not meaningless because they are insufficient.

A symbol creates permission.

It allows people to imagine an action before institutions make that action ordinary. The person who expected humiliation sees another possibility. The person who expected revenge is asked to consider citizenship. The person who expected liberation to mean the removal of everything associated with the former enemy encounters a more complicated form of possession.

Mandela did not ask South Africans to forget who had won.

He asked what winning was for.

---

The victorious leader can become trapped inside gratitude.

People waited for him. They marched beneath his image, spoke his name abroad, and imagined his release through years when release seemed impossible. Once he reaches office, every institution begins bending toward him. His presence reassures the movement. His story disciplines rivals. The idea that no one else can hold the country together becomes easy to believe.

This is how exceptional leaders become permanent ones.

The reasons for staying are rarely described as vanity. The work is unfinished. Opponents remain dangerous. Successors are unprepared. The people are not ready. History requires one more term.

Each explanation may be true, yet together they can produce a system unable to survive the person credited with saving it.

Mandela announced early that he would not seek another presidential term.[^v3] He stepped down in 1999 and was succeeded by Thabo Mbeki, having already relinquished the ANC presidency in 1997. The constitution would have allowed him to remain longer; he chose not to.

Age and circumstance mattered. Mandela was no young ruler walking away from decades of possible office. His role in day-to-day government had already shifted toward Mbeki and others. Leaving after one term did not mean withdrawing from public life or abandoning influence.

It still mattered.

A new democracy received an early example of political succession in which the founding president did not treat his personal legitimacy as an argument against replacement.

The distinction between leadership and indispensability is easy to praise and hard to practice. A leader may sincerely want other people to grow while continuing to occupy every space in which growth would become visible. He delegates tasks but retains symbolic ownership. Others can administer the work, yet every achievement remains part of his story.

Stepping aside changes the story’s grammar. The government no longer belongs to the liberator; the office becomes something another person can occupy without becoming a lesser copy.

Mandela’s legitimacy could not be transferred intact to Mbeki. No successor could inherit the prison years, the Rivonia speech, the release, or the election. Example-based legitimacy ends at the boundary of the person who earned it.

But institutional legitimacy can be strengthened when the example-based leader accepts that boundary.

By leaving, Mandela did not give Mbeki his moral authority; he gave the presidency a chance to become more than Mandela.

---

The story of reconciliation is often told as though Mandela forgave South Africa.

No individual could do that.

The people whose relatives were tortured or killed possessed claims that the president could not surrender on their behalf. The family of a disappeared activist might value national peace and still demand to know where a body had been buried. A mother could support democracy without forgiving the person who murdered her child.

Forgiveness cannot be nationalized without becoming another form of dispossession.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission attempted a different arrangement. Established under the Government of National Unity and chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu, it created public processes for victims to describe gross human-rights violations and for perpetrators to seek amnesty. Amnesty was not meant to be automatic: applicants had to make full disclosure and demonstrate that the act was associated with a political objective. The Commission examined violations connected to the wider conflict, including those committed by the apartheid state and liberation movements.[^v5]

Truth was offered as something between vengeance and amnesia.

The country would not prosecute every politically motivated crime committed during decades of conflict. It would also not declare the past closed through silence. The person seeking amnesty had to speak. The acts hidden inside institutions and euphemisms had to acquire names, methods, locations, and victims.

This was not mercy without cost.

It was an attempt to make disclosure the price of release from punishment.

The Commission produced moments that altered public knowledge. Families learned what had happened to people who had disappeared. Officials and operatives described systems of detention, torture, killing, and concealment that could no longer be dismissed as isolated allegations. Its final report was presented to Mandela in 1998.

Yet truth does not become justice simply because it is spoken.

Some victims experienced the amnesty process as another transfer of burden. Perpetrators could receive legal protection through disclosure while survivors remained with grief, poverty, injury, and reparations that often came late or inadequately. People denied amnesty were supposed to remain vulnerable to prosecution, but many cases did not proceed. The Commission’s emphasis on individual violations also struggled to address apartheid as a system of land, labor, education, wealth, and institutional design rather than only a collection of brutal acts. Even the Nelson Mandela Foundation has acknowledged that, for many South Africans, reconciliation later came to feel like a betrayal because reparations, prosecutions, restitution, and structural transformation did not follow with sufficient force.

This limitation cannot be placed outside Mandela’s leadership as though it belongs to another story.

Reconciliation helped make democracy possible; it also risked becoming a beautiful language spoken over arrangements that remained profoundly unequal.

Political power changed hands much faster than land, capital, housing, education, and economic security. The democratic government expanded services and social support, but the distributional architecture of apartheid did not vanish. Critics have long argued that the negotiated transition protected too much existing economic power and that the language of national unity made more disruptive forms of redistribution harder to pursue.

A moral success can create the shelter beneath which another failure survives.

This does not mean reconciliation was a mistake. It means reconciliation was unfinished.

The absence of civil war is not the presence of justice; a country can avoid vengeance and still owe repair.

---

Rare leaders are often remembered through the choices in which they appeared most generous.

This can make generosity look painless.

Mandela’s public warmth toward former enemies became part of his moral authority. He invited former jailers to his inauguration. He met with figures who had defended the old order. He cultivated gestures designed to reassure. His presence seemed capable of lowering the temperature of a room before any policy had changed.

Relational credibility mattered.

People who feared the new government could look at Mandela and imagine that they might survive it. People who had suffered under apartheid could look at him and see that the state now contained someone whose life had been marked by the same system.

But charisma can also hide coordination.

Mandela did not personally reconcile each town, integrate each institution, or negotiate each local conflict. Other people had to translate the national promise into schools, police services, workplaces, courts, neighborhoods, and families. Sometimes they did. Sometimes they did not.

The country’s most durable achievements came not from everyone becoming Mandela, but from enough people becoming capable of making the next decision without him.

A civil servant had to apply new law through an inherited bureaucracy; a police officer had to understand that citizenship no longer had a color; a teacher had to enter a classroom where children carried different memories of the same country; a local organizer had to tell supporters that the restraint requested of them was not surrender; a white family had to decide whether equality meant loss or belonging; a Black family had to decide whether national reconciliation had any credibility when daily life remained unequal.

No president could answer all of these questions.

Leadership circulated whenever someone else accepted responsibility for part of the transition.

This circulation was fragile because Mandela’s legitimacy could sometimes substitute for the institutions still being built. “Mandela wants this” could move people who did not yet trust the constitution, the party, or one another. That power was useful.

It was also temporary.

A nation cannot remain held together by affection for one person. Eventually procedures must carry what personality once carried. Courts, elections, public norms, opposition parties, civic organizations, and transfers of office must become legitimate even when the person occupying them lacks the founder’s moral stature.

Mandela’s greatest symbolic strength therefore created one of the central dangers facing his leadership:

South Africans could believe in him before they learned to believe in the system that would remain after him.

Leaving office was one answer. Not a complete one.

---

There is a photograph in nearly every victory.

The candidate stands above the crowd. The athlete lifts the trophy. The soldier raises the flag. The movement gathers around the person whose face has come to represent years of labor by thousands of people.

The photograph makes victory look like possession. Someone has won it. But no leader owns what happens afterward.

Mandela could influence the direction of the transition, model restraint, lend legitimacy to new institutions, and use symbols to enlarge the meaning of citizenship. He could not determine what future presidents would do with the state, whether the governing party would remain accountable, whether corruption would hollow out public trust, or whether reconciliation would become a living practice rather than a memory recited at ceremonies.

Enduring influence is never control extended through time.

It is capacity released into people and institutions the leader cannot command forever.

This may be why voluntary limitation matters so much. The leader who refuses to leave is still trying to govern the future personally. The leader who circulates responsibility accepts that other people must eventually make choices he would not make, misunderstand parts of his example, and test whether the principles survive without the person.

Mandela’s record was not pure enough to become a formula.

That is part of its usefulness.

He had participated in armed struggle and later became the global symbol of reconciliation. He embodied extraordinary moral authority while governing through difficult compromises. He helped secure political freedom while presiding over a transition that left much economic inequality intact. He offered gestures of generosity that changed what millions could imagine, yet gestures could not complete the work they began.

The pattern is not that great leaders forgive.

Forgiveness can be personal, premature, coerced, or used to protect the powerful.

The pattern is something closer to restraint after legitimacy has accumulated.

Mandela acquired the authority to tell a victorious movement that winning did not require reducing its former opponents to permanent enemies. He acquired enough trust to make inclusion look less like weakness. He used personal legitimacy to stabilize institutions, then accepted that the institutions had to continue without him.

He did not eliminate the conflict between justice and reconciliation.

He refused to solve it by sacrificing either one entirely.

---

At the end of a game, the winner and loser meet briefly in the middle.

The handshake does not change the score. It does not ask the loser to be grateful. It marks the boundary between competition and the life that must continue afterward.

Nations do not receive such clean boundaries.

The people who lost power remain. The people who won remain wounded. The field is also the neighborhood, the workplace, the government office, the school, and the land beneath every house. There is no locker room to which either side can retreat.

The handshake, if it comes, must be repeated in a thousand less visible places.

It must survive when the symbol has faded and the crowd no longer feels generous.

It must be accompanied by truth, repair, and institutions strong enough to restrain the next leader who discovers that reconciliation is less useful to him than division.

Mandela cannot be credited with every time this happened in South Africa.

He cannot be absolved for every time it did not.

His distinctive act was to stand at the point where history had reversed the direction of power and make another reversal possible.

Instead of asking how completely the victors could occupy the center, he asked how the center might be rebuilt so that victory did not require another population to disappear.

The answer remained incomplete. Perhaps every serious answer does.

In 1994, Mandela stood before a country that had made him prisoner and accepted its presidency. Five years later, he left the office to someone else. Between those moments, he used the legitimacy gathered around one extraordinary life to make room for people whose lives were not like his—including some who had once regarded his freedom as a threat.

Victory had brought him to the center.

Leadership appeared in what he chose not to keep there.
