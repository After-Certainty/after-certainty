# **Chapter 2**

## **The Signature**

A clinical note waits for a doctor’s signature.

The note looks finished. Sentences are complete. Codes are suggested. The history of present illness reads as if someone sat with the patient and translated the encounter into institutional language. In a sense, someone did—or something did. The clinician’s remaining act is small: a click, a scrawl, a confirmation that this record may enter the permanent file.

That act is not small in what it transfers.

A July 2026 Government Accountability Office technology assessment found that AI tools for medical notes and coding may reduce administrative burden while remaining difficult to verify, with uncertain effects on spending and oversight. The same assessment noted that U.S. clinicians average a 57-hour workweek, including roughly seven hours of administrative work.[^c2-gao-notes] The promise is real. Documentation toil is not a romantic form of care. Reducing it could return attention to patients, to teaching, to the parts of medicine that cannot be summarized from a template. The relocated work is also real. A generated note creates a new task—verifying that the summary belongs to the right person, preserves the clinically important detail, and does not quietly convert an error into the chart that everyone afterward will treat as fact.

Verification is not the same work as drafting. Drafting asks what to say. Verification asks whether what was said is true enough for the decisions that will lean on it. A wrong allergy omitted. A symptom softened into something easier for the model to pattern-match. A billing code that fits the language better than the patient. A prior diagnosis carried forward because it sounded consistent. None of these failures need malice. They need only a fluent surface and a tired reader. Once signed, the note becomes institutional memory. Nurses, consultants, insurers, and future clinicians will inherit it as if a careful human had authored every sentence under conditions that made care possible.

Saved documentation time does not automatically become patient attention. It can become more patients scheduled into the same day. It can become more notes to sign under the same fatigue. It can become a thinner pause between generation and responsibility. The organization may still call the tool “assistance” while the workflow treats the human as a bottleneck to be minimized rather than as the point where judgment is supposed to occur. Assistance and autonomous administrative action are not distinguished by marketing copy. They are distinguished by whether the institution still allocates time and authority for a person to refuse, revise, or stop.

Who reviews the machine-generated note, and under what time pressure? If the verifier is already exhausted, the signature becomes theater: the appearance of ownership without the conditions ownership requires. A 57-hour week does not leave a generous margin for close reading of every paragraph the model produced. Seven administrative hours, if reduced, may simply be reallocated into more encounters, more inbox messages, more signatures waiting in the queue. The constraint moves. Documentation generation becomes cheaper. Consequence contact—the clinician’s encounter with what the record will do after it leaves the screen—does not automatically expand. It may shrink.

The difficulty of verification is easy to underestimate because the note reads as finished English. Fluency disguises uncertainty. A human draft often carries visible hesitation—gaps, hedges, unfinished thoughts that invite a second look. A generated draft can arrive already polished, which reduces the social cue that something still needs checking. Under time pressure, polish becomes a kind of camouflage. The verifier is asked to do harder epistemic work in less time, while the interface presents the work as nearly done.

This is not an argument that clinicians should write every note by hand forever. Handwriting did not guarantee accuracy. Dictation did not guarantee attention. Templates did not guarantee truth. The older bottleneck of documentation could also harm care by stealing time and attention from the bedside. The diagnostic point is narrower. When generation accelerates, the scarce work becomes verification under consequence. If the organization measures only notes completed and minutes saved, it will miss whether anyone still had enough context, rest, and permission to catch the sentence that should not have been signed.

The shifted bottleneck is easy to hide because the signature still looks like control. A human name remains on the chart. Liability still attaches. Regulators and payers still see a responsible party. What may have changed is the match between that formal ownership and the actual conditions for judgment. Responsibility without time is not safety. It is a transfer of risk onto the person least able to refuse the queue. An institution that wants the benefits of generated documentation without degrading care has to staff the pause the signature pretends to be—not only the model that fills the page above it.

The signature takes one second. It transfers responsibility for every sentence above it.
