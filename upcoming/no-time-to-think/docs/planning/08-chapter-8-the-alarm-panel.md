## Chapter title

Chapter 8 — The Alarm Panel

## Governing question

When alerts arrive faster than understanding, can responsibility remain meaningful?

## Ordinary opening image

A control panel with warning lights and alarms arriving faster than a person can interpret them.

## Case / terrain

During the 1979 Three Mile Island accident, equipment failures, confusing indications, alarms, and operator misunderstanding contributed to the seriousness of the event. The aftermath produced major changes in operator training, human-factors engineering, emergency planning, and the design of nuclear operations.

## Conceptual movement

Automation can leave people responsible for exceptional recovery while depriving them of a coherent picture of the system. More alerts do not necessarily create more awareness. Information can arrive faster than understanding.

## Questions to preserve

- When does an alert become noise?
- What knowledge must a human retain when normal operation is automated?
- How should systems reveal state rather than merely report symptoms?
- Can an operator be held responsible for a system whose signals do not support comprehension?

## Return image

The instruments sensed many things. The constraint had moved into comprehension: whether a human operator could assemble the signals into an accurate picture before consequence arrived.

**Historical terrain:** Three Mile Island (1979).
