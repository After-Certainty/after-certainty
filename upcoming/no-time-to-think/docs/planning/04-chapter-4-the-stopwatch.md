## Chapter title

Chapter 4 — The Stopwatch

## Governing question

What disappears when measurement defines which parts of work count?

## Ordinary opening image

A stopwatch held beside a worker performing a repeated task.

## Case / terrain

Frederick Winslow Taylor’s scientific management used time studies to make work observable, divisible, standardizable, and improvable. The method identified genuine inefficiencies and helped shape modern management. It also encouraged organizations to treat the motions visible to an observer as the whole of work.

## Conceptual movement

Measurement can reveal waste while editing out tacit knowledge, adaptation, cooperation, recovery, and the worker’s own understanding of the task. The stopwatch does not merely record the pace. It defines which parts of the work count.

## Questions to preserve

- What disappeared when work was divided into only the actions a stopwatch could see?
- When does standardization preserve knowledge, and when does it remove judgment?
- Who decides what counts as wasted motion?
- How does a productivity measure become a moral account of the worker?

## Return image

The stopwatch made each movement easier to see. The variation it removed returned elsewhere as supervision, breakdown, resistance, and exception.
