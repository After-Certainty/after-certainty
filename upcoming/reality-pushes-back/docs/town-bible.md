Reality Pushes Back
Town Bible — Working Draft v1.1
A pattern language is not a catalog of isolated events. It names recurring movements that become visible because people share a world long enough to experience their causes, consequences, and returns.
________________

Document Status
This document is the canonical production reference for the town in Reality Pushes Back.
It is intentionally incomplete.
Future revisions should clarify, correct, or simplify the existing architecture rather than continually expand it. New detail should be added only when it supports the manuscript’s continuity, strengthens a recurring pattern, or resolves a practical drafting problem.
The Town Bible is not an encyclopedia. It preserves the stable relationships the chapters need.
Status: Locked for first-draft use.
Revisions after this point should respond to discoveries made in the manuscript rather than further abstract planning.
________________

Purpose
Reality Pushes Back explores twelve recurring patterns through the ordinary life of one town across approximately fifteen years.
The town is not merely a setting for examples. It is a shared world in which perception, power, time, and contact continually shape one another. Decisions become routines. Routines become structures. Structures drift away from their original purposes. Consequences return through people, materials, failures, exceptions, and changed conditions.
Every chapter should discover its pattern from this same lived environment rather than inventing an unrelated illustration.
Readers should gradually feel that they know the town—not because everything has been explained, but because its places, rhythms, objects, and people recur with accumulated history.
________________

Possible Front Matter
Preserve this passage for evaluation after the manuscript exists. It may eventually belong in the introduction, jacket copy, proposal, or nowhere. It is not yet canonical manuscript text.
This town is not exceptional.
It is not famous. It has no defining tragedy and no miraculous success. Most people outside it have never heard its name.
Yet within it, children become adults, strangers become neighbors, decisions become traditions, buildings outlive their builders, and ordinary people continually discover that reality has something more to say than their plans anticipated.
That is enough.
It is in places like this that history quietly becomes the world we all inherit.

I. Governing Principles
Reality Remains an Active Participant
Plans, categories, institutions, and decisions encounter materials, people, histories, and consequences they did not fully contain.
Wood has grain. Buildings settle. Rivers rise. Children exceed their records. Rules meet cases their authors never imagined. Decisions produce consequences somewhere, even when those consequences remain temporarily invisible.
The town does not illustrate this principle from outside. Its ordinary life continually reveals it.
________________

Nothing Extraordinary Happens. Everything Important Happens.
The manuscript should resist the gravitational pull of small-town melodrama.
There are no conspiracies, spectacular crimes, miraculous rescues, or catastrophes introduced merely to produce narrative momentum.
The events that shape the town are ordinary:
* children begin and finish school
* businesses change hands
* buildings age
* roads are repaired
* budgets tighten
* policies accumulate
* people move away or return
* neighbors become ill
* people retire and die
* traditions adapt
* mistakes reveal themselves slowly
* someone notices what others have missed
* someone fails to notice in time
The book’s stakes arise from accumulation, not spectacle.
________________

Patterns Emerge Before They Are Named
Readers should first encounter an ordinary moment, object, or resistance.
The chapter then widens through craft, history, art, science, public life, institutions, or another field in which the same movement appears.
Only after readers have experienced the shape should the chapter name and clarify the pattern.
The pattern should feel discovered rather than imposed.
________________

Continuity Deepens but Never Unlocks
Every chapter must stand on its own.
A reader opening directly to Structures Outlive Reasons should understand the chapter without reconstructing earlier events. A reader moving through the book chronologically should recognize additional echoes, consequences, and returning objects.
Earlier continuity may deepen the chapter. It may never be required to unlock it.
________________

Recognition Rewards but Never Burdens
Recurring people, objects, locations, and phrases are rewards for attentive reading.
They are not tests.
The prose should never pause to update the reader on every character. A returning person should be reintroduced through present action, not summarized biography.
Each chapter may include no more than one explicit recap of a prior event, and only when that earlier event is necessary to the featured pattern.
________________

Objects Carry More Continuity Than Exposition
A stained recipe card, a repaired sweater, a forgotten key, or an old council notice can carry years of history without a paragraph of explanation.
Whenever possible, continuity should travel through physical objects, routines, locations, or repeated actions rather than narrative summary.
________________

The Narrator Explains Patterns Completely and People Incompletely
The philosophical work should become precise.
The people should remain larger than the explanation.
The narrator may clarify a pattern’s movement, consequences, tensions, and balancing response. The narrator may not reduce a person to the pattern currently visible in their life.
No character exists merely to embody an idea.
Every character encounters multiple patterns. Every pattern appears in more than one life.
________________

II. Narrative Discipline
Things the Narrator Never Says
These examples demonstrate the rule. They do not reserve these exact actions for the manuscript.
The narrator does not say:
Ben was the practical one.
The prose might instead show Ben turning a board over, checking its grain, and measuring again before deciding that the first cut cannot be saved.
The narrator does not say:
June valued tradition.
The prose might instead show June holding a pencil over her mother’s handwriting and choosing to add a note in the margin rather than cross the original line out.
The narrator does not say:
Daniel struggled with responsibility.
The prose might instead show Daniel reading the same complaint twice, setting it beneath the permit awaiting his signature, and going downstairs to see the affected place before deciding.
Character should emerge through attention, action, speech, hesitation, habit, and consequence.
________________

Things the Narrator Never Knows
The narrator may know:
* what happened
* what someone said
* what a person did
* what an object bears
* what an institution recorded
* what consequences became visible
* what a person appeared to notice or avoid
The narrator does not possess:
* the complete reason Ben hesitated
* the final explanation of what Tessa wanted
* certainty about whether June regretted a decision
* a complete account of what Daniel believed years later
* the whole meaning of anyone’s silence
The narrator may infer, suggest, compare, and leave room.
The narrator does not enter a person merely to close uncertainty.
________________

Reintroduce Through Action, Not Biography
Avoid:
Ben Alvarez, the carpenter who had later become the community center’s facilities manager, entered the boiler room.
Prefer:
Ben set his toolbox below the boiler panel and waited for the knock in the pipe to come again.
The role becomes apparent because the person is doing the work.
________________

One Chapter, One Remembered Cause
A chapter may depend centrally on one earlier decision, event, or object movement.
It should not require the reader to retrieve several separate strands of continuity before the featured pattern makes sense.
Connections may accumulate around the edges, but the central motion must remain clear.
________________

The Ending Preserves Attention, Not Resolution
The town may learn. It does not graduate from uncertainty.
The final chapter should not prove that the community has mastered the pattern language. It should show that someone has become more capable of noticing, asking, listening, or keeping a decision open.
Attention may improve.
Outcomes remain unsettled.
Reality continues answering.

III. The Town
Name
TBD
The name is deliberately deferred until several chapters have established the place’s sound, geography, and history.
The final name should:
* sound plausible rather than symbolic
* avoid strong associations with existing fictional towns or series
* not suggest a specific real town unless intentionally chosen
* feel natural in dialogue and repeated prose
* carry enough age to suggest that people inherited it rather than invented it recently
The name must be selected before the manuscript reaches its final editorial stage.
________________

Scale
Working population: roughly 12,000–18,000.
The precise number may remain unstated.
The town is large enough for:
* school administration
* municipal engineering
* a town council
* distinct neighborhoods
* professional and civic institutions
* residents who do not all know one another
It is small enough for:
* routines to overlap
* people to recognize one another’s cars
* the same locations to serve multiple public purposes
* reputations to travel faster than official information
* decisions made in one room to reach people encountered elsewhere
The town is neither booming nor dying.
It changes gradually and unevenly.
________________

Geography
The town developed near a river crossing.
Its oldest commercial streets sit close to the river and the original bridge. Older residential blocks spread behind downtown. Later subdivisions extend toward higher ground and the highway. The school complex and municipal buildings sit between the older neighborhoods and the newer edge, making them places where different eras of the town meet.
The river is treated first as a physical presence with practical effects:
* crossing
* drainage
* maintenance
* recreation
* weather
* neighborhood boundaries
* travel time
* flooding risk
* soil and building conditions
Any larger resonance must arise from those actual effects rather than being imposed upon it.
________________

Stable Spatial Relationships
These relationships should remain consistent even if no formal map is created.
1. The bakery and Town Hall are within walking distance of one another.
 Daniel can leave a council meeting and reach the bakery without driving. Informal conversations often continue there after official meetings end.
2. The elementary school and community center share an older residential corridor.
 Children walk between them for programs, voting days, rehearsals, sports, and public events. Nora and Ben naturally encounter one another through maintenance and community use.
3. The river separates part of the older town from a later neighborhood expansion.
 The bridge is ordinary infrastructure but creates practical differences in access, traffic, emergency routes, school travel, and political attention.
4. The hardware store sits near the edge of downtown rather than in a distant retail district.
 Ben can leave a repair temporarily, walk or drive only a few minutes for a part, and encounter people who ask him about unrelated problems.
5. Town Hall sees maps of the whole town; the bakery, school, and community center experience the town in particulars.
 This is not symbolic architecture. It is a practical difference in what information each place naturally receives.
Additional spatial facts should be added only when chapters require them.

IV. Important Places
June Park’s Bakery
The bakery is a working business before it is a social symbol.
It opens early. Its rhythms are shaped by proofing time, oven heat, deliveries, weather, holidays, staffing, and what people actually buy.
It also becomes one of the town’s informal meeting places:
   * Nora gets coffee before school.
   * Ben buys bread on Saturdays.
   * Daniel holds conversations there that began elsewhere.
   * Notices are posted near the entrance.
   * Fundraising orders connect the bakery to schools, teams, funerals, and civic groups.
   * People arrive for food and leave with news.
The bakery carries memory through recipes, handwriting, equipment, smells, repeated customers, and expectations that may outlive the reasons behind them.
It should never become a magical place where every conflict is resolved over coffee.
________________

The Elementary School
Nora begins as a teacher and later works in administration, eventually becoming principal.
The school is where categories repeatedly meet individual children:
   * test scores
   * attendance
   * behavior codes
   * medical accommodations
   * family circumstances
   * funding formulas
   * classroom judgment
   * district policy
The school should not be portrayed as either a cold bureaucracy or a refuge of purely personal care. It contains both institutional necessity and human attention.
Most children pass through and appear briefly.
The school persists.
________________

The Community Center
The community center is old enough to contain several periods of construction.
It has been expanded, partitioned, repaired, and repurposed over decades. No surviving document perfectly describes its current physical form.
It hosts:
   * public meetings
   * elections
   * dances
   * youth programs
   * sports
   * fundraisers
   * funerals
   * classes
   * temporary shelter during ordinary local disruptions
   * storage for things no current program claims
Ben gradually comes to know the building through repair rather than documentation.
He does not know everything about it.
The building contains obsolete controls, inaccessible spaces, layered paint, undocumented modifications, and repairs that solved one problem while relocating another.
________________

The Hardware Store
The hardware store is where residents bring practical questions before they know exactly what they need.
It sells materials, but its deeper function is translation:
   * a sound becomes a possible valve problem
   * a stain becomes a question about moisture
   * a measurement becomes a choice among imperfect parts
   * a project becomes a conversation about what the house was built to tolerate
It should remain a real business with limited inventory, closing hours, staff turnover, and products that change.
________________

Town Hall
Town Hall is where lived concerns become agendas, categories, budgets, maps, ordinances, notices, permits, and votes.
Daniel gradually spends more time here.
Rachel works with its records and planning systems but often must leave the building to understand what the records represent.
Town Hall is not inherently detached. Its work requires abstraction. The recurring question is whether abstraction retains a path back to the places and people it governs.
________________

The Library
The library keeps imperfect memory.
It contains:
   * local newspapers
   * council minutes
   * maps
   * school yearbooks
   * donated photographs
   * oral-history recordings
   * community bulletin boards
   * public internet
   * children’s programs
   * objects or documents no one knows how to classify
The library does not preserve everything, and preservation does not guarantee interpretation.
It is one of the places where the town can recover reasons that its structures have outlived.
________________

The River and Bridge
The river is ordinary most days.
The bridge is used more often than it is noticed.
Together they affect:
   * commuting
   * school routes
   * traffic
   * business access
   * public works
   * neighborhood development
   * drainage
   * emergency response
   * which consequences remain visible to whom
Neither should be romanticized. Their importance comes from repeated use.
________________

V. The Witnesses
Each recurring person sees the town from a different position.
These are gravitational tendencies, not assigned symbolic roles.
No one sees the whole.
________________

Nora Jensen
Work: Teacher → school administrator → principal
Relationship to the town: Arrived after college and stayed.
Nora is no longer an outsider, but she retains some sensitivity to assumptions that lifelong residents treat as natural.
She often notices:
   * how people are categorized
   * what records reveal and omit
   * how judgment changes with proximity
   * how institutional requirements shape individual care
   * how explanations about children harden
   * the difference between what a system can record and what a person may need
Nora should not be written as the uniquely compassionate person surrounded by bureaucracy. She participates in the institution, benefits from its order, enforces some of its rules, and occasionally discovers that her own judgment has compressed someone.
________________

Ben Alvarez
Work: Carpenter and repair contractor → facilities manager
Relationship to the town: Never left.
Ben knows the town through materials, buildings, repeated routes, and accumulated repairs.
He often notices:
   * grain
   * wear
   * sound
   * water
   * settling
   * temporary fixes that became permanent
   * the difference between a plan and the material in front of him
   * consequences that appear physically before they become administrative facts
Ben is not infallible because he is close to the work. Familiarity can also make a person stop noticing. He sometimes accepts a workaround precisely because it has always held before.
________________

June Park
Work: Bakery owner
Relationship to the town: Inherited the bakery.
June experiences inheritance as both resource and obligation.
She often notices:
   * repetition
   * family memory
   * seasonal changes
   * gradual shifts in customers
   * recipes that depend on knowledge not fully written down
   * traditions whose reasons have changed
   * the difference between preserving a practice and preserving its purpose
June is not “tradition.” She adapts constantly. Some changes come easily; others expose what she has confused with loyalty.
________________

Daniel Okoye
Work: Community organizer → council member
Relationship to the town: Grew up here, left, and later returned.
Daniel can compare the town he remembers with the town that now exists. He can also mistake memory for knowledge.
He often notices:
   * public attention
   * informal influence
   * who gets heard
   * how participation becomes legitimacy
   * the unequal distribution of leverage
   * how a solution that once felt local becomes distant once translated into policy
   * the temptation to make uncertainty actionable by simplifying it
Daniel should not become the book’s protagonist or complete a tidy redemption arc. His decisions matter because he acquires leverage, not because his life organizes the town’s meaning.
________________

Rachel Kim
Work: Municipal engineer
Status: Recurring secondary witness
Relationship to the town: A newer arrival.
Rachel asks questions longtime residents have stopped asking:
   * Why does the road bend here?
   * Why does this review happen twice?
   * Why is that drainage line missing from the current map?
   * Why is the community center still operated as though an old addition exists?
Her fresh eyes expose inherited assumptions.
Her technical confidence can also obscure local knowledge she has not yet learned to recognize.
Rachel should be central in at least one chapter and materially important in two or three others. She does not require a full independent life arc.
________________

Tessa — Surname TBD
Status: Recurring secondary witness
Relationship to the town: First appears as a young resident; later encounters occur at irregular intervals.
Tessa is intentionally unusual within the chronology.
Most children pass through the schools and appear only briefly. The continuity of youth belongs primarily to the institution, not to one child.
Tessa is the deliberate exception. She repeatedly crosses the town’s attention at different ages and from different vantage points.
Nora knows one Tessa.
Ben encounters another aspect of her.
June sees something neither of them sees.
Daniel may interpret her public actions differently still.
Each encounter may be accurate.
No encounter becomes the final account.
Tessa is not a mystery to solve, a symbolic child, a narrative prize, or a future savior of the town. She must appear directly in specific scenes, speak, act, make choices, and remain capable of surprising both the town and the reader.
________________

VI. Relationships and Ordinary Intersections
These relationships provide recurring contact without forcing plot.
   * Ben buys bread from June most Saturdays.
   * Nora stops at the bakery before school often enough that June recognizes changes in her routine.
   * Daniel uses the bakery for informal conversations, but June does not allow the business to become his unofficial office without resistance.
   * Nora and Ben encounter one another through school and community-center maintenance.
   * Rachel and Ben inspect buildings or infrastructure together and often begin with different kinds of evidence.
   * Daniel and Rachel work together when public concerns become municipal projects.
   * June participates in school, funeral, holiday, and fundraising orders that bring her into indirect contact with families she may not otherwise know well.
   * Tessa crosses these routines irregularly rather than appearing on a schedule.
   * The library connects all of them through records, meetings, public notices, and the retrieval of forgotten information.
Not every relationship needs emotional intimacy.
Shared routines are enough.
________________

VII. Rhythms
Rhythms recur whether or not they become chapter events.
Their repetition allows the reader to notice change.
Spring
   * school testing and the approach of graduation
   * youth sports beginning again
   * gardens and public beds being planted
   * pothole and road repair
   * the river path reopening after winter
   * building inspections delayed during colder months
   * the bakery shifting from winter orders to lighter seasonal work
Summer
   * farmers’ market
   * construction and maintenance
   * outdoor concerts or library events
   * school buildings emptied, cleaned, altered, and repurposed
   * road closures residents navigate by habit
   * longer evenings along the river path
   * public projects moving faster because fewer formal meetings are scheduled
Autumn
   * school beginning
   * new students entering old systems
   * sports and community events
   * budget planning
   * leaves accumulating around drains and older buildings
   * the bakery returning to inherited seasonal recipes
   * the first cold mornings revealing what summer repairs missed
Winter
   * boiler inspections
   * snow and ice exposing differences among neighborhoods and routes
   * budget meetings
   * the holiday parade or civic gathering
   * short power outages and frozen pipes
   * the bakery’s busiest period
   * older residents becoming more dependent on ordinary town systems
   * maintenance decisions made under pressure becoming next year’s inherited workaround
Locally Specific Rhythms to Develop
At least two recurring rhythms should arise from the town’s particular geography and history rather than a generic small-town calendar.
Candidates:
   * the first spring inspection of the river path and bridge approaches
   * an annual community-center maintenance week during school break
   * a library sale built around decades of donated local material
   * a seasonal bakery item made according to an inherited date rather than current demand
   * a recurring traffic diversion so familiar to longtime residents that newcomers find it irrational
   * an annual check of old flood or high-water markers
These should be selected through drafting rather than added merely for charm.

VIII. Object Biographies
Objects enter the book as used things, not as declared symbols.
Their histories should remain practical. Meaning accumulates because people handle them over time.
Not every object must recur. The ones that do should move naturally.
________________

The Lost Sweater
Likely first location: School lost-and-found
Physical particulars: Worn cuff, repaired elbow, mismatched button, something ordinary left in a pocket
Possible first holder: Tessa or another child; final choice deferred
Possible movement: Claimed, misidentified, donated, repaired again, later recognized in another context
Pattern possibilities: Compression Loses the Person; Care Preserves Particulars
Constraint: The sweater must not function as a puzzle whose contents reveal the child’s “true” identity.
________________

The Recipe Card
Origin: Written by June’s mother, grandmother, or another earlier bakery worker
First known place: Bakery kitchen
Physical particulars: Stains, several handwritings, additions that do not all agree
Possible movement: Copied, amended, loaned for a fundraiser, scanned, partially damaged, passed to another generation
Pattern possibilities: Understanding Circulates; Structures Outlive Reasons; Commitment Leaves Room; Care Preserves Particulars
Constraint: The handwritten version must not automatically be treated as more authentic than every adaptation.
________________

The Warped Board
Origin: Ordinary stock selected for a repair, class, or public project
First known holder: Ben
Physical particulars: Rocks on a flat surface; grain or moisture history not visible from one face
Possible movement: Rejected, repurposed, cut into something smaller, used where the warp matters less
Pattern possibilities: Results Conceal the Game; Commitment Leaves Room
Constraint: The board is not “bad.” Its history and intended use determine whether it can serve.
________________

The Empty Chair
First location: Public meeting, classroom, council room, funeral, or bakery gathering
Possible movement: Literal chair reused in different settings, or recurring arrangement rather than one unique object
Pattern possibilities: Authority Follows Attention; Participation Creates Legitimacy
Constraint: Avoid making it a heavy-handed symbol of leadership or absence.
________________

The Community Quilt
Origin: Shared project connected to a specific town effort
First location: Community center
Physical particulars: Different stitches, fabrics, repair methods, and contributors
Possible movement: Displayed, stored, removed during renovation, repaired, used rather than merely viewed
Pattern possibilities: Participation Creates Legitimacy; Understanding Circulates; Care Preserves Particulars
Constraint: Shared creation must include disagreement, uneven contribution, and later maintenance.
________________

The Long Wrench
First holder: Ben or municipal crew
Use: Applying force to a stuck fitting, valve, or piece of infrastructure
Possible movement: Borrowed, misplaced, inherited with a job, replaced by a more efficient tool
Pattern possibilities: Responsibility Follows Leverage; Distance Conceals Consequence
Constraint: Its leverage should remain materially accurate rather than merely metaphorical.
________________

The Forgotten Key
Origin: Earlier use or renovation of the community center
First known place: Maintenance ring, drawer, hook, or mislabeled envelope
Possible movement: Ignored, tested, discarded, recovered, eventually matched to a door or control whose function has changed
Pattern possibilities: Structures Outlive Reasons; Bad News Reaches Power
Constraint: What it opens need not be dramatic. The significance lies in lost context, not hidden treasure.
________________

The Pencil Line
First use: Marking wood, revising a map, altering a recipe, or planning a cut
Possible movement: Erased, redrawn, cut across, left visible on a finished surface
Pattern possibilities: Commitment Leaves Room
Constraint: Preserve the distinction between commitment and irreversibility.
________________

The Worn Rug
First location: Town Hall, community center, bakery, school office, or library
Physical particulars: Damage hidden or normalized by repetition and furniture placement
Possible movement: Rotated, patched, removed, revealing the floor beneath
Pattern possibilities: Distance Conceals Consequence; Structures Outlive Reasons
Constraint: The hidden wear must result from repeated use or pressure, not serve as a contrived revelation.
________________

The Painted-Over Crack
First location: Community center or another public building
Physical particulars: Multiple coats, recurring line, underlying movement not corrected by surface repair
Possible movement: Reported, dismissed, monitored, cosmetically repaired, later investigated
Pattern possibilities: Bad News Reaches Power; Distance Conceals Consequence
Constraint: Do not require catastrophic collapse. The pattern concerns whether the warning reaches capacity to act.
________________

The Patched Coat
First holder: TBD
Physical particulars: Repair shaped to one person’s particular use and wear
Possible movement: Repaired more than once, handed down, altered, outgrown, retained
Pattern possibilities: Care Preserves Particulars; Compression Loses the Person
Constraint: Avoid reducing care to sentimentality. The repair should require accurate attention.
________________

The Unsigned Form
First location: School or Town Hall
Use: A decision prepared by a system but not yet completed
Possible movement: Left overnight, circulated for more information, eventually signed or replaced
Pattern possibilities: Care Preserves Particulars; Commitment Leaves Room; Bad News Reaches Power
Constraint: Leaving it unsigned is not automatically virtuous. Delay can preserve judgment or evade responsibility.

X. Pattern Echoes
Each pattern receives one featured chapter and should appear at least twice elsewhere:
   * once as earlier foreshadowing or ambient presence
   * once as later recurrence or consequence
Echoes should remain brief unless they become necessary to another chapter’s central pattern.
Provisional Echo Map
Compression Loses the Person
   * Featured: Chapter 1
   * Earlier or lateral echo: Categorized orders or customer assumptions in Chapter 2
   * Later echo: A municipal map omits lived use in Chapter 10 or 11
Understanding Circulates
   * Featured: Chapter 2
   * Earlier echo: Nora learns something from another observer in Chapter 1
   * Later echo: Multiple partial warnings must be assembled in Chapter 11 or 12
Results Conceal the Game
   * Featured: Chapter 3
   * Earlier echo: A school result is treated as explanation in Chapter 1
   * Later echo: A successful public project hides relocated costs in Chapter 10
Authority Follows Attention
   * Featured: Chapter 4
   * Earlier echo: Informal influence at the bakery or school
   * Later echo: Daniel’s visibility produces expectations in Chapters 6 and 10
Participation Creates Legitimacy
   * Featured: Chapter 5
   * Earlier echo: Shared knowledge in the bakery
   * Later echo: A process loses trust after participation is removed in Chapter 8
Responsibility Follows Leverage

   * Earlier echo: Small forms of authority in Chapters 1 or 4
   * Later echo: Daniel cannot claim distance from the consequences in Chapter 10

   * Featured: Chapter 7

   * Later echo: Accelerated systems preserve old assumptions in Chapters 8 and 11

XII. Reader Discoveries That Remain Open
These are not drafting gaps. They are permanent philosophical openness.
   * Who is Tessa completely?
   * Does the town become wiser, or merely differently attentive?
   * Which consequences never return visibly to those who caused them?
   * Which traditions preserved their purposes, and which preserved only their forms?
   * Which relationships were understood differently by each person inside them?
   * When did Daniel’s public responsibility begin: when he was elected, when people first listened, or earlier?
   * How much of Ben’s practical knowledge can be transferred?
   * What does June lose when a practice survives in a new form?
   * Which judgments Nora corrected, and which she never recognized as judgments?
   * What realities remain outside the book’s frame?
The manuscript should not close these questions merely to provide emotional completion.

XIII. Production Notes: What the Town Must Not Become
This section governs drafting only. It is not manuscript prose.
The town is not:
   * an allegory
   * a model community
   * a political microcosm designed to represent the nation
   * “every town”
   * a disguised account of one real hometown
   * a nostalgic fantasy
   * a utopia
   * a place in inevitable decline
   * a puzzle whose pieces all converge
   * a machine built to prove the twelve patterns
It is one town with its own partial history.
Drafting Test
When a scene becomes too convenient, ask:
Is this happening because it belongs to the town’s life, or because the argument needs an example?
When the answer is primarily the argument, revise the scene until the event has practical causes, competing interpretations, and consequences that exceed its conceptual use.
