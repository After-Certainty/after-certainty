# Perspectives We Do Not Naturally Possess

> What responsibilities accompany judgment?

Judgment is the responsible use of limited perspectives. Provisional commitment teaches how to act when proof runs out. Neither answer completes the question this chapter must ask: **who** bears the cost of what you chose—and what you still owe them when your sight was never wide enough to see them clearly.

Responsibility is not the same as guilt. It is the work of remaining **answerable** to people affected by a decision you made from somewhere. That answerability does not end when the vote is recorded or the order is signed. It includes what you were willing to learn before you decided, and what you are willing to learn after.

The housing committee had spent three months inside the packet. Elevator repairs, facade work, reserve projections, contractor bids. The chair knew how to read a spreadsheet the way some people know how to read weather. Most of the board had lived in the building for years—owners with stable incomes, familiar routines, the same entrance they used without thinking about steps.

The tenant who came to public comment did not come to perform outrage. She used a wheelchair. She said the proposed “interim” ramp would deposit residents into a side alley where garbage trucks reversed at dawn. She said the board had described the plan as **temporary** in the same tone people use for inconveniences they do not expect to carry themselves. She asked, quietly, why temporary solutions so often become permanent for the people who had no seat when the decision was made.

Several board members heard an attack. One heard a **gap**—a part of the building’s life their perspective had never needed to include. The chair had voted in good faith from the materials in front of her. Good faith was not the whole of responsibility.

## What responsibility requires

If judgment is how partial sight becomes action, responsibility is how action remains tied to the lives it touches.

That tie has at least three faces.

**Fair process.** People deserve to know how a decision was made, what was weighed, what was ignored, and who had voice before the outcome landed. Process does not guarantee wisdom. It reduces the cruelty of surprise and the arrogance of deciding about people without them in the room.

**Proportionate care.** The stronger the action, the stronger the obligation to widen the sight you act from—not to achieve neutrality, but to reduce preventable harm. A ramp is not a symbol. It is a path someone will use or be barred from using while the board moves on to the next agenda item.

**Repair when wrong.** Revisability is not only for forecasts and protocols. It is for relationships and structures damaged by earlier partial judgment. Responsibility includes returning when the world shows you what your perspective missed—not to perform humility, but to **change** what can still be changed.

These are not additions to judgment after the fact. They are part of what it means to judge responsibly under finitude. You cannot see everything. You can still ask whether the people most affected by your blind spots were treated as part of the reality you were deciding about.

## Perspectives we do not naturally possess

Part I showed how perspective is built—attention, memory, interpretation, trust, structure. Each layer solves a problem and leaves a remainder. Part II asks how to decide inside that remainder without pretending it away.

One discipline remains: learning from **perspectives you do not naturally possess**.

Not as diversity theater. Not as a box checked before the vote. Not as the fantasy that one conversation with someone unlike you erases your own situated view. **Foreign**, in this book, means simply **not default**—not the angle your body, role, history, or incentives would reach for without effort.

This obligation is not only moral. It is **epistemic**. People who bear the consequences of a decision often notice different parts of reality—not because suffering grants infallibility, but because consequences reveal features invisible from elsewhere. The tenant knew the alley at dawn because the alley was her morning. The board knew reserve projections because reserves were their language. Responsible judgment asks which parts of reality only become visible from where the costs land.

The board member who walks the alley at dawn is not becoming the tenant. She is borrowing a partial view long enough to let it **count**—the way trust extends what one person can verify, but here the extension is moral rather than informational. She is practicing a form of judgment Part I prepared and Part II now names: you judge from somewhere, and responsible judgment asks what sits outside your somewhere that still belongs to the decision.

This is difficult for a reason that has nothing to do with virtue signaling. Foreign perspectives often arrive as **correction**, and correction threatens identity. They can require revising a story you already committed to—a budget already balanced, a policy already defended, a self-image already polished. Waiting is also a decision. So is refusing to hear.

Intentional incorporation is a **practice**: who gets consulted before the choice hardens; which routines force you out of the packet and into the consequence; what dissent is recorded rather than smoothed away; what would make you change course if you heard it early enough to matter. None of this removes your responsibility. It widens the perspective **you** must answer for.

## When responsibility becomes performance

Seeking foreign perspectives becomes dangerous when it is treated as **completion**—as if one hearing, one working group, one advisory seat satisfied the obligation to see.

Token consultation is its own costume: the appearance of openness worn to avoid changing the outcome. The board that listens to public comment and votes unchanged without explanation has not incorporated a foreign perspective. It has **displayed** one. The leader who asks for feedback after the decision is already made is often asking for absolution, not information.

Responsible judgment refuses that shortcut. It distinguishes hearing from **weighing**, and weighing from **acting**. It accepts that incorporation may slow a process urgency wants to rush—and that slowing is sometimes the cost of not deciding about people as if they were abstractions on someone else’s map.

The tenant’s alley was not the only perspective the board lacked. It was the one that arrived in time to matter. Responsibility includes building systems—inside a committee, a profession, a life—where such perspectives arrive **before** “temporary” becomes someone else’s permanent.

## What even excellent judgment cannot carry

The chair revised the ramp plan. She walked the route. She documented the truck schedule. She did not become unbiased. She did not see the building from every tenant’s life at once—the night worker, the parent with the stroller, the resident who feared the alley for reasons unrelated to garbage. She remained one person with one partial view, now slightly less narrow than before.

That is often the honest outcome of responsible individual judgment: not omniscience, but **movement**—a decision less blind than it would have been, owned more fully because it was made with fewer excuses about what could not have been known.

Even then, limits remain.

No individual can inhabit every perspective that bears on a complex choice. No amount of personal virtue replaces the need for others who notice what you still miss—not as a moral failure, but as a structural fact about finite minds. The most disciplined judge, the most careful emergency manager, the most attentive oncologist still decides from somewhere. Borrowed sight helps. It does not turn one person into many.

The goal was never to become the person who could see everything. The goal was to become the kind of person who knows when others must help you see.

Part II began when action could no longer wait for neutral sight. It ends where individual responsibility meets its **horizon**: you can judge well for a finite being, and still not see enough alone.

Which opens the next question—not whether you tried, but whether excellent judgment, even at its best, can ever be **complete**.
