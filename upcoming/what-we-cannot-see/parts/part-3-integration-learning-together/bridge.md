# The Limits of Individual Wisdom

> Why does even excellent individual judgment remain incomplete?

Part II asked three linked questions. **What is judgment?** The responsible use of limited perspectives—not neutrality, but a recommendation someone is willing to own. **How do we judge when certainty is impossible?** Through provisional commitment—proportionality and revisability while the fog remains. **Who do we answer to?** The people who bear the costs of what we chose from somewhere—including perspectives we do not naturally possess, and obligations that continue after the vote is recorded.

That arc can be lived well. The oncologist who names what she knows and what she does not. The emergency manager who moves before the picture clears and stays answerable afterward. The housing chair who walks the alley and changes the ramp plan. These are not failures of effort. They are judgment at its most disciplined.

And still incomplete.

Not because anyone lacked virtue. Because **finite minds** remain finite even at their best. One person can widen what they must answer for—fair process, proportionate care, repair when wrong, borrowed sight from lives the packet never pictured. One person cannot inhabit every perspective that bears on a complex choice. The night worker, the parent with the stroller, the resident who feared the alley for reasons unrelated to garbage: each notices a part of the building’s reality the chair could not carry alone, even after she listened in good faith.

The limit is structural, not moral. It is the same finitude Part I traced through attention and trust, now meeting decisions that outgrow any single vantage point. When consequences are shared, no disciplined individual holds the whole picture. Others keep seeing what you miss—not as a rebuke, but as a fact about partial sight. Those who bear costs often notice features invisible from elsewhere. Suffering does not grant infallibility. Consequences still reveal what a spreadsheet was never built to carry.

Integration is not optional decoration on personal excellence. It follows from the horizon Part II reached.

The goal was never to become the person who could see everything. The goal was to become the kind of person who knows when others must help you see.

Part I showed why no perspective is complete. Part II showed how finite people should judge anyway. Part III asks what becomes possible once we stop asking individuals to do work that only many perspectives together can accomplish—how finite perspectives become collective wisdom, and how societies design processes that improve judgment without pretending anyone possesses the whole.

> Why do other people keep seeing things I miss?

That is where the inquiry goes next.
