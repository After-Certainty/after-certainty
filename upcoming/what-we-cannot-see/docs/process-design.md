# What We Cannot See — Process Design

Planning document for Part III and the book's culminating argument. Collects ideas for **designed epistemologies**—how societies organize knowing when individuals cannot see everything.

**Planning term:** *designed epistemologies* — do not use as reader-facing jargon unless earned in prose. Reader-facing: institutions, practices, methods, processes.

## Culminating question

Given that individuals have finite perspectives, how should we organize ourselves so that we can know more together than we can alone?

Science, engineering, medicine, journalism, constitutional government, and well-run software organizations are all attempts to answer this question—not by creating unbiased people, but by designing processes that integrate partial perspectives over time.

## New conceptual principle (non-negotiable)

The book does **not** argue that bias can be eliminated from individuals.

It argues that humans have finite perspectives and therefore necessarily engage in selective cognition. Bias is one important manifestation of that selective process.

However, societies can design **processes** that reduce the effects of individual bias without assuming unbiased people exist.

This distinction prevents readers from concluding that because bias is unavoidable, nothing can be improved.

## Four levels (always distinguish)

| Level | What it is | What it is not |
|-------|------------|----------------|
| **1. Individual cognition** | How finite perspectives take shape; selective work of noticing, remembering, interpreting | Something debiasing training can remove |
| **2. Individual judgment** | Responsible use of partial perspectives when deciding | Neutrality, omniscience, or escape from perspective |
| **3. Collective integration** | Many partial views constructing a larger model | Compromise, averaging, or false equivalence |
| **4. Institutional process design** | Deliberate structures that integrate perspectives and create feedback | A claim that institutions are unbiased |

**Flow:** Cognition → Judgment → Integration → Process design

Parts I–III map roughly to levels 1–3, with level 4 as Part III's deeper subject—not integration alone, but **how integration is designed, sustained, and corrected**.

## Process vs person

| Person-level claim | Process-level claim |
|--------------------|---------------------|
| Individuals cannot become unbiased | Processes can reduce unchecked dominance of any one perspective |
| Bias is structural to finite cognition | Error correction can be structural to institutions |
| Responsibility remains individual | Improvement can be institutional |
| Partial sight is permanent | Collective sight can be iteratively improved |

**Guardrail:** Never imply that process design fixes individuals. Process design compensates for finitude—it does not repeal it.

## Why institutions exist

Institutions exist partly because individual objectivity is impossible.

They preserve memory across generations, distribute attention across roles, slow decisions that require scrutiny, and create records that outlive any single observer. They also accumulate blind spots—but that is why **designed** feedback matters, not merely accumulation.

The impossibility of individual objectivity is precisely why humanity has developed institutions and practices that integrate many partial perspectives over time. This strengthens the core thesis; it does not weaken it.

## Correcting for bias without assuming objectivity

Processes do not eliminate bias. They:

- Integrate multiple perspectives
- Create feedback mechanisms
- Reduce the likelihood that any one perspective dominates unchecked
- Make error visible before it compounds
- Allow revision when outcomes contradict expectation

Examples (Part III material):

- Scientific replication and peer review
- Randomized controlled trials and double blinding
- Adversarial collaboration
- Engineering design reviews
- Code review and security review
- Incident postmortems and morbidity conferences
- Aviation checklists and safety reporting systems
- Legal appeals and adversarial procedure
- Democratic checks and balances
- Organizational learning loops

Each assumes partial observers—not neutral ones.

## Feedback loops

Healthy process design builds loops where:

- Outcomes return as information
- Disconfirmation has a path
- Dissent can be recorded without instant ostracism
- Failure teaches the system, not only the individual
- Success does not silence inquiry

Weak loops: metrics without context, authority without appeal, consensus without dissent, learning without memory.

## Error correction

Error correction is not the absence of bias. It is a **designed response** to bias—distributed across people, methods, and time.

Science: hypothesis, test, replicate, revise.  
Engineering: review, test, fail safely, postmortem.  
Medicine: second opinion, rounds, adverse event reporting.  
Democracy: opposition, appeal, election, public record.

## Distributed cognition

No single mind holds the whole model. Process design **distributes** cognition across:

- Roles (pilot / copilot / controller)
- Methods (blind review / replication)
- Artifacts (checklists, logs, archives)
- Time (appeals, historical record)
- Machines (instruments, models—as partial perspectives)

## Scientific methods as institutional design

Science is not "unbiased individuals doing experiments." It is a **designed epistemology**: many partial observers, shared methods, public record, replication, peer criticism.

Ch 10 material. Connect to Ch 11 (engineering/medicine) as parallel designed epistemologies.

## Engineering review processes

High-reliability domains treat review as part of knowing, not paperwork after knowing:

- Design review before build
- Red team / blue team
- Failure mode analysis
- Incident investigation without blame-as-default

Ch 11 material.

## Democratic checks and balances

Democracy is not majority-as-truth. It is institutional design under persistent disagreement: separation of powers, appeal, record, opposition, periodic reassessment.

Ch 12 material. Pair with organizational learning (institutions that remember and forget).

## AI as another perspective

AI is not an objective observer. It is another partial perspective—trained on partial data, optimized for partial objectives, integrated (or not) by human judgment.

Ch 13 material. Seed in Part I Technological bias (Ch 5).

Process design question for AI: how do we integrate machine partiality without treating it as oracle or dismissing it as noise?
