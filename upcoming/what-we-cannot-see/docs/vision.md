# What We Cannot See — Vision

## Purpose

Help readers understand why every perspective is necessarily partial, how individuals make responsible judgments despite those limitations, and why integration allows groups to construct understanding that exceeds any single person's perspective.

This is **not** a book about eliminating bias.

It is a book about understanding the role bias plays in finite cognition, how judgment operates under uncertainty, and why integration is necessary for collective intelligence.

## Central question

If no one can see everything, how can individuals and societies make wise decisions?

## Core thesis (summary)

Every perspective is partial.

Bias is not primarily a flaw. Bias is how finite minds allocate limited attention.

Every bias reveals something. Every bias obscures something.

Judgment is the responsible use of limited perspectives.

Integration is the process by which many incomplete perspectives become more complete understanding.

See [`thesis.md`](thesis.md) for full articulation.

## Reader experience

The reader should never feel lectured.

The reader should feel like they are slowly discovering a hidden structure that has always existed.

The goal is not agreement.

The goal is that readers begin noticing these patterns in everyday life.

## Tone

Curious. Thoughtful. Restrained.

Never preach. Never mock. Never reduce complex issues to heroes and villains.

Always assume people are adapting to limited information.

## What this book is not

- A debiasing handbook
- Implicit "bias = bad" morality tale
- Culture-war ammunition
- Management consulting for "unconscious bias training"
- A trust or diversity guide (those are application domains, not this book's scope)

## What this book is

- Foundational epistemics: why partial sight is universal
- A three-movement arc: bias → judgment → integration
- Observational discovery, not rhetorical certainty
- One continuous inquiry across the manuscript
