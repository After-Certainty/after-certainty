# **Introduction**

## **Why Judgment No Longer Coordinates Action**

Most institutions did not lose their moral compass. They lost their
ability to rely on judgment.

Judgment works when people share meaning. It depends on common reference
points, enough proximity to see consequences, and enough time to explain
tradeoffs. For a long time, those conditions held. Leaders could decide,
explain why, and expect disagreement to remain intelligible rather than
adversarial.

That world no longer exists at scale.

Modern institutions span countries, cultures, time zones, legal regimes,
and audiences with conflicting values. Decisions now travel farther and
faster than the explanations meant to justify them. A judgment that
sounds reasonable in one context looks arbitrary, biased, or reckless in
another. The same explanation that reassures one group inflames another.

This is not a failure of ethics. It is a failure of coordination.

As organizations grew, judgment stopped working as a shared language. It
did not disappear, but it no longer coordinated action safely. Leaders
learned that even careful reasoning could not survive distance, speed,
and exposure.

So institutions adapted.

They began to rely on signals instead of stories. They replaced
explanations with metrics, rules, and targets that could travel intact
across difference. Incentives entered first as translators, turning
complex values into something legible and repeatable. Over time, they
took on a second role. They stopped translating moral aims and started
substituting for them.

This shift did not require anyone to stop caring about right and wrong.
It required decisions that could survive scrutiny.

Consider large-scale corporate layoffs. When companies such as Google
and Meta reduced their workforces in 2022 and 2023, leaders did not
defend those decisions by appealing to personal judgment about
individual circumstances. They emphasized consistency, criteria, and
process. Performance bands, role redundancy, and organizational
alignment became the public justification.[^doc-ref-12-source] The companies did not deny
harm. They defined fairness procedurally, because procedure survives
audit better than judgment.

The same substitution appears in healthcare. Hospitals do not claim that
discharge targets express compassion. They claim they express efficiency
and quality. Length-of-stay benchmarks and readmission penalties now
define “good care” in ways that survive payer review and regulatory
oversight.[^doc-ref-34-source] Clinicians still exercise judgment, but the system no
longer accepts judgment as its moral language.

Technology platforms followed a similar path. Large platforms did not
replace editorial judgment with engagement metrics because they believed
popularity equals virtue. They did so because editorial judgment
collapsed under accusations of bias and censorship once content
moderation operated at global scale. Engagement and relevance metrics
offered a defensible alternative.[^doc-ref-56-source]

In each case, incentives did not enter as expressions of value. They
entered as tools of survival.

Metrics, targets, and rules share three advantages that judgment no
longer reliably offers. They remain legible across difference. They
repeat without explanation. They protect institutions after the fact. A
spreadsheet withstands scrutiny better than a story. A threshold
deflects blame more effectively than a reasoned argument.

Moral language did not vanish. It moved.

Institutions still speak about fairness, quality, responsibility, and
value. They simply speak about them indirectly, through what they reward
and punish. Over time, those rewards stop pointing back to underlying
values. They become the values the system can articulate.

That displacement leaves residue. People inside institutions still make
judgments. They still feel when outcomes cause harm or violate
responsibility. But institutions do not absorb that tension. They push
it downward, onto individuals, while presenting neutrality at the top.

The result is familiar. Decisions feel inevitable rather than chosen.
Accountability feels procedural rather than owned. People feel complicit
without feeling responsible.

This book examines that pattern across domains. It does not argue that
incentives are evil or that metrics should disappear. It argues
something narrower and more unsettling: when judgment can no longer
coordinate action, institutions rely on incentives to decide for them.
Over time, those incentives become the moral language institutions can
still speak.

That shift explains why so many systems continue to function while
leaving the people inside them morally exhausted.

Key claim:

Institutions did not abandon judgment because it failed morally, but
because it failed operationally.
