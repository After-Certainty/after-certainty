# **Chapter 2**

## **Engagement as a Theory of Value**

Central question:

When does attention stop being a signal and start being a definition of
worth?

### **1. The Original Moral Aim: Connection and Information**

Large technology platforms began with simple, intelligible aims.

They promised connection. They promised access to information. They
promised to lower the cost of communication and expand the reach of
ideas. These goals carried implicit moral claims: connection is good,
information empowers, and expression deserves space.

Early platforms relied on editorial judgment to support those aims.
Engineers and product teams made choices about ranking, moderation, and
presentation based on perceived quality and user benefit. Those choices
were imperfect, but they were legible. Someone could explain why a piece
of content appeared where it did.

As platforms scaled, that explanation stopped coordinating action.

### **2. Why Editorial Judgment Collapsed at Scale**

Editorial judgment does not scale cleanly.

When platforms began serving millions—and then billions—of users across
cultures, languages, and political contexts, every ranking decision
carried moral weight. A choice that looked reasonable to one audience
looked biased or censorious to another.

Public scrutiny intensified. Journalists, governments, and users
demanded explanations for why certain content spread and other content
did not. Internal reasoning, once informal and iterative, became
evidence in regulatory hearings and public controversy.

In this environment, judgment became risky.

To say, “We judged this content to be valuable,” required defending a
value claim across audiences that did not share values. To say, “We
judged this content to be harmful,” required accepting responsibility
for defining harm globally.

Explanation itself became a liability.

So platforms adapted.

### **3. Incentive Substitution: Engagement and Time on Platform**

As editorial judgment lost legitimacy, platforms turned to signals that
did not require shared interpretation.

They measured engagement.

Clicks, likes, shares, comments, watch time, and session length became
primary inputs for ranking systems. These metrics did not claim to
represent truth, quality, or virtue. They claimed to represent behavior.

That distinction mattered.

If people interact with content, the system can treat that interaction
as evidence of relevance without asserting anything about meaning.
Engagement allows platforms to say, “This is what people respond to,”
rather than, “This is what we believe.”

Companies such as Meta and Google have repeatedly described their
ranking systems in these terms: algorithms surface content based on
signals of relevance, not editorial preference.¹² Internal documents and
public testimony show that engagement metrics play a central role in
those systems.³⁴

Engagement began as a proxy. Over time, it became a definition.

What the system promotes becomes what the system treats as valuable—not
because the platform endorses it, but because the platform no longer
speaks in any other moral language.

### **4. Moral Residue: Amplification Without Endorsement**

This substitution creates a distinctive moral residue.

Platforms amplify content without endorsing it. They insist on the
distinction, and formally, it is correct. Amplification reflects
engagement, not approval.

But amplification has consequences regardless of intent.

When systems repeatedly promote content that provokes outrage, fear, or
polarization, they shape public attention and discourse. Users
experience this as influence, not neutrality. Creators adapt to what the
system rewards. Attention reinforces itself.

Platform leaders often acknowledge this tension while denying
authorship. They describe outcomes as emergent rather than chosen. The
system did not intend harm; it optimized signals.

This explanation protects the institution. It does not absorb the moral
cost.

That cost appears elsewhere: in degraded trust, distorted information
environments, and users who feel manipulated without being able to
identify a clear decision-maker.

### **5. Why Neutrality Becomes the Defense**

Faced with these outcomes, platforms rarely claim moral authority. They
claim neutrality.

Neutrality does not mean absence of effect. It means absence of
judgment.

By grounding decisions in engagement metrics, platforms position
themselves as mirrors rather than editors. They reflect user behavior
back to users at scale. When challenged, they point to the data.

This defense survives scrutiny.

A leader who says, “We promoted this content because people engaged with
it,” does not need to explain why the content deserved attention. The
system answered that question in advance. A leader who says, “We
believed this content was valuable,” must defend a belief.

Neutrality becomes the safest posture once judgment stops coordinating
action.

### **The Pattern Repeats**

Technology platforms did not abandon their original aims. They still
speak about connection, information, and expression. But those aims no
longer organize decisions directly.

Engagement does.

Attention stops being a signal of interest and becomes a theory of
value. What spreads becomes what matters. What holds attention becomes
what the system treats as worthy of space.

This does not require bad faith. It requires scale.

Platforms continue to function. Content continues to flow. But moral
responsibility diffuses. Judgment moves inward. Institutions operate.
Individuals argue about outcomes no one fully owns.

That is the tradeoff.
