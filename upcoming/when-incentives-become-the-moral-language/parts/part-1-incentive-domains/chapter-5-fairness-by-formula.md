# **Chapter 5**

## **Fairness by Formula**

Central question:

When does fairness become something you compute rather than something
you own?

### **1. Judgment Before Scale**

Layoffs, promotions, and discipline decisions once carried visible moral
weight.

A manager who let someone go could be asked—not only by HR, but by
colleagues and communities—whether the decision was fair. Fairness meant
more than legality. It meant proportion, context, and recognition of what
a person contributed.

Organizations still speak about values: respect, integrity, opportunity.
Those words imply that judgment matters. Someone is supposed to weigh
circumstances, explain decisions, and absorb at least part of the moral
cost.

As firms grew, diversified, and operated across jurisdictions, that
language stopped coordinating action as reliably as before.

### **2. Why Discretion Became Indefensible**

Judgment did not fail because managers stopped caring about people. It
became risky because discretion scales poorly under scrutiny.

A single layoff can trigger lawsuits, media coverage, union action, and
internal unrest. Variation between teams looks like favoritism. A
decision that seems humane in one context appears discriminatory in
another. Legal frameworks demand consistency; employees demand
transparency; shareholders demand efficiency.

In this environment, individualized judgment creates exposure.

To say, “I let this person go because of my assessment of performance
and context,” invites demands for proof that are difficult to produce
later. To say, “This decision followed the process,” shifts
responsibility to a system.

So HR functions adapted.

### **3. Incentive Substitution: Performance Bands and Redundancy Matrices**

Over time, workforce decisions increasingly relied on instruments designed
to standardize fairness.

Three mechanisms matter most.

First, performance management systems. Rating scales, forced
distributions, and calibration sessions translate complex work into
comparable scores.[^c5-ref-1-peter-cappelli-and-anna] These systems allow leaders to justify decisions by
reference to documented evaluations rather than contested judgment.

Second, redundancy frameworks. Large layoffs are often executed through
matrices that combine role criticality, cost centers, and performance
bands.[^c5-ref-2-us-worker-adjustment-and] The matrix produces a list. The list produces coverage for
boards and regulators.

Third, procedural compliance layers. Legal review, documentation
requirements, and severance formulas ensure decisions survive challenge.
Fairness becomes adherence to process—timelines met, notices delivered,
packages calculated.

None of these mechanisms claim that harm disappears. They claim that harm
was administered correctly.

A “fair” layoff becomes one that followed the model. These measures
travel across regions. They survive audits. They protect institutions.

They also replace the moral language of fairness with the administrative
language of defensibility.

### **4. Moral Residue: Harm Without Authorship**

Managers and HR professionals notice the substitution immediately.

They still judge who is struggling, who is essential, and who deserves
another chance. When those judgments conflict with matrix outputs, they
experience moral strain—not because they oppose efficiency, but because
the system cannot absorb their reasoning.

Employees receive decisions as opaque. A rating becomes destiny. A
spreadsheet row becomes a life disrupted. The institution speaks in
process; the individual experiences consequence without a person who
will own it.

Research on organizational justice consistently shows that procedural
fairness matters—but that people also need accounts they can believe.[^c5-ref-3-jerald-greenberg-organizational-justice]
When process replaces explanation, trust erodes even when rules are
followed.

The system does not record this erosion as unfairness. It records
compliance.

### **5. Fairness as Procedural Legitimacy**

Companies rarely say, “We outsourced moral responsibility to a model.”
They say, “We followed a rigorous, equitable process.”

That sentence does essential work. It converts a moral event into an
operational outcome. It distributes authorship across committees,
vendors, and templates.

At the system level, this language reduces legal risk and enables scale.
It allows global firms to act quickly under pressure.

At the individual level, the cost remains.

Managers carry memories of people reduced to scores. Employees carry the
sense that no one—not really—made the call. Fairness continues as a
value on the website while disappearing as a practice in the room.

Fairness and formula diverge.

Fairness requires judgment about what is owed in a specific case.
Formula requires consistency across cases the system can see.

When formula becomes the moral language of workforce decisions, care for
people does not vanish. It becomes private discomfort inside institutions
that can only speak in process.

Work continues.

Fairness becomes procedural.
