# **Chapter 3**

## **Publishing as Truth**

Central question:

What happens when knowledge production is evaluated by visibility rather
than meaning?

### **1. The Original Moral Aim: Truth-Seeking**

Academic and research institutions exist to produce reliable knowledge.

That aim is not merely technical. It carries a moral claim: some
questions matter, some methods discipline belief, and some findings should
change what communities treat as true. Peer review, replication, and
publication were never perfect. They were, for a long time, legible ways
of saying that truth-seeking had standards.

A journal article, a dissertation, or a funded study was supposed to
answer a question—not only generate a line on a CV. Disagreement was
expected. Slow correction was a feature, not a failure.

As research scaled across nations, disciplines, and funding regimes, that
language stopped coordinating action as cleanly as it once did.

### **2. Why Judgment Fractured**

Judgment did not fail because scholars stopped caring about truth. It
fractured because truth became expensive to defend at scale.

Disciplines splintered. Specialized journals multiplied. Reviewers
evaluated work through narrow methodological lenses that did not always
translate across fields. Political and institutional pressures made some
questions safer than others. A claim that was uncontroversial in one
subfield could be radioactive in another.

At the same time, universities faced mounting financial pressure.
Departments competed for rankings, grants, and prestige. Administrators
needed metrics that traveled: publication counts, citation indices,
grant dollars, impact factors. These measures were not designed to
capture meaning. They were designed to compare institutions.

In this environment, individualized judgment became risky.

To say, “This line of inquiry matters even if it will not publish
quickly,” required defending a value claim against systems that reward
velocity. To say, “This result should not be emphasized,” required
slowing a career in a market that punishes delay.

So research systems adapted.

### **3. Incentive Substitution: Publications, Citations, and Grants**

Over time, knowledge production increasingly defined quality through
outputs that could be counted.

Three mechanisms matter most.

First, publication volume. Hiring, promotion, and tenure committees treat
the number and venue of publications as proxies for contribution. A
“strong record” means a list that fits expected patterns, not
necessarily a body of work that changed understanding.

Second, citation metrics. Tools such as the h-index and journal impact
factors compress complex intellectual influence into single numbers.¹²
They reward visibility within citation networks—networks that can
amplify fashionable topics, reinforce existing paradigms, and undercount
work that is careful but slow.

Third, grant competitiveness. Funding agencies rank proposals using
review criteria that favor feasibility, measurable outcomes, and
alignment with strategic priorities.³ Proposals that promise clear
deliverables on short timelines outperform exploratory work that might
fail in instructive ways.

None of these mechanisms claim to replace truth. They claim to measure
productivity.

A “successful” researcher becomes one who publishes frequently, collects
citations, and wins grants. These measures travel cleanly across
departments. They survive audits. They protect institutions in league
tables.

They also replace the moral language of inquiry with the administrative
language of throughput.

### **4. Moral Residue: Replication Crises and Risk Aversion**

Researchers notice the substitution immediately.

Many still judge whether a question is important, whether a method fits,
and whether a finding should be trusted. When those judgments conflict
with incentive structures, they experience friction—not because they
oppose rigor, but because rigor and visibility are not the same thing.

Replication crises across psychology, medicine, and other fields exposed
how publication incentives can reward novel, positive results over
careful, disconfirming work.⁴⁵ Pre-registration and open data practices
attempt to repair the system, but they operate inside the same competitive
logic. Reform becomes another metric to satisfy.

Risk aversion follows. Early-career scholars learn which questions are
“fundable,” which journals are “safe,” and which conclusions are
career-preserving. Negative results remain difficult to publish. Slow,
foundational work looks like stagnation on a CV.

The system does not record this narrowing. It cannot.

Citation counts track attention. Grant totals track revenue. Neither
captures the study not pursued, the replication deferred, or the
skepticism suppressed to meet a deadline.

### **5. The Difference Between Productivity and Contribution**

Universities rarely say, “We rewarded visibility over understanding.”
They say, “This candidate has an excellent publication record.”

That sentence does essential work. It shifts responsibility from judgment
to tally. It turns a contested evaluation into a completed spreadsheet.

At the institutional level, this language is necessary. It allows
comparison across fields that do not share methods or audiences. It
reduces disputes about merit. It keeps hiring pipelines moving.

At the individual level, the cost remains.

Researchers carry the memory of work shaped to fit metrics, of findings
packaged for impact, and of conversations where “what counts” meant what
could be counted. They carry it quietly, because the institution cannot
hear it without reopening the standards it depends on.

Productivity and contribution diverge.

Productivity is what the system can measure: articles, citations, grants.
Contribution is what changes what a field knows, what a clinic does, or
what a public debate assumes—often slowly, often without immediate
credit.

When productivity becomes the moral language of research, truth-seeking
does not disappear. It becomes private labor inside a public machine that
speaks in counts.

Knowledge continues to be produced.

Meaning becomes harder to defend at scale.
