# **Chapter 4**

## **Targets Without Judgment**

Central question:

What does it mean to act morally when harm is global, delayed, and
uncertain?

### **1. The Original Moral Aim: Harm Reduction**

Climate policy and corporate responsibility programs begin with a
straightforward moral aim: reduce harm.

That aim is not abstract. It concerns lives disrupted by drought, cities
flooded by storms, workers exposed to unsafe conditions, and ecosystems
pushed past recovery. Responsibility here means limiting damage—not
only documenting it.

For decades, institutions spoke in the language of duty: prevention,
precaution, stewardship, accountability to future generations. Companies
published environmental reports. Governments negotiated emissions
limits. NGOs pressured firms to change practices.

The moral vocabulary was contested, but intelligible. Someone could ask
whether a policy actually reduced risk, whether a commitment was serious,
and whether those bearing costs were the same parties receiving benefits.

As harm became global, delayed, and statistically distributed, that
language stopped coordinating action as reliably as before.

### **2. Why Judgment Paralyzes Coordination**

Judgment did not fail because leaders stopped caring about the climate.
It became risky because harm could no longer be owned cleanly.

A factory closure reduces local emissions but shifts production elsewhere.
A net-zero pledge covers operational carbon while leaving supply-chain
emissions unresolved. An offset purchase claims equivalence between
tons avoided today and tons possibly removed decades later.

Each decision requires comparing incomparable futures. Uncertainty is not
an excuse; it is the condition. Moral responsibility without clear
causal chains invites litigation, activist scrutiny, and shareholder
pressure.

In this environment, saying “In my judgment, this path is responsible”
exposes institutions to charges of greenwashing, hypocrisy, or
negligence. Saying “We met the target” shifts the argument to
compliance.

So coordination adapted.

### **3. Incentive Substitution: Targets, Offsets, and Reporting Frameworks**

Over time, climate action increasingly defined seriousness through
measurable commitments that could be compared across firms and nations.

Three mechanisms matter most.

First, emissions targets. Net-zero pledges, science-based targets, and
national caps translate moral urgency into dated numerical goals.¹ They
allow institutions to announce alignment with global benchmarks without
resolving every downstream tradeoff.

Second, carbon markets and offsets. Voluntary and compliance markets
price carbon through credits, removals, and avoidance claims.² Offsets
enable organizations to report progress by purchasing reductions
elsewhere—sometimes real, sometimes contested.

Third, ESG reporting frameworks. Disclosure regimes require standardized
metrics on emissions, risk exposure, and governance practices.³
Investors use these reports to rank firms, allocate capital, and manage
reputational risk.

None of these mechanisms claim to eliminate harm. They claim to make
seriousness visible.

A “responsible” company becomes one that publishes targets, buys offsets,
and files compliant reports. These measures travel across jurisdictions.
They survive audits. They satisfy boards and regulators.

They also replace the moral language of harm reduction with the
administrative language of accounting.

### **4. Moral Residue: Displacement and Accounting Without Clarity**

Practitioners notice the substitution immediately.

Sustainability officers still judge whether commitments are credible,
whether projects help vulnerable communities, and whether reported
progress matches physical reality. When those judgments conflict with
reporting deadlines or investor expectations, they experience strain—not
because they oppose measurement, but because measurement is not
responsibility.

Offsets displace attention. A firm can meet near-term targets while
deferring structural change. Reporting frameworks aggregate complex
operations into scores that obscure local harm. Communities near
polluting facilities may see little improvement even as corporate
dashboards improve.

The system does not record this displacement. It cannot.

ESG ratings track disclosure quality. Markets track sentiment. Neither
captures the village still breathing contaminated air, the worker still
unprotected, or the policymaker who knows a target is technically met but
morally thin.

### **5. Why Seriousness Replaces Responsibility**

Institutions rarely say, “We chose accounting over harm reduction.” They
say, “We are on track to net zero.”

That sentence does essential work. It converts an ongoing moral argument
into a timeline with checkpoints. It signals seriousness without
requiring full ownership of consequences.

At the system level, this language enables coordination among actors who
do not share priorities. It allows capital markets to price risk. It
creates comparability.

At the individual level, the cost remains.

People inside institutions carry the gap between what metrics show and
what places experience. They carry the knowledge that seriousness can be
performed—targets announced, reports filed, panels attended—while harm
continues unevenly.

Responsibility requires judgment about tradeoffs that numbers cannot
settle. Seriousness requires meeting the next reporting cycle.

When seriousness becomes the moral language of climate action, concern
for harm does not vanish. It becomes private judgment inside systems
that can only speak in targets.

Action continues.

Accountability thins.
