# **Chapter 6**

## **Attention as Importance**

Central question:

What happens when what spreads is treated as what matters?

### **1. The Original Moral Aim: Informing the Public**

Journalism exists to inform the public.

That aim carries a moral claim: citizens need accurate, contextualized
information to make collective decisions. Editors once acted as stewards
of that need—choosing what deserved prominence, what required
investigation, and what could wait.

The language was imperfect. Bias, ownership, and access always shaped
coverage. Still, the institution could explain itself. A front page, a
lead story, or an investigative series was a judgment rendered in
public: this matters now.

As newsrooms digitized and distribution fragmented, that language stopped
coordinating action as cleanly as before.

### **2. Why Editorial Judgment Lost Legitimacy**

Editorial judgment did not fail because reporters stopped pursuing truth.
It lost institutional protection because the business model collapsed
and the audience fractured.

Advertising revenue migrated to platforms. Local papers shrank. National
outlets competed globally for attention. Every story faced not only
editorial standards but also analytics dashboards measuring clicks,
time on page, shares, and subscription conversions.

In this environment, saying “This story matters even if it will not
perform” required defending a value claim against metrics that determine
staffing and survival. Saying “This piece is performing” answered the
question in advance.

Newsrooms adapted.

### **3. Incentive Substitution: Clicks, Shares, and Velocity**

Over time, editorial priorities increasingly followed signals of
audience behavior.

Three mechanisms matter most.

First, real-time analytics. Editors and producers monitor traffic minute
by minute, adjusting headlines, placement, and follow-up coverage.¹ What
rises on the dashboard receives resources; what flatlines disappears.

Second, platform distribution. Social networks and search engines reward
velocity, emotional charge, and shareability.² Stories shaped for those
incentives travel farther—even when they sacrifice nuance.

Third, engagement-oriented formats. Newsletters, podcasts, and video
clips are evaluated by open rates, completion, and viral reach. Formats
that hold attention outperform formats that deepen understanding.

None of these mechanisms claim that informing the public no longer
matters. They claim that importance can be inferred from behavior.

A “successful” story becomes one that spreads. These measures travel
across desks. They justify budgets. They keep organizations alive.

They also replace the moral language of public interest with the
operational language of attention.

### **4. Moral Residue: Outrage Saturation and Context Collapse**

Journalists notice the substitution immediately.

Reporters still judge what is true, what is proportionate, and what harm
sensational coverage might cause. When those judgments conflict with
traffic goals, they experience friction—not because they oppose
audiences, but because audiences are not the same as the public interest.

Outrage saturates. Context collapses when complex stories are compressed
into headlines built for sharing. Trust declines even when individual
pieces are accurate, because the overall environment feels manipulative.

Studies of media trust document persistent skepticism toward institutions
that appear to chase clicks.³ The system records engagement. It does not
record the citizen who stops reading because news feels like a trap.

### **5. Why Relevance Becomes Self-Referential**

News organizations rarely say, “We followed traffic over importance.”
They say, “We cover what the audience wants.”

That sentence does essential work. It converts editorial judgment into
market feedback. It makes relevance self-referential: what matters is
what spreads; what spreads is what the system promotes.

At the institutional level, this language preserves revenue and reach. It
aligns newsrooms with measurable demand.

At the individual level, the cost remains.

Journalists carry stories they know deserved quieter treatment, and
stories they know deserved more. They carry the sense that the front
page is no longer a moral decision but a performance report.

Informing the public and capturing attention diverge.

When attention becomes the moral language of journalism, truth-telling
does not vanish. It competes inside a machine that speaks in metrics.

News continues.

Importance becomes what travels.
