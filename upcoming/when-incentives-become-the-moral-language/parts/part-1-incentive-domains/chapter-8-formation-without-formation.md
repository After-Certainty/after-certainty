# **Chapter 8**

## **Formation Without Formation**

Central question:

What happens when what cannot be measured is treated as optional?

### **1. The Original Moral Aim: Human Formation**

Education exists to form human beings—not only to transmit information.

Formation includes judgment, character, attention, and the capacity to
live with others. Parents send children to school hoping they will learn
to read and calculate, but also to become responsible, curious, and
capable of restraint.

Teachers once spoke in the language of development: maturity, discipline,
care, and the slow cultivation of habits. Report cards captured only part
of the work. The rest lived in classrooms, conversations, and
corrections that never became data.

As schooling became accountable to states, markets, and anxious
parents, that language stopped coordinating action as reliably as before.

### **2. Why Judgment Fragments Across Stakeholders**

Judgment did not fail because teachers stopped caring about students. It
fragmented because no single stakeholder could own the whole child.

Districts answer to legislatures. Principals answer to districts. Teachers
answer to evaluations. Parents answer to college prospects. Students
answer to tests. Each party demands evidence that its priorities were
served.

In this environment, saying “I judged this student to be growing in ways
the test cannot capture” invites skepticism. Saying “Scores improved”
closes the meeting.

Schools adapted.

### **3. Incentive Substitution: Test Scores and Graduation Rates**

Over time, education increasingly defined success through measurable
outcomes.

Three mechanisms matter most.

First, standardized testing. Federal and state accountability systems tie
funding, reputation, and intervention to performance on standardized
exams.[^c8-ref-1-us-department-of-education] Teachers orient instruction toward what is tested; what is not
tested shrinks.

Second, graduation and completion metrics. Rates function as moral
shorthand for institutional quality.[^c8-ref-2-national-center-for-education] Schools face pressure to graduate
students on time—even when support services, counseling, and remedial
work are underfunded.

Third, ranking and comparison regimes. Public school ratings, college
admissions statistics, and international benchmarks turn formation into
competition.[^c8-ref-3-organisation-for-economic-cooperation] Communities learn to treat education as a positional good.

None of these mechanisms claim that character no longer matters. They
claim that character cannot be managed without proxies.

A “successful” school becomes one that raises scores and graduation
rates. These measures travel across districts. They survive audits. They
satisfy boards.

They also replace the moral language of formation with the administrative
language of measurement.

### **4. Moral Residue: Character Without Curriculum**

Educators notice the substitution immediately.

Teachers still perceive curiosity, cruelty, courage, and fragility in
their students. When those perceptions conflict with metric pressure,
they experience moral strain—not because they oppose standards, but
because standards are not formation.

Test prep crowds out discussion. Counseling loads grow while pastoral care
is treated as extracurricular. Teachers burn out when they are asked to
form persons while evaluated as technicians of scores.[^c8-ref-4-rand-corporation-reports-on]

The system records proficiency bands. It does not record the student who
learned restraint, the class that learned disagreement without contempt,
or the teacher who spent a year on habits no rubric names.

### **5. The Quiet Disappearance of Formation Language**

Schools rarely say, “We stopped forming children.” They say, “We are data-
driven and student-centered.”

That sentence does essential work. It converts formation into service
delivery. It makes care sound measurable.

At the system level, this language secures accountability and equity
claims. It allows comparison across unequal communities.

At the individual level, the cost remains.

Parents carry the sense that something essential is missing—lessons in
attention, integrity, and common life—while report cards look fine.
Teachers carry the knowledge that their real work happens off the
spreadsheet.

Formation and measurement diverge.

Formation requires judgment about persons over time. Measurement requires
snapshots the system can aggregate.

When measurement becomes the moral language of education, formation does
not vanish. It becomes private labor in institutions that can only speak
in scores.

School continues.

Formation goes quiet.
