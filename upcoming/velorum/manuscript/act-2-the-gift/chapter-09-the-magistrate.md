# Chapter 9 - The Magistrate

Kestrel Gate rose out of the plain in layered stone and timber, bigger than anything Cael had seen outside storybooks. The outer wall was patched in fresh beams. Guards in grey tabards counted wagons as they came in.

By the time Cael and Riven reached the south gate, word had already outrun them. Two officers and a clerk met them. The clerk carried a slate and kept glancing up like he expected sparks.

"Lord Magistrate Halverin requests your presence," the clerk said. "Immediately."

Riven muttered, "Could at least ask if we're fucking hungry first."
Cael kept walking.

The city smelled like hot iron and old rain. Shutters were half down in daylight. Messengers ran between posts. In the square, someone had nailed up a board that read MISSING.

At the next corner, two bakers and a guard shouted over a ration board with numbers rubbed out and rewritten three times. A woman in flour-streaked sleeves pointed at the board and yelled that her line already wrapped the lane.

Their clerk didn't slow.

Halverin met them upstairs in a council room, windows on one wall, maps on the other, candles bleeding wax onto a crowded table.

He was thinner than Cael expected. Pale. Neat coat. Dark hair tied back. No rush in his body, even with the city coming apart under him.

"Cael. Riven." Halverin said their names like he'd practiced them. "Thank you for coming."

An aide coughed into his sleeve and kept writing.

Riven crossed his arms. "Didn't know we had options."

Halverin gave him a polite smile with no warmth in it. "You always have options. Some are just worse."

He gestured to the table. Pins marked raids and collapses around the trade roads. Cael stepped in and braced both hands on the edge.
River lines and ridge lines cut the map into sections. Once he saw it, he couldn't unsee it: breaks tightening around Kestrel Gate.
Chalk notes lined the margins in another hand: grain delayed, bridge pending, patrol missing. Cael read each one. Task. Delay. Cost.

"You've seen the roads," Halverin said. "What you're calling attacks are weak spots giving way."

Riven frowned. "Pressure from what?"

Halverin didn't answer straight. "From things already stretched thin."

Cael felt that land immediately. Bridges. Banks. Crowds right before they break.

Halverin watched him and nodded. "You understand quickly."

"I understand what happens when things fail," Cael said.

"Good." Halverin put two fingers on the map where the ring narrowed. "What happened to you in Ashfen wasn't random. It's rare, but known. Pairs touched by Velorum can steady breaks other people don't even see. If they go all the way in."

Riven laughed once, sharp. "Commit fully. Nice way to say no private fucking thought."

Halverin turned to him. Calm. Rehearsed. "Privacy is a luxury of less necessary lives."

Riven's laugh this time had no humor in it. "Print that on your broadsides."

One aide looked up. Halverin raised two fingers without looking; the aide dropped his eyes.

Riven's anger hit through the bond like heat. Cael felt it. He also felt his own colder reaction: if Halverin was right, then what they were losing could still be used.

He hated that thought as soon as it settled.

"What do you need from us?" Cael asked.
He kept his hands flat so no one saw the shake.

"I need people to see something steady," Halverin said. "They're frightened. Fear travels faster than facts. I need you on patrol, then in public. Show them the city still holds."

Riven looked between them. "You mean put on a show."
"I mean lead," Halverin said. "Or people die."

He crossed to a side table and opened an old woodcut: two oath-bound figures back to back in stylized flame.

"Legends last because they teach behavior," Halverin said. "People follow what they think will hold."

Below the woodcut sat a stack of fresh broadsides, one corner still wet.
Cael didn't need to read them. He could hear them in Halverin's tone: order is returning, stay in quarters, trust the sworn pair.

Riven's disgust scraped through the bond. Cael felt it, but the shape of the work still made sense to him: patrol routes, public calm, clear chain of command. Not fate. Work.

Halverin slid over another sheet, plain columns and names. Missing captains. Understrength gates. Civilians on bucket lines because there was no one else.

Cael scanned it once and saw why Halverin leaned on story language. The numbers were worse than the room sounded.

"How many can still hold a line?" Cael asked.

"On paper? Eighty-three."

"In reality?"

"Fifty on a good shift."

Riven clicked his tongue. "And you're burning two of them to escort us upstairs for theater."

"I'm using two so people see someone holding the line," Halverin said. "If they believe things are still standing, they act like they are."

He handed Cael a sector list and watch rotations. "First light start. Captain Merrow briefs you. I want impressions by nightfall."

Cael took the sheet. Warm from Halverin's hand.

South market at dawn. East barracks check. River postern sweep. Evening square presence.

At the bottom, one line was marked twice in heavier ink:
REPORT PERCEIVED BOND INSTABILITY IMMEDIATELY.

Cael folded the page once.

Riven saw it over his shoulder. "Perceived by who."

"Anyone with eyes," Halverin said. "Especially yours."

"We'll do it," Cael said.
Riven turned on him. "We?"
Cael met his eyes. "We're already in it."

He meant the city and the bond. Riven heard both.

Through the bond, Cael felt fear, resentment, and a tired loyalty that still hadn't broken.

Out loud, Riven only said, "Fine. But if he starts calling us saints, I'm leaving."
Halverin almost smiled. "No saints in Kestrel Gate. Only obligations."

That landed with Cael. Obligation was real. You could build on it.

Outside the room, a junior guard intercepted them with Merrow's seal on a wax-folded order.

"Captain says report before first light," he told Cael. "Bring your own chalk. We ran out in north watch."

Riven barked a laugh. "City's ending and they need fucking chalk."

The guard gave him a flat look. "How else you keep count."
Cael tucked the order into his belt and headed down.

On the square floor, a cobbler with pitch-black hands stepped in front of them.

"Can you really feel each other before a strike lands?"

"Sometimes," Cael said.

The cobbler nodded like that was enough and went back to his bench.

Riven watched him work and thought about what got called heroism and what actually kept a city standing.

Merrow's office was a converted counting room off the inner yard: half maps, half weapon racks.

She was broad-shouldered, close-cropped, and moved like every second cost something.

"You're late," she said.

"We were in council," Cael said.

"Then you know the shape." She jabbed three chalk circles on a slate map. "Market quarter twitchy. River postern compromised. East barracks overfull and understaffed. You two rotate those until first bell tomorrow."

Riven leaned over the slate. "And if all three go bad at once?"

Merrow met his eyes. "Then I pick which fire becomes a ruin."

Cael took the duty board. Under their names: rookies, volunteers, two veterans with bad knees, one courier barely old enough to shave.

One name already had a cross through it and a replacement in fresh chalk.

"We'll start river postern," Cael said.

Merrow nodded. "Good. It scares easy."

She handed Riven a short chalk stick. "When crowds start to drift, mark it. Time and place."

Riven frowned. "Drift."

"Where fear bunches before violence. You'll feel it."

Riven tucked the chalk into his belt and glanced at Cael. Through the bond, he could already feel Cael turning Merrow's words into route options.

When they stepped back into the corridor, light had gone blue. Square noise rose up the stairwell in broken pieces: hammer blows, ration arguments, one child crying until a door shut.

Riven walked half a step ahead, anger rolling off him through the bond.
"He talks like he owns the weather," Riven said.
Cael kept his eyes on the stairs. "He talks like someone trying to keep a city standing."
"By turning us into posters."

Cael waited until they hit the square cold. "If posters keep the gates from breaking, I can live with posters."

Riven said nothing. Their first rotation started at river postern before full dark.

The guards were young and jumpy, spears held too high, eyes cutting to every movement on the water. Cael walked the hinge line with a lantern, checking bolts and brace plates. Riven stayed above with Merrow's slate under one arm.

By first watch he had three marks: grain queue crowding fish stairs, shouting at east shelters, one alley choke near tanners' row.

At fish stairs, two men started shoving over ration order and one reached for a belt knife.

Riven dropped to the landing as Cael came in from quay side. They split the men before steel cleared leather.

"Home," Cael told the first man.

"No home left," the man shot back.

Cael didn't blink. "Then lower chapel floor. Move."

The man moved.

By second watch, Riven's chalk was a stub. Cael felt Riven's irritation every time someone called them saviors like that fixed anything. Riven felt Cael's fatigue grinding deeper into his shoulders.

Near dawn they reported to Merrow. She read the slate in silence and circled three locations.

"These break tomorrow if we don't get ahead," she said.

Cael nodded, already counting routes.

Riven stood with chalk dust in his palm while Merrow called for the next slate.
No one asked if he agreed.
