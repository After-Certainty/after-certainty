# Chapter 9 - The Magistrate

Kestrel Gate rose out of the plain in layered stone and timber, higher than anything Cael had seen outside old stories. The outer wall was scarred from repairs, beams newer than the stone they sat in. Guards in gray tabards watched the road with tired eyes and counted wagons as they came through.

By the time Cael and Riven reached the south gate, someone had already sent word ahead. They were met by two officers and a clerk who carried a slate and kept glancing up like he expected them to glow.

"Lord Magistrate Halverin requests your presence," the clerk said.
"Immediately," he said.

Riven muttered, "Could at least ask if we're hungry first."

Cael ignored the comment and followed the officers through the inner streets. Kestrel Gate smelled like hot iron, old rain, and too many people trying not to panic. Storefront shutters were half down even in daylight. Messengers moved at a run between watch posts. Near the central square, a list of names had been nailed to a board under the heading MISSING. Above it, laundry lines hung between buildings, pale cloth snapping in the crosswind. The city looked like a structure still standing only because everyone was bracing it by hand.

At the next corner they passed a chalked ration board where two bakers and a guard were arguing over grain allotments with numbers rubbed out and rewritten three times. A woman with flour on both sleeves pointed at the board and shouted that her queue already ran to the next lane.

The clerk escorting them did not slow.

Halverin received them in a council room above the square, bright windows on one wall, maps pinned on the other. Late light came in hard from the west side and left the far corners of the room in shadow. Wax dripped on two desk candles near the map table where aides had been writing and rewriting route marks.

He was thinner than Cael expected, pale, neatly dressed, dark hair pulled back at the nape. Nothing in his posture looked rushed, though the city below him was straining at the seams.

"Cael. Riven." Halverin said their names like he had tested how they sounded first. "Thank you for coming."
One of Halverin's aides coughed into his sleeve and kept writing.

Riven crossed his arms. "Didn't know we had options."

Halverin gave him a brief, polite smile that did not reach his eyes. "You always have options. You simply have fewer than most."

He gestured to the map table. Colored pins marked raids and collapses across the trade routes. Cael stepped closer before he meant to, bracing both hands on the table edge. Inked river lines and ridge marks cut the board into sectors, and the pattern was obvious once seen: breaks forming a ring around Kestrel Gate and tightening toward the city.

Small chalk marks ran along the map margins where someone had updated counts in a different hand: grain delayed, bridge repairs pending, patrol missing. Cael read them without meaning to. Every note was a task. Every task implied another failure if nobody took it.

"You've seen the instability on the roads," Halverin said. "What you call attacks are symptoms. Strain breaks what is already thin."

Riven frowned. "Pressure from what?"

Halverin did not answer that directly. "From misalignment, usually. From things held together by routine until routine fails."

Cael felt recognition in that word before he decided whether to trust it. Misalignment. It matched what he had felt in bridges, banks, and frightened crowds, the same wrong timing he and Riven could sometimes smooth by moving together.

Halverin watched him catch up and nodded once. "You understand quickly."

"I understand consequences," Cael said.

"Good. Then hear this clearly." Halverin placed two fingers on the map where the ring narrowed. "What happened to you in Ashfen is not random. It's a path. Rare, but documented. Pairs touched by Velorum can stabilize fault lines others cannot even perceive. But only if they commit to full alignment."

Riven gave a short, sharp laugh. "Full alignment. That's a clean phrase for not having a private thought."

Halverin turned to him with calm patience that felt rehearsed. "Privacy is a luxury of less necessary lives."

Riven's laugh this time had no humor in it. "Try printing that on your broadsides."

One of Halverin's aides looked up sharply, then back down at his notes when Halverin raised two fingers without looking.

Cael felt Riven's anger spike through the bond. He also felt his own reaction, colder and more useful: if Halverin was right, then what they were losing had a price and a use. That thought settled in him before he finished hating it.

"What do you need from us?" Cael asked.
He kept his hands flat on the table edge so nobody saw the shake.

Halverin's attention returned to him at once. "Kestrel Gate needs a visible center. People are frightened. Raids escalate when fear spreads faster than facts." He tapped the square outside the window. "I need you on patrol, then in public. Demonstrate control. Give them shape."

Riven looked between them. "You mean perform."

"I mean lead," Halverin said.

He moved to a side table and unfolded an old paper copy of a woodcut Cael had seen in cheap chapbook stalls, two oath-bound figures standing back to back in stylized flame. Cheap ink and clean lines over what the road actually did to bone and breath.

"Legends survive because they teach behavior," Halverin said. "People will follow what they believe holds."

Below the woodcut sat a stack of broadsides stamped with the city seal, still damp at one corner where ink had not fully set. Cael did not need to read them to know what they said. He could hear the shape of them in Halverin's tone: order is returning, stay in assigned quarters, trust the sworn pair.

Riven's disgust came through the bond like grit in Cael's teeth. Cael felt it, and still the framework in front of him made sense. Patrols, public reassurance, planned deployments, clear command chain. Not fate. Work.

Halverin set the woodcut down and slid a second sheet over, this one plain, all columns and names. Missing patrol captains. Gate sections understrength. Civilian volunteers with no training assigned to bucket lines because there was no one else left.

Cael scanned it once and understood why Halverin kept reaching for story language. The numbers alone were worse.

"How many can still hold a line," Cael asked, tapping the column that listed available watch by gate section.

"On paper?" Halverin said. "Eighty-three."

"In reality?"

Halverin met his eyes. "Fifty on a good shift."

Riven clicked his tongue. "And you're spending two of them to escort us upstairs for theater."

"I'm spending two to make sure the city sees where continuity lives," Halverin said, voice still level. "If people believe structure holds, they behave like it does."

Halverin handed Cael a list of sectors and watch rotations. "We start at first light. Captain Merrow will brief you on current threats. I want your impressions by nightfall."

Cael took the list. The paper was dry and warm from Halverin's hand.

The route blocks were precise: south market at dawn, east barracks check, river postern sweep, evening square presence. Not just defense. Staging.

At the bottom of the page, Halverin had marked one line twice in heavier ink: REPORT PERCEIVED BOND INSTABILITY IMMEDIATELY.

Cael read it and folded the sheet once.

Riven saw that line over his shoulder and felt his own pulse jump in annoyance. "Perceived by who."

"By anyone with eyes," Halverin said.

"We'll do it," Cael said.

Riven turned on him. "We?"

Cael met his eyes. "We're already in it." He meant the city. He meant the bond. Riven heard both and hated both answers.

The bond carried Riven's answer before words did: fear, resentment, and a thin, exhausted loyalty that had not broken. Out loud he only said, "Fine. But if he starts calling us saints, I'm leaving."

Halverin almost smiled again. "No saints in Kestrel Gate. Only obligations."

That, more than anything else in the room, settled Cael. Obligation was something he could trust.

Outside, just before they hit the stairwell, a junior guard intercepted them with Merrow's seal on a wax-folded order. The corridor lamps had already been lit, and their glow ran in bars down plaster walls scraped by shield rims.

"Captain says report before first light," he told Cael. "And bring your own chalk. We ran out in north watch."

Riven barked a short laugh. "City's ending and they need chalk."

The guard gave him a flat look. "How else you keep count."

Cael tucked the order into his belt and started down the stairs.

At the square floor, they were stopped again by a cobbler with both hands black in pitch who blocked their way long enough to ask if the stories were true.

"Can you really feel each other before a strike lands?"

Cael answered first. "Sometimes."

The cobbler nodded like that was enough to carry home and went back to his bench where boots sat in three rows waiting for repair.

Riven watched him work as they passed and thought about what got called heroism and what just kept a city standing.

Merrow's office sat in a converted counting room off the inner yard, half maps and half weapon racks. She was broad-shouldered, close-cropped, and moved like every second had a price.

"You're late," she said before they reached the table.

"We were in council," Cael answered.

"Then you know the shape." She jabbed three chalk circles on a slate map. "Market quarter twitchy. River postern compromised. East barracks overfull and understaffed. You two rotate those zones until first bell tomorrow."

Riven leaned over the slate. "And if all three go bad at once?"

Merrow met his eyes. "Then I decide which fire gets to be a ruin."

Cael took the duty board from her and read the names beneath theirs: rookies, volunteers, two veterans with bad knees, one courier barely old enough to shave. Same feeling as Halverin's office, only worse. Just math and bodies.

One name on the duty board had a cross through it and another written beside it in fresh chalk. Replaced before the shift even started.

"We'll start river postern," he said.

Merrow nodded once. "Good. It scares easy."

She handed Riven a short chalk stick and pointed at the slate margin. "When you see drift in crowds, mark it. Time and place."

Riven frowned. "Drift."

"Where fear bunches before violence. You'll know it when you feel your skin crawl."

Riven tucked the chalk into his belt and glanced at Cael. Through the bond, he felt Cael already turning Merrow's language into routes and contingencies.

When they stepped back into the corridor, the light had thinned to blue and the square noise came up through the stairwell in broken pieces: hammer strikes, someone arguing over ration chalk, a child crying until a door shut. Riven walked half a step ahead and radiated anger through the bond like heat off brick.

"He talks like he owns the weather," Riven said.

Cael kept his eyes on the stairs. "He talks like someone who has to hold a city together."

"By turning us into posters."

Cael did not answer until they reached the square and the cold hit. "If posters keep the gates from breaking, I can live with posters."

Riven said nothing after that.

Their first rotation started at river postern before full dark. The gate guards were young and restless, spears held too high, eyes cutting to every movement on the water. Cael walked the hinge line with a lantern, checking bolts and brace plates like he was inspecting a workshop frame, while Riven stayed above on the wall path with Merrow's chalk slate tucked under one arm. River light flashed between pilings below, and each flash turned moving heads into brief cutout shapes.

By first watch, he had three marks on the slate: grain queue crowding at fish stairs, shouting at east lane shelters, one alley choke near tanners' row where no one moved because everyone expected someone else to go first.

At the fish stairs, two men started shoving over ration order and one reached for a belt knife. Riven dropped to the landing at the same moment Cael came through from the quay side. They split the men apart with body weight and voice before steel cleared leather.

"Home," Cael told the first man.

"No home left," the man shot back.

Cael shifted without blinking. "Then lower chapel floor. Move."

The man moved.

By second watch, Riven's chalk had worn to a stub. He could feel Cael's fatigue in his own shoulders and Cael could feel Riven's rising irritation every time another frightened stranger called them saviors like that solved anything.

When they returned to Merrow near dawn, she read the slate entries in silence and circled three locations.

"These break tomorrow if we don't get ahead," she said.

Cael nodded once, already calculating.

Riven stood there with chalk dust in his palm while Merrow called for the next slate and no one asked if he agreed.
