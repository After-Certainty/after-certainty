# Chapter 11 - No Privacy

Riven chose the roof because it was the one place in Kestrel Gate where people usually left him alone.

The watch tower above the east wall had a maintenance platform half hidden behind spare shingles and split beams.
From there he could see inner streets, market fires burning low, and the black road to Greyhaven. A sliver moon sat behind thin cloud; below, two watch lanterns crossed like clock hands.

He sat with his back to stone, knees up, and tried to hold one thought in place without letting it spill through the bond: Lyra's mouth when she said boundary injury.

He kept the phrase small, no image, no voice, just words. For three breaths it held.

Then Cael felt it.

Riven knew the second it crossed because Cael's reaction snapped back from below: irritation, suspicion, then a question shaped like an order.

Where are you.

Riven clenched his jaw. Nowhere.

The answer went through anyway, location and mood bundled together, like a door with no latch. Thirty heartbeats later, heavy steps came up the ladder to the platform and Cael pulled himself over the lip, hair damp from a hurried wash, shirt half fastened.

He had come without lacing his boots, one heel crushed flat, like he'd run mid-button.

"You skipped evening report," Cael said.

"I was thinking."

"About Lyra."

Riven scrubbed a hand over his face like he could wipe the thought out before it crossed.

Not a question. Riven laughed once, no humor in it. "You see the problem."

Cael stayed standing, arms folded against the wind. "You act like we can separate what's shared."

"No. The problem is I can't have one goddamn thought without it becoming committee business."

Cael's expression tightened. Riven felt the hurt before he saw it. "That's not fair."

"Fair." Riven stood up too, the roof edge at his back. "You want fair? Try wanting someone and feeling your friend flinch at the same time."

Cael looked away toward the wall lights. "This is not about wanting someone."

"It is for me."

Cael's shoulders dropped a fraction, the only sign he had heard what that meant.

The words hung between them. Through the bond came Cael's refusal, disciplined and immediate, and underneath it fear Cael was trying not to name: that any private attachment might make the next patrol slower, the next fight late.

"We have people depending on us," Cael said.

"We always have people depending on us. That's not new."

"No. This is." Cael tapped two fingers against his chest, then toward Riven. "We steer it or it eats us."

Riven spread his hands. "You keep saying steer like there's a wheel. There isn't. There's just impact."

Riven almost said steering and eating were starting to look the same. He did not. The thought came through anyway, stripped raw.

Cael winced as if struck.

They stood in silence while wind rattled loose slate. Below them, a gate bell rang once and stopped.

Riven waited until Cael had climbed halfway down the ladder, then tested the seam again on pure spite. He pictured the roofline exactly as he saw it and then shoved his attention to the opposite side of the square, to a laundry line and a broken chimney pot and anything not Lyra, not fear, not Cael.

It lasted three breaths before Cael's awareness touched back, not words this time, just the shape of where Riven stood and how cold his hands were. Relief came first. Then anger at the relief.

Riven tried a different experiment after Cael left. He climbed down, crossed two streets, and bought a cup of sour plum wine from a night stall run by a woman with a ruined ear. He found a dark step behind a closed tailor shop and sat alone where no one would ask questions. Light from the nearest alley torch did not quite reach him, only a soft orange edge on the cobbles. Behind him, a second-floor window shutter banged once in the wind and then went still. If distance failed, maybe method would work.

He reached for a memory he had never spoken aloud: Greyhaven, age twelve, falling through rotten boards into winter canal water, panic and mud and a broken tooth, Cael hauling him out by the back of his shirt while shouting hard enough to make him cry afterward.

Then he tried to build a wall the way old dockworkers taught children to keep panic out: name five things you can see, four things you can touch, three sounds, two smells, one taste. Broken gutter chain. Split brick. Wet cobble seam. Torch reflection. Crate nail. Stone step under his thigh. Damp cuff at his wrist. Rough glaze on the cup. Leather at his boot heel.

By the time he got to sound, Cael was already there in his head again, uninvited and immediate.

Riven held the memory tight, not the facts only, but the shame in it, the child terror. For a moment he believed he had kept it.

Then Cael's grief hit him from across the square, sudden and deep. Not present grief, old grief, borrowed from the same memory he had never wanted shared.

Riven dropped the cup. It shattered on stone, wine jumping cold across his fingers and boot leather.

He pressed both palms into his eyes until sparks came and his temples throbbed. It did not help. Cael knew now. Not because Riven chose to tell him. Because there was nowhere to hide.

A drunk stumbled past the alley mouth singing half a market song about sworn heroes and never even looked in. Riven sat with wine on his hands and listened to the last line slur away up the lane, then laughed once.
Even the songs had no walls.

He paced to the alley mouth and back, like distance might matter. It did nothing. Cael's worry tracked him from across the square.

He tried one more trick and started counting in the market cipher Bran had taught them as kids, hoping the unfamiliar cadence might blur the edge enough to keep it his.

It didn't.

Back in the barracks dormitory, cots lined in two rows, men snoring and muttering in sleep, Cael sat awake on the edge of his bedroll waiting for him. One oil lamp burned down on a crate by the door, making the rafters look farther away than they were. Moonlight from the high slats laid pale stripes across three empty cots near the rear wall.

"I forgot that happened," Cael said quietly. "The canal."

"I didn't."

"You could have told me."

Riven stared at him. "That's the point. It was mine to not tell."

Cael's mouth tightened. "I didn't ask for your memories."

"Neither did I."

"Then what do you want me to do when it hits?"

Riven took a breath before answering. "I want one room in my head that isn't shared storage."

Cael did not answer right away. When he did, his voice was rougher than usual. "I don't want to take things from you."

Riven sat on his own cot and pulled off his boots. "You don't have to want it."

Cael rubbed both hands over his face and then held them there, elbows on knees, like he could press the bond back out through his palms. Riven felt the effort as clearly as his own heartbeat.

"Merrow wants us on first bell," Cael said into his hands.

Riven let out a short laugh with no humor. "Of course she does."

"I can ask for reassignment."

Riven looked at him. "To what. Two separate cots in the same skull?"

Cael dropped his hands and met his eyes. Neither of them looked away first.

"I don't know how to fix it."
Cael said it to the floorboards, not to Riven.

Riven let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "I noticed."

From outside the dormitory came the scrape of a broom on stone where night crew was clearing ash tracked in from the square. The ordinary sound made the conversation feel even worse, like this was now routine.

They sat in the dim dormitory while someone two cots down ground his teeth in sleep.

Cael spoke first. "Remember Heartbridge ridge. First time we argued about going north."

Riven stared at the rafters. "You kept saying purpose. I kept saying not to be stupid."

"You came anyway."

"Yeah." Riven swallowed. "I thought I'd still be me when we got there."

Cael said nothing. Through the bond, Riven felt the hit land hard and quiet.

Neither slept quickly. Every time Riven turned, Cael felt it. Every time Cael's guilt brushed the seam, it landed under Riven's ribs. Just before dawn Riven accepted what he had been refusing.

The bond was not only power. It was intimacy without permission.

When first bell rang, both of them were already awake.

Neither spoke while they dressed.

At the door, Cael paused with his hand on the latch and sent one clear thought through the seam, deliberate enough to feel like speech.

I am not trying to erase you.

Riven pulled his coat on and answered the same way.

Doesn't matter what you're trying.

Cael did not answer. He only opened the door and stepped into first-bell cold.

Riven followed and felt the square wake around them: bucket lines, hammer blows, a crier rehearsing names. All of it came in doubled.

At the foot of the stairs, Cael reached for the duty board nailed beside the barracks door. Riven watched his eyes track the columns and felt each line land through the bond before he read them himself: patrol, market sweep, council check-in, demonstration prep.

"We are already scheduled," Cael said.

Riven let out one short laugh and tore his bread in half without looking up. "Of course we are."

They took breakfast standing in the yard: hard bread, thin broth, salted fish like rope. Around them, teams sorted salvage by chalk marks.

Riven watched a pair of apprentices carry a plank marked with three different instructions and argue over which one came first. Through the bond he felt Cael choose the same thought he did at the same time: nobody had enough hands for this.

"You all right?" Cael asked without looking at him.

Riven barked a laugh. "Which part."

Cael took that and said nothing else.
