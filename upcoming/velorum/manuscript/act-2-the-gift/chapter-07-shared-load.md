# Chapter 7 - Shared Load

The road north of Ashfen cut between stone walls barely wide enough for a wagon. Shale slid under Riven's boots on the downgrade. Sun lit the tops of the rocks; the ditch below stayed blue with shadow. Canvas snapped somewhere ahead.

Something felt off. No birds. Fresh scrapes on the ditch lip. A length of rope snagged in a thorn bush. Then a pulse of tension hit him—not his, Cael's—and Riven felt it a heartbeat before he saw why.

Men climbed out of the ditch, four of them. Two more stepped around the lead wagon with knives already in their hands. Dirty scarves. Rusted blades. Eyes on the packs.

"Bags down," the nearest one said.
"Slow."

"Fuck that."

Riven moved. Cael moved too. No signal, no plan, just motion.

Riven ducked a swing that should have caught him across the jaw. Grit filled his mouth as he hit one knee. By the time he got back up, Cael had slammed into the attacker from the side hard enough to fold him over. A woman screamed. Somebody dragged her behind a wagon wheel.

The lead teamster had a club under his seat. He never raised it. Riven saw that later—trust or terror, he couldn't tell which.

Another knife flashed low. Riven stepped left—Cael's flinch crossed first, wrong way. The blade nicked his sleeve. Cael caught the attacker's wrist a heartbeat later. Riven was there immediately—too fast. The man hit the dirt.

One more rushed in. Riven ducked. Cael stepped through the space he'd left without hesitation, without shouting, without looking. They didn't call signals. Riven was already where Cael needed him. That scared him more than the knives. Not the blood. His body kept getting there first.

Afterward Riven stood in the ditch shade with blood on his sleeve and watched the caravan stare. A woman near the rear wagon made the sign against bad luck. A boy pointed. "Are they brothers?" His mother pulled him back before either of them could answer.

The bandit swung high. Riven dropped. Cael took the low line. The bond carried the choice before either spoke. The bandit hit the dirt. Riven's knuckles split on the man's jaw. Cael felt the split in his own hand and kept moving.

After the last man went down, Riven stood in the ditch shade with blood on his sleeve and watched the caravan stare. A woman near the rear wagon made the sign against bad luck. A boy pointed. "Are they brothers?" His mother pulled him back before either of them could answer.

The runner dropped to his knees before either of them reached him. Done. The whole thing couldn't have taken a minute. Maybe less.

Riven's ears rang. Cael's breath came hard through the bond—not exhaustion yet, the fight-high, the same buzz Riven felt in his wrists. A woman behind the lead wagon was crying quietly. Somebody else was vomiting in the ditch. The ordinary aftermath of violence, except Riven could feel Cael's hands shaking as if they were his own.

One bandit crawled toward a dropped knife. Riven grabbed for the mule line on the wagon rail. Cael grabbed the same spot. Their knuckles cracked together. The line slipped. The bandit got one hand free before Cael stepped on his wrist and Riven finally had the rope.

Riven felt Cael's flash of shame cross—wrong grab, half-beat late—and shoved it down because the knife was still in the dirt.

They worked the rest in silence—rope, knots, another rope—until six men ended up tied in the ditch shade.

Riven searched the ditch for dropped blades and purses while Cael kept the prisoners covered. One bandit had a ledger stub in his boot—route marks, dates, tower names in bad shorthand. Cael read it once and passed it to the father. "Split Mile gets this tonight."

The father nodded like he didn't want to know what it meant.

Only afterward did he notice the silence. Wagons stopped up and down the cut. Faces staring from behind canvas. A dog barked once and shut up. Then the whispers started—low, fast, too quiet to catch whole sentences. Riven heard pieces anyway: *touched*, *oath*, *don't look at them wrong*.

"You always fight like that?" a man asked from beside the lead wagon.

Riven looked over. Middle-aged, traveling coat, probably the father of the family huddled behind him. The man looked impressed and a little afraid.

"No," Riven said. His pulse still hammered in his ears. "No. Not usually."

Cael glanced over. "Close enough."

Riven wiped blood off his nose with the back of his wrist. Nobody laughed. A woman near the rear wagon made a sign against bad luck and looked away. A child pointed. "Are they brothers?" His mother pulled him back before either of them could answer. The lead teamster kept staring at the ditch—not at the prisoners, at them.

Two women behind the next wagon stopped talking when Riven glanced over. One touched her throat like she was checking her own pulse. A boy no older than eight peeked around a wheel, then hid again. Somebody farther back said *sworn* and *don't* in the same breath and got shushed hard.

The father swallowed. "Three hits this week," he said. "Same stretch of road. Split Mile tower's half a day east if you want to send word."

Riven looked up at the ridge. Boot prints. Broken brush. Half an arrow shaft caught in a thorn bush.

"Or somebody's selling routes," Cael said.

That felt worse. Cael looked at him. No words. Didn't need them.

Riven crouched beside the nearest captive and yanked the knot tighter. The man hissed through his teeth.

"You move before we say so," Riven said, "you lose hands."

The bandit spat into the dirt and looked away. Good enough.

One of the captives had a split lip and kept testing the rope with his tongue. Riven watched him until the man stopped. Cael checked the other knots without being asked—same hitch, same tension, the sort of work that made captives stop trying.

Cael turned toward the wagons. "One line. Nobody breaks off. Nobody wanders off alone. Somebody falls behind, everybody stops."

Nobody argued. That was new. The fight had lasted less than a minute, and somehow that made people more nervous, not less.

A woman from the rear wagon pressed a strip of cloth into Riven's hand—clean, folded, smelling of lavender. "For the blood," she said, and looked away before he could refuse. Cael felt Riven's embarrassment cross and said nothing.

Riven stood and rolled his shoulders. His pulse still hammered in his wrists. Dried blood cracked across his knuckles when he flexed his fingers. The nick in his sleeve had spread into a short tear. Nothing else hurt. That should have bothered him more than it did.

Cael crossed the road toward him. Sunlight caught the edge of the ditch between them.

"You felt that."

"The whole damn thing?"

"Yeah." Riven wiped blood across his sleeve. "Call it whatever helps you sleep."

A wagon door creaked open. The father stepped down. "You two headed for Kestrel Gate?"

"We are," Cael said.

The man nodded quickly, like he'd been waiting to ask. "They said two oath-bound were on this road."

The words stuck. Oath-bound—not cursed, not touched, not whatever they actually were, but something cleaner, something people could put in a story. Cael's satisfaction hit the bond before Riven saw it on his face—steady, unwanted. The fight-high still buzzed through Riven's veins, and he wanted to hit Cael and stand next to him in the same breath.

The father kept talking. "Raids all month. Every caravan's heard about it." Riven caught a few people watching from the wagons—watching them, not the prisoners, them. "We heard nothing could touch you."

A teamster two carts back muttered, "Heard they share one set of lungs," and his wife elbowed him until he shut up.

Riven looked away. "That's a stupid thing to hear."

Nobody disagreed.

Before the line moved on, Cael checked wagon straps and reinforced a cracked axle pin with a strip of cedar. Riven held the wheel and passed him tools without being asked. The father watched both of them work. When they finished, he pressed two hard biscuits into Riven's hand.

Two carts back a woman had cut her palm on a strap buckle. Riven cleaned it with river water and wrapped it with a strip from his shirt tail before Cael noticed. Cael noticed anyway—the bond carried the sting in his own palm—and said nothing.

"Three bad stretches before the Gate," he said. "Split Mile. Marsh bend. Then the south road."

Cael nodded. "We'll know if they're bad before you roll into them."

The caravan moved again. For the next mile Riven walked the ditch line and checked every bend before the wagons reached it. Cael worked the front—slow here, left there, close the gap—the sort of things he'd always done, only now Riven felt the decisions before he heard them.

At a blind turn a boy ran out with a message from three carts back—*wheel hot, stop now.* Cael stopped the line before Riven heard the shout. The wheel had cracked through the hub. They pulled it and lashed a spare while the father watched without blinking. When they finished he pressed a strip of dried meat into Riven's hand. Riven tried to refuse. The bond carried Cael's *take it* before Riven's *don't* finished forming.

When the road widened, they let the wagons roll ahead without them. The ridge ahead glowed gold where the late sun found it. For a while neither spoke.

Then Riven said, "This is what you wanted."

Cael kept walking. "Part of it."

Riven laughed once. "Terrifying answer."

"Yeah."

A teamster two carts back asked whether the bond made them fearless. Riven said no. Cael said careful. The teamster looked disappointed. Riven felt Cael's satisfaction cross and clamped down on his own before it could answer.

The wind pushed at their backs. Stone clicked beneath their boots. After another minute, Riven said, "You still think it was worth it."

Cael was quiet long enough to be honest. Finally he said, "I think we're past worth it."

Cael didn't answer again for the next mile.

They passed a shrine with fresh cord on the carved wrists. Riven cut it with his knife. Cael felt the choice cross—approval, fear, something like grief—and said nothing. A woman behind the next wagon crossed herself and looked away.

The trail narrowed on the switchback. Cael's stride pulled at Riven's legs. The fight had felt clean; walking didn't. Shoulders brushed once, then again. Riven's jaw locked. His breath went shallow. No room in his own head anymore.

Higher on the slope their breathing synced without either of them trying. Riven noticed because he tried to break it and couldn't, not for more than three steps. His chest rose when Cael's did. That was worse than the shoulder brushes.

Halfway up the switchback a stone clattered in the ditch below. They turned at the same instant—same angle, same half-step. A woman on the path behind them crossed herself and looked away. Riven felt his face heat. Cael felt it too.

At a spring lower on the slope, Cael knelt to fill the water skin. Before Riven looked over, he felt the shift—weight onto the left knee, leather creaking, the old ache Cael got after a long climb. Riven shut his eyes. The feeling stayed.

A woman from the rear wagon brought them cups—tin, dented, shared without asking. Riven drank because refusing would be a story. Cael drank because the bond carried Riven's thirst anyway.

When he opened them, Cael was watching him. Riven reached for the skin before he thought about it. Cael reached too. Their fingers touched the same strap. Cael pulled back first. Riven drank anyway, from the side Cael always used, and hated that he knew that without asking.

"You keep flinching."

"You keep noticing."

Cael corked the skin and tossed it over. "We're not split yet."

Riven caught it against his chest. "Says who?"

Cael looked back toward the road. "Both of us."

That wasn't reassuring.

Near dusk they caught the caravan again where the road pinched between two boulders. A mule had gone lame in harness. The line stacked single file around it—heads down, easy pickings for anyone with a knife. Sun on one rock face. The other in black shadow.

The father from earlier was arguing with a teamster about whether to leave the mule or butcher it for camp meat. Riven kept his voice out of it and checked the ditch line anyway—fresh boot prints, same size as the morning bandits, heading east toward Split Mile.

Cael waved the wagons back and lined them wheel-to-wheel, hubs almost touching. Riven took the lead rope. The animal blew hard through its nose, ears flat. Riven kept his voice low and moved it one step, then another while a teamster crouched at the hoof with shaking hands.

"Stone split," the teamster said. He ran a dirty finger along the crack. "I can wrap it. Won't hold long."

"Long enough to the next stable," Cael said.

Riven felt Cael's fatigue in his shoulders and lower back, the fight still there. Under it something steadier when somebody needed an order. His stomach tightened.

The teamster wrapped the hoof with strips torn from a sail. The mule tested weight and blew hard. Riven kept the lead rope low. Cael checked the ditch line again—nothing moving, nothing watching, just the wind in the pines and the bond carrying the same alert in both chests.

Two carts back a child had started crying and couldn't stop. The mother asked Riven if the road was safe now. Riven said safer. Cael said safe enough to move. The bond carried both answers and the child cried anyway until the line rolled forward.

The line moved again. The father from earlier raised a hand. "Gate's lucky you're ahead of us."

Riven muttered, for Cael only, "Lucky for who."

Cael didn't answer out loud. Through the bond: *anybody behind us who doesn't need another scare.*

Split Mile tower sat a mile farther on—a squat stone post, one watchman, one lamp swinging beneath the eaves. Cael told him about the bandits, the caravan, the prisoners. The watchman wrote everything down in a damp ledger, ink spreading across the page.

"You want this under your names?"

Cael hesitated, then nodded.

Riven felt his stomach tighten before the pen touched paper. Names mattered. Names traveled. A clerk in the tower corner was already copying the entry onto a second sheet—*Gate archive, magistrate if relief makes it through.*

The watchman finished writing and blew gently across the page. He looked tired—past expecting miracles.

"Prisoners stay until the tower relief comes," he said. "You want credit for the take, you sign. You don't, I write *unknown pair* and the council clerk argues about it for a week."

The watchman scratched both names in a ledger that smelled like damp wool. Riven read the ink over his shoulder: *Cael. Riven. Road cut, six bound.* No mention of the bond. No mention of the way Riven's body had moved before Cael's. The omission felt worse than a lie.

A relief team arrived at dusk—two guards, one clerk, shackles for the prisoners. The clerk asked whether the pair wanted escort credit on the same sheet. Cael said no. Riven said nothing. The clerk shrugged and copied the names again anyway for the tower archive.

The watchman walked them to the tower door and pointed at the marsh track. "Boot prints doubled since last week. Don't camp in the reeds."

"We won't," Cael said.

"Marsh bend's half a day if the road holds," the watchman said. "Less if you don't stop for idiots."

"We don't stop for idiots," Cael said.

They camped short of Marsh bend because the father had warned them and because Riven's legs had started moving when Cael's did without permission. A shepherd's fire burned half a mile off the road. Nobody invited them closer. Riven didn't blame him.

Before dark the father brought them a tin cup of thin stew and sat on a stump without asking permission. "Split Mile clerk will copy your names onto three slates," he said. "Tower archive. Gate archive. Magistrate if the relief team makes it through."

"Good," Cael said.

"For who?" The father looked at the prisoners in the ditch shade, then at the wagons. "My boy asked if you share one heart. I told him no. He asked why you move like you do."

Riven stared into the cup. "Tell him we're tired."

The father almost smiled. "He'll like that less than a miracle." He stood, brushed mud off his knees, and went back to his fire without waiting for thanks.

At first gray they crossed the bend anyway—reeds on both sides, mud that looked solid until it wasn't. Halfway through, a cart ahead lost a wheel into the ditch. Cael waded in without being asked. Riven followed because the bond pulled and because the alternative was watching people drown in knee-deep mud.

A woman on the cart seat screamed for her daughter still inside the bed. Riven reached through the canvas flap and hauled the girl out by her collar—soaked, coughing reeds, alive. Cael wedged a board under the axle while two teamsters cursed the mud in the same breath. When the wheel came free the line moved again without a word of thanks.

They got the cart free. The driver didn't thank them. He crossed himself and kept moving. Riven wiped mud off his forearms and felt Cael's satisfaction cross before he could clamp down on it.

"Don't," Riven said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You didn't have to."

Past the bend the road climbed through scrub pines. Boot prints crossed and recrossed—same size as the morning bandits, Riven thought, though he couldn't prove it. Cael checked each fork without speaking. Riven felt the choices land in his legs before Cael called them.

At a water stop a woman pressed hard cheese into Riven's pack without meeting his eyes, like paying a toll nobody had agreed to. Cael felt Riven's irritation spike and his own shame answer it. The cheese sat in Riven's pack like a debt.

They walked the last miles in silence. The road climbed. Kestrel Gate's smoke showed on the horizon before noon. Every time Riven shifted his pack, he felt Cael adjust his strap a heartbeat later, like their bodies were learning a shared rhythm whether he wanted it or not.

At a fork a peddler tried to sell them charms against bad luck on the road. Riven said they had enough bad luck already. Cael bought two anyway with copper he didn't have to spare and pressed one into Riven's palm when the peddler wasn't looking. Riven felt Cael's stubbornness cross with the metal—warm, stupid, impossible to refuse without a fight.

A teamster from the caravan caught up at the fork and walked beside them without speaking for half a mile. Finally: "My boy asked if you share one heart." Riven said that was stupid. Cael said sometimes it felt that way. The teamster nodded like both answers were true and went back to his wagon.

A guard patrol from the Gate passed them going south—four men, spears vertical, faces closed. The sergeant asked their names and wrote them in a pocket ledger without looking up. "Magistrate's office knows you're coming," he said. "Don't make us regret it."

One of the patrolmen lagged behind long enough to mutter, "Gate's half empty on the south wall. If you're what the broadsides say, get there before the next raid."

"Raid?" Riven asked.

The man shrugged like the word embarrassed him. "Missing patrols. cut supports. Same as your road." He caught up to his squad before Riven could push further.

When the patrol was gone Riven muttered, "We're not their fucking bridge."

Cael kept walking. "Today we are."

The south road narrowed between stone walls again. Riven checked the ditch out of habit. Cael checked the ridge. Neither spoke. The bond carried the same alert in both chests anyway—boot prints, fresh, heading east toward the Gate like the road itself was funneling trouble home.

At the last rise before the walls they could see the whole city—watchfires, ration smoke, missing notices flapping on the outer face like white teeth. Riven stopped long enough to feel his stomach tighten. Cael felt it too and kept walking.

Behind them Split Mile's lamp was a pinprick in the distance. Ahead, Kestrel Gate already had their names on slates and broadsides and stories they hadn't agreed to tell. Riven shouldered his pack. The strap bit into the same shoulder Cael had braced during the fight.
