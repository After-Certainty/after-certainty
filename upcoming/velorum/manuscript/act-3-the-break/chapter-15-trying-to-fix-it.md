# Chapter 15 - Trying to Fix It

They built the square in three days like they were raising a barn.

Cael stood on the lower staging platform at dawn and watched labor crews slot timber braces into the old fountain basin. The central square had always been wide; now it was wider, cleared of market stalls and cart pens so sightlines ran clean from the magistrate's steps to the north gate road. Chalk arrows marked where crowds would stand. Ropes marked where they would not.

Merrow climbed up beside him with a slate under her arm. "You want the west lane left open for casualty wagons."

"Always," Cael said.

"Halverin wants it closed for optics."

"Leave it open," Cael said. "If someone bleeds, I don't want them running through a crowd to get help."

Merrow studied him, then nodded. "I'll tell the clerks it's your call."

It was the first call that felt like his in days.

Halverin's plan was simple on paper and ugly in practice: staged stress, controlled movement, public proof that the bond could hold under command. Cael and Riven would enter from the east steps. Halverin would call sequences from the magistrate platform. Guards would simulate pressure at the margins. Civilians would watch because fear made people watch.

Riven called it a trial with no accused.

Cael called it work.

They rehearsed at second bell, then again at dusk. Halverin stood on the platform with a speaking horn and counted beats like a foreman laying joists.

"Step on my count," he said. "Breathe on my count. You do not improvise until you can hold the line without improvisation."

Riven missed a count on purpose once. The bond jerked; Cael's knee buckled half a beat. Halverin stopped the run.

"Again," he said. "You are not performing for each other. You are performing for everyone who thinks the ground is about to give."

They ran it again. Cael felt the rhythm settle when he stopped fighting Riven's timing and started matching it. Not trust. Not forgiveness. Mechanics.

At night they slept in the east barracks annex because Halverin wanted them close. Cael lay on his back and listened to Riven turn on the cot. Every turn came through the bond with weight attached: Lyra's name unspoken, guilt, anger at the broadsides, hunger for something that still felt like his.

Cael stared at the ceiling beam and counted cracks. He told himself control was not the same as erasure. He told himself the demonstration would give the city a reason to stop panicking. He told himself he could carry Riven's doubt without letting it stop his hands.

The bond did not agree. It kept feeding him Riven's doubt until his own certainty felt thin.

On the morning of the final rehearsal, Halverin met them at the platform with two new broadsides. One showed their names under a woodcut of paired figures. The other listed rules for the crowd: no rushing the line, no weapons, children behind the rope.

"You'll read this from the steps," Halverin told Cael.

Cael scanned it. "You want me speaking civic law now?"

"I want you speaking steadiness," Halverin said. "People listen to the one who sounds like he believes the ground will hold."

Riven took the sheet from Cael's hand and read it once. "You left out what happens if we fail in front of them."

"Then we adjust in public and continue," Halverin said. "Panic is worse than a visible correction."

"For who?" Riven asked.

Halverin did not answer that. He never did when the answer was only his office.

Lyra came through the square at midday with a crate of bandage rolls and stopped when she saw the staging. She looked at Cael, then at Riven, then at the ropes.

"You're putting sick people in the front row," she said.

"We're not," Cael said. "Merrow cleared the lanes."

"You're putting hope in the front row," Lyra said. "Same problem."

Riven stepped down from the platform. "Don't come tomorrow if you can't watch it."

"I'll be where the hurt is," Lyra said. "That's the only place worth standing."

She left before Riven could say anything else. Through the bond Cael got the ache of that exchange and his own shame for wanting her approval less than he wanted Halverin's plan to work.

In the afternoon Halverin walked the sequence with them one more time. He placed their marks with chalk. He set guards at the margins. He tested the speaking horn until his voice carried flat and clear across stone.

"Tomorrow at first bell," he said. "You enter on signal one. You hold on signal two. You do not break on my voice until you have no choice left."

Cael heard the last clause and felt something cold slide down his spine. *Until you have no choice left* sounded like theology dressed as logistics.

He signed the staging slate anyway.

Riven watched him sign. "You still think this fixes it?"

Cael capped the ink. "I think we owe the dead a better next minute than we gave them at the stairs."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," Cael said. "It's what I can do tonight."

They walked the square perimeter after Halverin left. Paste smell. Fresh rope. A boy practicing the chalk arrows like a game while his mother shouted at him to come home. Broadsides drying on the wall with their names centered in the first line.

Riven stopped under one of them. "If we fail tomorrow, it's not just us."

"I know," Cael said.

"Do you?"

Cael looked at the empty fountain basin, at the braces, at the platform where Halverin would stand and call them like tools that could be tuned. He felt the bond pull tight between them, not in rhythm but in warning.

"I know," he said again.

He did not say he was afraid. The bond carried it anyway. Riven felt it and did not mock him for it. That was worse than mockery.

At second watch they lay down in the annex again. Cael listened to the city move below: buckets, bells, a drunk argument near the south gate, someone singing badly behind the barracks wall.

He rehearsed the counts in his head until numbers replaced names. He told himself that was focus. He told himself tomorrow he would hold the line.

The bond kept feeding him Riven's dread like weather he could not shut out.
