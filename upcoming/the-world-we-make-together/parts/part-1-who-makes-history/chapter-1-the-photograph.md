# **Chapter 1**

## **The Photograph**

A photograph often becomes more useful when something is removed.

We open the image on a phone and spread two fingers across the screen. The room disappears first. Then the people standing near the doorway. A shoulder at the edge becomes distracting, so we trim it away. The table is unnecessary. The ceiling contributes nothing. We move the frame inward until the face we wanted is large enough to see.

The picture improves.

The person who had been one figure among several becomes the subject. Their expression is clearer. Their importance is no longer in doubt. Someone receiving the photograph later will know immediately where to look.

Cropping is not a form of deception. It is an answer to a question.

Which child was opening the gift?

Who gave the speech?

Which runner crossed the line first?

Who signed the document?

A photograph containing everything may tell us less than one that has learned what to leave out.

But the crop also changes the event.

The person at the center no longer appears to be responding to someone beside them. The expression that looked amused within the original scene may look solemn once the source of the laughter is gone. A raised hand becomes a gesture of command when the person it was greeting has been removed. The crowd disappears, and the person who had been surrounded begins to look alone.

Nothing inside the new frame is necessarily false.

The mistake would be believing that the frame contains everything that made the moment possible.

History performs a similar operation.

Large events resist memory. Too many people acted. Too many institutions overlapped. Causes began years before the day that later acquired a name. Decisions moved through meetings, letters, rumors, laws, technologies, fears, accidents, and ordinary habits. Some people knew only a small part of what was happening. Others understood more but controlled less. No one saw the event from the distance at which history would eventually describe it.

A recognizable person gives the event somewhere to land.

A revolution becomes a revolutionary. A war becomes a general. A movement becomes a leader. A scientific achievement becomes an inventor. An administration becomes a president. A company becomes its founder.

The person offers a human scale for something too large to hold at once. We can study a face. We can follow a decision. We can ask what someone believed and what they did. Structural change may be historically important, but it does not enter a room, feel afraid, sign an order, refuse an instruction, or choose a sentence while thousands of people wait.

This is part of the enduring appeal of what became known as Great Man history. In lectures delivered in 1840 and published the following year, Thomas Carlyle presented history through heroes whose thought and character gave form to their age.[^c1-carlyle] The account now sounds too grand, too masculine, and too eager to convert power into proof of greatness. Yet it recognized something that more impersonal explanations can sometimes lose: people make choices, and certain choices alter what becomes possible for everyone else.

The error was not noticing that individuals matter.

The error was allowing their importance to become self-sufficiency.

---

On August 28, 1963, a crowd gathered before the Lincoln Memorial.

Photographs from the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom show people filling the space around the Reflecting Pool. Signs rise above heads. Men in suits stand near the memorial. Singers, clergy, labor leaders, organizers, journalists, and participants move in and out of the frame.

But the image that remains most available to public memory is simpler.

Martin Luther King Jr. stands at the microphone.

The monument rises behind him. The crowd extends in front. His position within the image feels almost inevitable, as though the entire day had been organized to produce this one arrangement: the speaker, the memorial, the people, the words.

There are good reasons for the photograph to settle there.

King’s address became one of the most recognizable speeches in American history. His language gathered moral argument, biblical rhythm, national symbolism, political demand, and prophetic imagination into a form millions of people could carry away. The Smithsonian notes that the speech drew upon the Bible, the Constitution, Lincoln, and the national anthem while giving memorable expression to the march’s demands.[^c1-smithsonian-speech]

The person at the microphone mattered.

He was not interchangeable with the crowd.

His years of preaching had trained his voice. His experience within the movement had formed his moral vocabulary. His public standing allowed his words to travel farther than the same words might have traveled from another mouth. When Mahalia Jackson called from behind him to speak about the dream, King moved away from his prepared text and returned to language he had developed through earlier sermons and speeches.[^c1-jackson-dream] The moment was not created from nothing, but neither was it mechanically predetermined. He heard the invitation, judged the room, and responded.

The speech depended upon preparation and became more than its preparation.

This is where attempts to correct heroic history can become too eager. Once we learn that King had used the dream imagery before, we may be tempted to explain away the moment. Once we learn that Jackson prompted him, we may begin treating the speech as a collective artifact in which no individual contribution deserves particular weight.

But influence is not a subtraction problem.

Mahalia Jackson’s intervention does not reduce King. His earlier language does not make his performance less consequential. The movement’s ideas do not turn the speaker into a delivery system. A person can receive, combine, interpret, and transform what others have made available.

The speech belonged to a history larger than the speaker, and the speaker changed what that history could become.

That is the tension the photograph has difficulty holding.

The camera can show King at the microphone. It cannot easily show the decades through which the microphone arrived.

A. Philip Randolph had imagined a major march on Washington long before 1963. He had threatened such a march in 1941 while pressing the federal government to confront discrimination in defense employment. When a new march took shape more than two decades later, Randolph served as its director. Bayard Rustin managed much of the day-to-day planning, working through transportation routes, marshals, sound systems, schedules, organizational rivalries, security concerns, and the enormous practical problem of bringing people from across the country into one shared public action.[^c1-randolph-rustin]

An estimated 250,000 people came by bus, train, car, and plane.[^c1-nps-attendance]

They did not arrive as one undifferentiated crowd. They came through churches, unions, civil-rights organizations, student groups, religious communities, friendship networks, and local campaigns. They brought demands shaped by employment discrimination, segregation, voting restrictions, housing, education, police violence, and the repeated betrayal of formal equality.

The march itself was a coalition, and coalitions leave traces of their tensions.

Women performed essential organizing labor but received little space within the principal speaking program. Anna Arnold Hedgeman and Dorothy Height pressed the organizers to include a woman speaker. Daisy Bates eventually delivered a brief tribute to women in the movement, while many prominent women whose work had made the gathering possible remained outside the central speaking roles.[^c1-women-speakers]

The photograph can widen, but the wider frame does not automatically correct the account.

A larger photograph might include Randolph and Rustin. It might include Jackson, Bates, Height, John Lewis, Walter Reuther, clergy members, singers, marshals, television crews, and rows of participants. Yet even this fuller image would leave out the planning office in Harlem. It would leave out the route maps. It would leave out the person who convinced a local congregation to hire a bus, the family that saved money for a ticket, the worker who risked a day’s wages, and the volunteer who learned where people should stand if panic began.

The edge of the photograph is not the edge of the cause.

This does not mean everyone contributed equally to everything that happened that day.

The person who booked a bus did not write King’s speech. The person who printed a sign did not make Rustin’s logistical decisions. The participant standing far from the memorial did not possess the same ability as King to shape the event’s public meaning. Collective action is not a substance divided into identical portions.

It has form.

Some actions create the occasion. Others organize it. Others authorize it, interpret it, fund it, protect it, oppose it, or carry it beyond the day itself. The event depends upon their relationship without requiring us to pretend that every role has the same consequence.

History is collective. Causation remains uneven.

The difficulty is learning to see unevenness without turning it into a pedestal.

---

A photograph makes hierarchy visible even when the photographer did not intend it.

Some faces are large. Others become dots.

The person at the microphone occupies more of the frame than someone standing near the Reflecting Pool. The person who signs an agreement appears larger than the people whose pressure made the agreement necessary. The founder stands onstage while the workers who designed, assembled, shipped, supported, and repaired the product remain outside the auditorium.

This is partly a property of public action.

A crowd needs a spokesperson. A government needs someone whose signature binds the institution. A movement needs voices through which its demands can travel. A complicated achievement needs symbols that people can remember.

The problem is not that public attention concentrates.

The problem is what we infer from the concentration.

We begin treating the most visible person as the source from which the rest of the event flowed. The leader appears to possess the idea before anyone else. Followers appear mainly as extensions of the leader’s will. Institutions become tools awaiting command. Circumstances become obstacles overcome by character.

The photograph teaches a causal grammar:

Someone stood at the center.

Therefore, the center produced the whole.

Modern organizations reproduce this grammar with remarkable efficiency.

A chief executive walks onto a stage. A large screen brightens behind them. They describe what “we” have built, but the event steadily converts the plural into a singular image. News reports use the executive’s name. Investors read the person’s gestures and confidence. The product becomes evidence of vision. Thousands of decisions disappear into the presentation because the company needs a face through which the achievement can become legible.

Again, the compression may contain truth.

The executive may have made pivotal choices. They may have protected a project others wanted to cancel, recruited unusual talent, changed the design, accepted a risk, or found language that allowed people to see why the work mattered. Their role may have given them unusual power to redirect the whole effort.

But the stage cannot tell us which parts of the achievement required this person and which parts required only that someone occupy the role.

It cannot show which ideas traveled upward from people whose names never appeared in the presentation.

It cannot show the infrastructure that made individual vision executable.

Nor can it show the failures removed before the launch, the disagreement converted into alignment, the labor outsourced beyond the company’s visible boundaries, or the people who absorbed the cost of speed.

The spotlight reveals something real.

It also makes the darkness look empty.

---

Few images compress collective achievement more powerfully than the first human steps on the Moon.

The astronaut stands against a landscape that contains almost nothing else. The figure appears radically alone. No crowd surrounds the spacesuit. No building, road, factory, university, or control room enters the frame. The distance from Earth seems to confirm the independence of the person who crossed it.

But Apollo was one of the least solitary achievements imaginable.

At its height, the program involved roughly 400,000 Americans and more than 20,000 companies and universities.[^c1-nasa-apollo] Engineers worked on propulsion, guidance, communications, heat protection, navigation, life support, materials, software, training, testing, food, photography, medicine, and recovery. Contractors built components that had to operate as a system even though no person could understand every part at equal depth.

The astronauts still mattered in ways the number 400,000 should not erase.

They accepted unusual risks. They trained to act when procedures met conditions no simulation had perfectly reproduced. Their decisions aboard the spacecraft could not always be replaced by the collective intelligence on the ground.

During Apollo 11’s descent, computer alarms created uncertainty about whether the landing should continue. Guidance controller Steve Bales and the support team concluded that the mission could proceed, allowing the descent to continue.[^c1-bales-alarms] The later image of an astronaut on the lunar surface contains none of this judgment. It cannot show the people in Houston, the software decisions that produced the alarms, the engineers who understood their meaning, or the organizational discipline that allowed a short recommendation to carry enormous consequence.

The Moon landing did not belong to one person.

Neither did it belong to 400,000 people in the same way.

Some participants created conditions. Some solved narrow problems without which the mission would have failed. Some made decisions at moments when very few people possessed the authority or information to make them. Some became the public bearers of an achievement that exceeded everyone involved.

The collective did not replace the individual.

The individual existed inside a collective capacity.

That distinction changes how we understand greatness.

A person may be remarkable because they can do what few others could do. They may also be remarkable because a vast arrangement of people and institutions placed them where their capacities could matter.

The photograph captures the first fact more easily than the second.

---

There is a reason stories resist this complexity.

A story needs action. Structures do not make satisfying protagonists. “Industrial capacity,” “racial hierarchy,” “institutional legitimacy,” and “communications technology” may explain much, but they do not look into another person’s face and decide whether to continue.

Removing individuals from history can create its own distortion.

It can make responsibility sound atmospheric.

A war begins because nationalism, alliances, economic rivalry, and military planning converged. A financial crisis emerges from incentives, leverage, regulation, and market psychology. An institution fails because its culture normalized silence. All of these explanations may be accurate. Yet wars are authorized by people. Risks are approved by people. Warnings are ignored by people. Cultures persist through repeated human acts that may be constrained without becoming involuntary.

A structure is often an earlier pattern of human action that has become difficult to see as action.

Rules outlive the people who wrote them. Incentives make yesterday’s judgment feel like today’s necessity. Offices concentrate authority in whoever occupies them. Customs turn choices into expectations. Technologies make certain actions easier and others nearly invisible.

By the time a new person enters, the arrangement feels inherited rather than made.

But inherited does not mean natural.

The person at the center of the photograph did not create the whole setting. They still act within it.

This is why explanation must resist two forms of innocence.

Heroic history grants innocence to the background. Institutions, followers, and ordinary participants become scenery even when their cooperation sustained the outcome.

Structural history can grant innocence to the foreground. The visible person becomes the product of forces so large that their choices begin to look almost automatic.

Neither is sufficient.

The leader did not create the world alone.

The world did not act without the leader.

---

One way to test a person-centered account is to ask a counterfactual question:

What would have happened if this person had not been there?

The question is useful because it restores contingency. Perhaps another leader would have made a different decision. Perhaps the timing would have changed. Perhaps the movement would have found another voice, but the voice would not have carried in the same way. Perhaps the institution’s structure made the result likely enough that another person would have produced something similar.

Yet counterfactuals can also flatter the people already visible.

We imagine history without the famous person because we know whom to remove. We rarely imagine the event without the unknown organizer who connected two groups that otherwise would not have trusted one another. We do not know which technician, translator, clerk, fundraiser, caregiver, or local leader to subtract because the historical record has not taught us where their influence entered.

The photograph determines whose absence feels imaginable.

A better inquiry asks several questions at once.

What could this person do because of their particular judgment, character, knowledge, or position?

What could they do only because other people had already created the opportunity?

Which parts of the event would likely have proceeded without them?

Which parts depended upon people responding to them?

What alternatives became visible because they acted?

What alternatives disappeared because attention gathered too tightly around them?

These questions do not produce a neat percentage of historical credit.

They produce a more honest account of relationship.

King’s speech mattered because King delivered it. It also mattered because a movement had created the language, urgency, audience, institutions, and public confrontation through which the address could travel. The march depended upon Randolph, Rustin, Hedgeman, Height, Jackson, Lewis, many organizations, and hundreds of thousands of participants. Yet naming them does not exhaust the cause either. Each person leads us toward another edge.

Collective history has no final uncropped version.

There is always another room beyond the frame.

---

The way we assign historical credit also shapes how we assign historical blame.

When a successful institution becomes the achievement of one leader, its harms can be personalized in the same way. Removing the person begins to look like removing the cause. A resignation, election, firing, or public disgrace promises a clean moral reset.

Sometimes the person really is a central cause. Their departure may stop an abuse, change a policy, or permit suppressed information to surface.

But institutions are often eager to sacrifice a face while preserving the arrangement that produced the behavior.

The leader becomes the whole explanation precisely when the institution needs the explanation to remain small.

The reverse can happen too. A person can appeal to complexity when personal responsibility becomes uncomfortable. They point toward advisers, incentives, inherited rules, public pressure, and organizational culture. The wider frame becomes a place to hide.

Seeing collective causation should not make responsibility disappear.

It should make responsibility more exact.

The person with authority may bear greater responsibility because they possessed greater freedom to act, greater access to information, or greater power to alter the conditions surrounding others. The organizer may bear responsibility for what the event made possible. Participants may bear responsibility for cooperation, silence, resistance, or interpretation. Institutions may require reform because replacing one person cannot reach the pattern embedded in rules and incentives.

The whole picture does not absolve the person at the center.

It shows us what kind of responsibility the center actually held.

---

When we return to the photograph on the phone, we can undo the crop.

The room reappears. The table returns. Other people move back into view. The person at the center becomes smaller, though not necessarily less important.

Now we can see that someone was looking toward them. Someone else was holding the object they seemed to possess alone. A hand at the edge explains the smile. The background reveals that the moment was public rather than private, coordinated rather than spontaneous, inherited rather than self-created.

The wider image corrects the first one.

It does not complete it.

The photograph still excludes what happened before the shutter opened and after it closed. It cannot show the histories carried into the room, the roles that organized where people stood, or the unequal permissions governing who could approach the center. It cannot reveal which people felt free to speak, who had learned to remain silent, or who was never invited into the picture.

The person in the center may matter greatly.

The frame cannot tell us how greatly by itself.

To understand that, we must look beyond the visible face toward the conditions that made the moment possible: the institutions, rules, habits, resources, exclusions, and earlier decisions already waiting when the person arrived.

The photograph shows us who crossed into history’s attention.

It does not yet show us how the path was built.
