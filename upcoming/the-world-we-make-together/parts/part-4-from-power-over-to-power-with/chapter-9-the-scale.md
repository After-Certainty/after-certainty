# **Chapter 9**

## **The Scale**

A scale can reveal an imbalance without explaining how the weight arrived.

We place an object on one side. The beam tilts. We add something to the other until the line returns to center.

The movement feels like resolution.

Too much here.

Not enough there.

Add or remove until the sides become equal.

The scale does not ask what the objects are. A stone and a loaf of bread may weigh the same. So may a tool and the hand injured while using it. The instrument compares force without interpreting meaning.

This makes the scale useful.

It also makes the scale dangerous as a model of power.

When power is imagined as weight, every gain appears to require an equivalent loss. One person rises because another descends. A worker gains discretion only if management surrenders control. A minority gains protection only if the majority loses freedom. A government becomes capable only by making citizens less free. A leader’s authority grows as everyone else’s agency contracts.

The relationship appears to contain a fixed amount.

Someone must possess it.

Someone else must yield.

Many conflicts really do contain this structure. A person cannot occupy the same office as the person they replaced. A court cannot enforce both incompatible claims. A factory owner who raises wages may accept lower profits, increase prices, reduce investment, or find savings elsewhere. A government that protects one person from another must limit what the second person is permitted to do.

Power has edges.

Someone decides.

Someone bears the consequence.

The problem is not that the scale is false.

The problem begins when the scale becomes the only instrument through which power can be imagined.

Then every relationship must be organized through control, resistance, surrender, or victory. Even benevolence travels downward. The person above decides what the person below needs. The stronger side may act generously, but generosity remains something it controls.

The balance can improve.

The relationship remains vertical.

---

In the early 1880s, George Pullman built a town south of Chicago beside the factory where his company manufactured railroad sleeping cars.

The town was planned rather than accumulated.

Its brick homes, parks, public buildings, streets, hotel, market, and factory were intended to form a coherent environment. Pullman believed that orderly surroundings could improve workers’ habits, reduce labor conflict, encourage loyalty, and produce a stable workforce.

The company purchased approximately four thousand acres. It owned the town’s buildings and charged rents designed to generate a return on the company’s investment.[^c9-pullman-town]

The arrangement could be described as generous.

Workers received housing with sanitation and amenities superior to many industrial neighborhoods of the era. The town attracted national attention as an experiment in combining business efficiency with social improvement.

The arrangement could also be described as control.

The employer was not merely the employer.

It was the landlord.

It determined the design of the community, the use of public space, the management of services, and the conditions under which residents inhabited homes they could not purchase from the company.

The factory whistle organized the workday.

The company organized much of the world beyond it.

Pullman did not need to regard his workers with hatred for this arrangement to become dominating. Paternalism rarely begins by saying that the people below deserve nothing.

It begins by saying that the people above know what is best.

The paternalist may genuinely want others to live better. They build the house, clean the street, provide the park, and establish the rule. Their care is expressed through design.

But design without participation contains a judgment:

You may receive the good life.

You may not help define it.

The difference between benevolence and shared power lies partly in who retains the right to interpret what is good.

---

The town worked best when the company prospered.

Orders remained strong. Wages generally supported the rents. The environment could be presented as evidence that corporate authority and worker welfare were compatible.

Then the economy changed.

The Panic of 1893 weakened the railroad industry and reduced demand for Pullman cars. The company cut jobs and wages. Rents in the company town did not fall correspondingly, and rent could be deducted directly from workers’ paychecks.[^c9-panic-rents]

The same arrangement that had appeared protective became difficult to escape.

The company controlled the wage.

The company controlled the rent.

A worker could receive a smaller paycheck while owing the same landlord, and the employer and landlord were the same institution.

The scale moved on both sides.

Income fell.

Obligation remained.

The town’s beauty did not vanish. The brick walls, parks, and careful design remained visible. But an attractive environment has limited value to a person who cannot afford food.

This is one of the limits of benevolent power-over.

It can provide benefits without creating the relationship through which recipients may challenge how those benefits are governed.

The person above may listen.

They may revise.

They may act compassionately.

But the person below remains dependent upon the willingness of authority to reconsider itself.

When conditions changed in Pullman, workers formed a grievance committee and attempted to negotiate. They wanted the company to address the relationship between wages and living costs.

The company did not make the concessions they sought.

Workers struck on May 11, 1894.[^c9-strike]

The model town had been designed to reduce conflict.

Its design had not eliminated conflicting interests.

It had concentrated them inside one relationship.

---

Power-over often appears efficient because it shortens the path between judgment and action.

One person decides.

A system executes.

The employer can coordinate the factory, housing, infrastructure, and services according to one plan. No prolonged negotiation is required with workers about the use of company property. No separate municipal government complicates the company’s vision. No scattered ownership interferes with the coherence of the town.

The same concentration that creates coherence also creates fragility.

When the central judgment is wrong, fewer independent centers possess the authority to correct it.

A worker can complain.

A resident can leave.

A committee can petition.

But if the institution controlling wages, housing, and work refuses to revise its interpretation, the person affected must either submit, exit, or gather enough counterpower to make refusal costly.

This is how a relationship presented as harmonious becomes a contest.

The worker’s grievance is not persuasive enough by itself.

It must acquire weight.

The strike attempts to place that weight on the other side of the scale.

Labor is withheld.

Production stops.

The cost of ignoring workers rises.

The strike does not escape the logic of power-over.

It uses that logic from below.

You can compel us because you control employment and property.

We will compel you by denying the labor upon which that property depends.

The relationship becomes visible through interruption.

Before the strike, the company appears to make the trains.

After workers stop, the achievement reveals its dependence.

---

The Pullman workers initially possessed less leverage than the company.

The factory could endure a temporary stoppage. Workers without wages could not wait indefinitely. The town itself gave the company continuing power over the people challenging it.

The conflict expanded when the American Railway Union, led by Eugene V. Debs, supported the Pullman workers. Union members refused to handle trains carrying Pullman cars, turning a local labor dispute into a national interruption of rail traffic.[^c9-strike]

The scale changed.

Pullman workers alone could not impose sufficient cost.

Railway workers acting across many companies could.

Shared action created power no individual worker had possessed.

But collective power did not escape moral complication merely because it arose from people with less institutional authority.

A rail boycott affected passengers, mail, businesses, workers, and communities far beyond the original dispute. People who had not determined Pullman’s policies experienced the consequences of the attempt to change them.

The union also carried its own exclusions. Black railroad workers had often been excluded from white unions, and the racial boundaries of organized labor weakened claims that workers had discovered a fully shared interest transcending every hierarchy.[^c9-racial-exclusion]

The people resisting one form of domination could reproduce another.

This is why the language of solidarity must be examined rather than simply admired.

A group may create power together internally while exercising power over people it refuses to include.

Workers can resist corporate domination and marginalize other workers.

A democratic majority can overcome an elite and oppress a minority.

A liberation movement can create discipline so rigid that dissent becomes betrayal.

Collective power is not automatically power-with.

The word collective tells us how many people are acting.

It does not tell us what kind of relationship they have created or who remains outside it.

---

The federal government intervened in the Pullman conflict.

A federal injunction ordered union leaders to stop interfering with rail traffic and the movement of the mail. President Grover Cleveland sent federal troops into Chicago. Violence intensified, the boycott collapsed, and Debs was jailed for violating the injunction.[^c9-strike]

Power gathered above both company and union.

The state asserted its authority to restore transportation and public order.

This authority also altered the balance between the parties. The Pullman Company did not need to defeat the national boycott through negotiation alone. Courts, injunctions, soldiers, and the federal government entered the conflict.

The state could describe its intervention as neutral enforcement.

The effects were not neutral.

An order to return trains to service weakened the workers’ primary source of leverage. Protecting the movement of commerce meant protecting the system through which the company’s power operated.

This does not prove that the government should have allowed every disruption to continue.

Rail systems carried mail, passengers, food, and commerce. Violence and property destruction created obligations the state could not simply ignore. A government that refuses to act whenever economic conflict becomes disruptive does not establish shared power. It leaves public order to whichever private force can prevail.

The harder question is what the state protects when it restores order.

Does it return the parties to a relationship in which meaningful negotiation is possible?

Or does it return the less powerful party to obedience?

Order can prevent harm.

It can also freeze an imbalance.

The scale may stop moving because one hand has pressed it down.

---

The commission appointed to investigate the strike later criticized the paternalism of Pullman’s company town and the company’s refusal to address workers’ grievances more constructively. The town’s attractive features had not compensated for an arrangement that denied residents meaningful independence and left them vulnerable when wages fell.[^c9-commission]

The criticism exposed the contradiction inside Pullman’s experiment.

The company wanted the loyalty associated with community.

It preserved the authority associated with property.

Workers were expected to experience the town as home while the company governed it as investment.

They were participants in the social life of the place and subordinates in the structure that controlled it.

This confusion persists in many institutions.

A company describes itself as a family but retains the right to end the relationship when business requires it.

A leader asks for candor while controlling every consequence of candor.

A government calls citizens partners while treating participation as consultation after the decision has been made.

A school asks students to take ownership of learning while granting them little authority over how learning is organized.

The language is mutual.

The structure is vertical.

This does not mean every hierarchy is dishonest.

A company is not literally a family. A school cannot ask every student to design the curriculum independently. A government cannot transfer every decision to whoever attends a meeting. Roles create real differences in obligation and authority.

The trouble begins when the institution wants the emotional benefits of partnership without accepting the constraints partnership places upon power.

It wants trust without vulnerability.

Loyalty without voice.

Responsibility without authority.

The bowl is passed, but one person still owns the table.

---

Mary Parker Follett spent much of her work examining this problem.

Writing and speaking in the early twentieth century, she distinguished between power-over and power-with. Power-over referred to coercive power exercised by one person or group upon another. Power-with referred to power developed through relationship: people studying the situation together, integrating knowledge, and creating a capacity that did not exist in either person separately.[^c9-follett-power]

The distinction can sound like a moral choice between bad and good behavior.

Power-over is domination.

Power-with is cooperation.

Choose cooperation.

But Follett’s thought was more demanding than an appeal to kindness.

She did not deny conflict.

She believed conflict could reveal differences that a group needed in order to understand the situation more completely. The goal was not to suppress disagreement or split the difference automatically. It was to discover whether the parties could create an integrated response that addressed the realities beneath their competing positions.

This required each person to enter the relationship prepared to be affected.

A manager did not merely invite worker input and then decide privately whether any of it was useful.

A worker did not merely gather enough leverage to replace the manager’s judgment with their own.

Both were asked to study the situation through knowledge neither possessed alone.

Follett’s related idea of the “law of the situation” attempted to depersonalize orders. Instead of one person obeying another simply because of rank, the people involved would examine what the situation required and allow authority to emerge from function, knowledge, and responsibility.[^c9-law-situation]

The phrase can be misunderstood.

Situations do not issue orders by themselves.

Someone still interprets the evidence.

Someone decides what counts as relevant.

Someone possesses the authority to act.

The appeal to the situation can become another disguise for power when the powerful person claims that necessity, the market, policy, efficiency, or reality itself has made the decision.

There is no alternative.

The budget requires it.

The data says so.

The law gives us no choice.

The decision-maker disappears behind the situation they have interpreted.

Power-with does not mean pretending interpretation has vanished.

It means that the people living within the situation participate meaningfully in discovering what it asks of them.

---

The difference can be seen in the giving of an order.

A supervisor sees a problem and tells a worker what to do.

The order may be correct.

The supervisor may possess wider information. They may understand downstream consequences the worker cannot see. The organization may need consistency, and the moment may not allow extended debate.

Power-over can coordinate.

It can also waste knowledge.

The worker may understand the machine, customer, route, patient, neighborhood, or exception more closely than the supervisor. An order given without that knowledge may solve the visible problem while creating another.

Power-with does not require the supervisor to abandon responsibility.

It requires the relationship to produce a better decision than either person’s isolated position could produce.

What are you seeing?

What am I missing?

Which constraint is real?

Which part of the instruction can change?

Who must decide if we cannot agree?

These questions do not make authority disappear.

They make authority more intelligent and more accountable.

The supervisor may still give the final instruction.

But the worker has not been reduced to the body through which someone else’s judgment becomes action.

Agency remains inside coordination.

---

This is easier when interests are compatible.

A worker wants to prevent a defect.

Management wants to prevent a defect.

They differ only because each sees a different part of the process. Shared investigation can produce a solution that serves both.

The challenge becomes harder when interests genuinely conflict.

Workers want higher wages.

Owners want to preserve returns.

Residents want affordable housing.

Developers want profitable construction.

A community wants environmental protection.

Employees want secure jobs in the industry being restricted.

No amount of listening guarantees an integrated answer that gives everyone everything they want.

Some conflicts reach the scale.

A resource must be divided.

A cost must be assigned.

A boundary must be enforced.

The phrase power-with becomes dishonest when it is used to make real losses sound consensual.

A leader says everyone collaborated on the plan, though only one side had the power to reject it.

A company invites employees to help choose how layoffs will occur, then calls the layoffs a shared decision.

A government consults communities about the location of a project whose construction has already been guaranteed.

Participation can soften power-over without changing who ultimately controls the outcome.

The meeting is mutual.

The decision is not.

Shared power must be honest about where integration ends and authority begins.

Who can say no?

Who bears the loss?

Who controls the alternatives?

What happens when agreement fails?

Without these questions, collaboration becomes a style through which domination speaks politely.

---

In December 1936, workers at General Motors plants in Flint, Michigan, used a tactic that made the company’s dependence upon them difficult to ignore.

They sat down.

Instead of leaving the factory and allowing production to resume with replacement workers, employees occupied the plants. By remaining inside, they controlled crucial facilities and prevented General Motors from operating them normally.

The sit-down strike began at the end of December and continued for approximately six weeks. Workers sought recognition of the United Automobile Workers and changes in wages, working conditions, job security, and the handling of grievances.[^c9-flint]

The action reversed the ordinary meaning of the factory.

The buildings belonged to General Motors.

The machinery belonged to General Motors.

Management possessed formal authority over production.

Yet production depended upon workers whose labor had been organized as though each person were replaceable within the system.

By occupying the plant, the workers used the physical structure of production as leverage against its legal owner.

They did not seize the company permanently.

They made the company unable to exercise ownership as usual.

The scale tilted.

---

A sit-down strike is difficult to describe as power-with.

The workers did not invite management into a collaborative inquiry and wait for a jointly discovered solution.

They created pressure.

They occupied property.

They prevented the company from producing.

General Motors sought injunctions and considered ways to regain control. Police confronted strikers, and violence occurred around the plants. Michigan governor Frank Murphy deployed the National Guard but resisted using it simply to remove the occupiers by force.[^c9-brigade]

The conflict demonstrates why people with less power sometimes need power-over.

An employer has continuing leverage built into the ordinary arrangement.

It can assign work, discipline employees, close facilities, alter schedules, and remove individuals from the workforce. These actions may be lawful expressions of ownership and management, but they shape the negotiating field before either side arrives at the table.

Workers entering that negotiation as isolated individuals do not meet the company on equal terms.

Collective action changes the cost of refusal.

It does not make the relationship equal in every respect.

It makes the workers difficult to ignore.

Power-over can therefore be protective.

A strike can compel negotiation where requests failed.

A court order can stop a violent person who refuses persuasion.

A government can prohibit discrimination even when those benefiting from the old arrangement have not agreed.

A boundary can preserve another person’s freedom from someone who would otherwise dominate them.

Shared power does not require the vulnerable to remain undefended while everyone waits for mutual understanding.

Sometimes power must interrupt power.

---

The Flint strike itself depended upon more people than the men occupying the plants.

Women organized food, first aid, communication, and public support. Members of the Women’s Emergency Brigade defended picket lines and helped sustain the strike from outside the occupied factories.[^c9-brigade]

The power created inside the plants depended upon care beyond them.

Workers could remain because food arrived.

The occupation could continue because families and community members performed labor the formal story of industrial conflict might treat as background.

The strike appears on the scale as labor against management.

The fuller relationship contains more sides.

Workers inside.

Women organizing outside.

Families absorbing lost wages.

Local businesses losing or extending credit.

Police receiving orders.

Government officials judging whether to intervene.

Other GM workers deciding whether to join.

The public deciding whose claims appeared legitimate.

Power is rarely held by only two actors even when the conflict is told that way.

The scale simplifies because the negotiation eventually requires identifiable parties.

General Motors.

The union.

But each party contains relationships that may be unequal within itself.

Who speaks for the workers?

Whose risks shape the strategy?

Whose labor sustains the action without entering the bargaining room?

Who can accept the settlement?

Collective bargaining creates a representative voice.

Representation necessarily compresses.

A union can make workers powerful enough to negotiate.

It can also develop its own hierarchy, incentives, exclusions, and distance from the people it represents.

The correction to corporate power is not automatically beyond the dangers of power.

It becomes another institution that must remain answerable.

---

On February 11, 1937, General Motors reached an agreement that recognized the United Automobile Workers as a bargaining representative for its members. The Flint occupation ended after forty-four days.[^c9-settlement]

The workers had acquired standing the company had resisted granting.

They did not become the owners of General Motors.

Management retained broad authority over the company.

The union did not eliminate hierarchy.

It changed the relationship through which hierarchy operated.

The individual worker no longer confronted the corporation only as an individual.

The company’s authority became subject to an organized counterparty capable of negotiating wages, conditions, discipline, and grievances.

This is sometimes called a balance of power.

The phrase fits the scale.

Management sits on one side.

Labor sits on the other.

Each possesses enough weight to prevent the other from acting without consequence.

Balance can protect freedom.

When neither side can dominate completely, negotiation becomes necessary. Rights become durable because someone has the capacity to defend them. Agreements can carry more credibility because neither participant depends solely upon the goodwill of the other.

But balance is not yet power-with.

Two armed countries can balance one another through fear.

Two departments can defend territory so successfully that the organization becomes incapable of learning.

A labor relationship can become a repeated exchange of demands, pressure, concession, and grievance without creating shared understanding of the work.

Counterpower restrains domination.

It does not necessarily create a common capacity.

This is why the scale is necessary and insufficient.

---

The language of partnership often becomes available only after power has been balanced.

A person with complete control has little reason to integrate another person’s knowledge. They may listen voluntarily, but nothing requires them to remain open when listening becomes inconvenient.

A person with no leverage may appear agreeable because disagreement is too costly.

The absence of visible conflict is then mistaken for trust.

Counterpower makes conflict safer to express.

The worker can raise a grievance because a process exists.

The minority can challenge the majority because rights limit what a vote may authorize.

The citizen can criticize an official because law protects speech and elections allow removal.

The partner with less money can negotiate because a contract clarifies ownership.

Boundaries and rights create the conditions in which power-with may become more than a request for kindness.

This is the paradox.

Shared power often requires the capacity to refuse sharing.

A union can cooperate meaningfully with management because it can withhold labor.

A community can negotiate because it has legal standing, organizing capacity, or political leverage.

A person can consent because no remains possible.

Without refusal, agreement may be only compliance.

The scale establishes that each side has weight.

The next question is whether the weight can be used for more than holding the other side in place.

---

Power-over also changes the person who holds it.

The obvious concern is what control does to those subjected to it.

They become cautious.

They wait for permission.

They hide mistakes.

They narrow their contribution to what authority has requested.

Less attention is given to what domination does to the person above.

Power reduces the amount of correction that reaches them.

People soften bad news.

They anticipate preferences.

They perform agreement.

The leader begins receiving evidence from a world already adapted to their authority.

This can produce a sincere but dangerous belief:

Everyone agrees.

The policy works.

People are not ready for more autonomy.

No one has raised a serious concern.

The more completely the institution organizes itself around one person’s judgment, the harder it becomes for that person to know what the institution actually sees.

Power-over weakens its own information.

This is one reason domination often requires increasing control.

The first rule produces concealment.

Concealment produces distrust.

Distrust produces monitoring.

Monitoring produces more concealment.

The person at the top concludes that the people below have confirmed the original need for supervision.

The scale grows heavier through the attempt to keep it still.

---

Benevolent power faces the same problem.

The leader may be intelligent, humane, and committed to the welfare of others.

They create good programs.

They protect people from arbitrary treatment.

They make better decisions than many alternatives would make.

The success becomes evidence that authority should remain concentrated.

Why introduce conflict when the current arrangement works?

Why risk decisions being made by people with less information?

Why distribute power when the person holding it uses it well?

The argument is strongest when the ruler really is wise.

A cruel authority reveals the need for limits.

A benevolent authority makes limits look unnecessary.

But institutions outlive character.

The next person may not be as wise.

The current person may be wrong about the very issue through which their goodness is being judged.

Those benefiting from the arrangement may have little capacity to correct it because the benevolent leader never helped them develop one.

Good power-over can leave people safe and dependent.

The leader’s success becomes the group’s weakness.

Power-with asks a different measure of leadership:

Not only, Did the person make good decisions?

Also, Did other people become more capable of making decisions together?

---

This does not mean every leader should continually surrender authority.

An emergency may require command.

A child may need protection before understanding the danger.

A professional role may carry responsibility that cannot be distributed casually.

A court must issue a ruling.

A government must sometimes enforce the ruling against people who continue to disagree.

Power-over remains part of public life.

The moral question is what follows its use.

Does emergency authority return when the emergency ends?

Does protection develop the protected person’s agency, or preserve dependence?

Does the expert explain the decision and remain answerable for error?

Does enforcement protect a shared rule or merely the people already strongest?

Does the person exercising control understand the power as a responsibility held within relationship, or as proof that they stand above it?

Legitimate authority can compel.

Domination treats compulsion as the normal shape of relationship.

The difference is not always visible from the command itself.

It appears in limits, accountability, reciprocity, and what the exercise of authority makes possible afterward.

A teacher and a student are not equals in classroom responsibility.

A judge and defendant do not possess the same authority in court.

A surgeon cannot govern an operation through a vote among everyone present.

But each role exists within a larger system of obligation.

The teacher’s authority should enlarge the student’s capacity.

The judge is constrained by law, evidence, procedure, and appeal.

The surgeon depends upon distributed expertise and remains accountable to the patient, profession, and institution.

Authority becomes less dominating when it is attached to function rather than human rank.

The person directs this action.

They do not become a higher kind of person.

---

The balance scale has a small point at its center.

The beam rests upon it.

Both sides move in relation to a support that belongs completely to neither.

Without the center, the comparison cannot occur.

Public conflict also depends upon things neither side should own completely.

Courts.

Election rules.

Evidence.

Contracts.

Professional standards.

Constitutional rights.

Shared facts.

The legitimacy of these institutions is always imperfect. Courts can favor the powerful. Rules can encode past injustice. Evidence can be selected, hidden, or interpreted through unequal access.

Still, a relationship becomes more than a contest when both sides accept some authority outside their immediate preference.

Follett’s law of the situation pointed toward this possibility.

The manager does not win because they are manager.

The worker does not win because they have gathered enough force.

Both ask what the work, evidence, purpose, limits, and people affected require.

The answer will not emerge without interpretation.

The interpretation will not always produce agreement.

But the center of the relationship begins shifting away from personal supremacy.

The question changes from:

Who has enough power to make the other obey?

To:

What can we understand and create only because neither of us is the whole situation?

This is the opening toward power-with.

---

The scale will not disappear.

There will still be unequal wealth, authority, expertise, risk, and responsibility. Some people will possess greater ability to act, interrupt, fund, punish, persuade, and leave.

A shared world cannot be built by denying this.

It must ask how leverage is constrained, how those affected gain standing, how refusal remains possible, and how power is prevented from becoming a permanent ranking of human worth.

Balance matters.

The Pullman workers needed leverage.

The Flint workers needed organization.

Rights need enforcement.

Boundaries need defenders.

But a perfectly balanced conflict can remain a conflict forever.

Each side watches the other.

Each accumulates enough weight to prevent loss.

Neither can move without threatening the balance.

The instrument has protected them from domination.

It has not shown them what they might make together.

For that, the parties must look somewhere else.

Not above, toward the person who will decide for everyone.

Not only across, toward the opponent whose weight must be matched.

They must look outward, toward the situation neither side can see completely from where it stands.

A scale compares what has already been placed upon it.

It cannot reveal what lies beyond the table.

For that, the room needs a window.
