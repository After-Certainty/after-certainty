# **Part IV — From Power-Over to Power-With**

A scale can look balanced while everything placed upon it remains separate.

One side rises as the other falls. Add weight here, and something must be removed there. The instrument teaches comparison through opposition.

More for one.

Less for another.

The image fits many conflicts because resources are limited and power is often distributed through loss. A promotion goes to one person. A budget funds one program instead of another. A court rules for one party. A government compels an action someone else resists.

Power can decide.

It can protect, coordinate, restrain, and enforce. A society unable to stop violence, collect taxes, uphold rights, or make binding decisions does not become free. It becomes vulnerable to whoever can act without permission.

The problem begins when power is imagined only as control over someone else.

Then every relationship becomes a contest over who will occupy the heavier side of the scale. The strong direct. The weak comply. Reform means transferring control from one group to another while leaving the structure of domination intact.

Shared power asks whether another form is possible.

Not power without authority.

Not cooperation without conflict.

Not a world in which every decision pleases everyone.

Power-with begins when people create a capacity through relationship that none possessed alone. It appears when knowledge moves across roles, when affected people help define the problem, when authority becomes answerable to those living with its consequences, and when coordination enlarges agency rather than replacing it.

This kind of power is less visible than command.

No single person can point to it and say, I did this.

Its achievements emerge through connection: workers who can stop a defective process, residents who can shape a public budget, communities that maintain peace without surrendering identity, caregivers whose knowledge changes the institution, and leaders who make other people more capable of judgment.

But shared creation does not make power innocent.

Groups can dominate outsiders.

Participation can conceal who still holds the final decision.

A collaborative process can exhaust people with less time while allowing powerful participants to describe the outcome as mutual.

Power-with must still confront unequal leverage, conflicting interests, bad faith, and the necessity of legitimate refusal.

The final part turns toward that difficulty.

The scale asks what happens when power is understood as something one person holds over another. It asks why domination can appear efficient, protective, and even benevolent—and what it costs the people whose agency it replaces.

The window asks what becomes visible when power is reorganized through relationship. A window does not remove the wall. It creates an opening through which one room can encounter what lies beyond its own boundaries.

Neither object promises harmony.

The scale can expose inequality.

The window can reveal difference.

The question is what we do after seeing them.

Shared power does not begin by pretending no one has more weight.

It begins by refusing to treat weight as the only form of power available.
