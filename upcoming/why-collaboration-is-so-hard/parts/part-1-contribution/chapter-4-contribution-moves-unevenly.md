# **Chapter 4**

## **Contribution Moves Unevenly**

In a university department revising its undergraduate curriculum, one
associate professor quietly maintains the spreadsheet everyone pretends
is temporary. They track which courses satisfy accreditation language,
which labs require new safety sign-offs, and which adjunct contracts expire
before the registrar's deadline. In committee meetings, chairs debate
intellectual direction. Afterward, the associate answers emails that begin,
"Sorry to bother you—quick question."

Their annual review praises "collegial service." A colleague who piloted
one high-profile seminar receives the public mention. Both contributions
are real. Only one maps easily onto the department's story about merit.

Collaboration rarely distributes effort, recognition, or risk evenly.

### **1. Invisible labor**

**Invisible labor** is work without which the collaboration fails, but
which does not appear in the artifacts the organization already knows how
to count: tickets closed, slides presented, revenue attributed.

Invisible labor includes maintenance, translation between functions,
onboarding newcomers, repairing trust after conflict, and holding context
when the group churns members. It often falls to people with social
capital to spare—or to people without enough capital to refuse.

When invisible labor is chronic, collaboration feels unstable to those who
perform it and efficient to those who benefit from it. That asymmetry is
a structural fault line, not a misunderstanding.

### **2. Conversational dominance and emotional maintenance**

Some participants dominate airtime not because they are narcissistic but
because the room rewards fast articulation, confident tone, or status.
Others do **emotional maintenance**: defusing tension, checking on quieter
members, reframing attacks as questions, absorbing irritation so the
meeting can end with a decision.

Maintenance work is collaboration work. It is also disproportionately
gendered and racialized in many organizations, and disproportionately
unrewarded.¹ Groups that treat smooth meetings as evidence of good
collaboration often depend on maintenance they never name.

### **3. Unequal participation and recognition**

Unequal participation is not only about who speaks. It is about who can
afford to speak: who risks being labeled "difficult," who is interpreted
as authoritative when silent, who must over-prepare to be heard once.

Recognition systems amplify the asymmetry. Promotions follow visible
wins. Bonuses follow measurable output. Collaboration metrics count
meetings attended or documents edited, not relational load carried.

The result is a familiar bitterness: "I keep this team functioning, and
the org rewards the people who present my work." Even when no one intends
injustice, **unequal recognition** teaches contributors that collaboration
is a bad bargain.

### **4. Risk absorption**

Contribution asymmetry includes **risk**: who loses reputation if the
project fails, who bears emotional cost if conflict erupts, who has exit
options if the group turns toxic.

Tenured staff, founders, and people with strong networks can challenge
bad decisions at lower personal cost. Contractors, junior staff, and
people from marginalized groups often contribute candor at higher risk.
Collaboration that depends on their silence is not stable. It is
extractive with a calmer surface.

### **5. Instability created by asymmetry**

When contribution moves unevenly, the group experiences instability even
if timelines look fine. High contributors burn out or withdraw. Low-
visibility contributors learn performative engagement. Leaders interpret
withdrawal as disengagement rather than as feedback about distribution.

### **6. When "steady" is a warning signal**

Organizations often praise **steady** collaborators—the person who never
misses a handoff, who absorbs the extra meeting, who translates after
conflict. Steady can mean reliable. It can also mean the group has located
elasticity in someone with least power to refuse.

Watching who remains steady while others spike and withdraw is a simple
diagnostic. If the same roles are steady through every crisis, asymmetry
is not an accident. It is a design the group is living inside, whether or
anyone drafted it.

Stabilizing practices—rotating facilitation, explicit credit for
maintenance, decision logs that name who did what—do not erase asymmetry.
They make it harder to pretend asymmetry is moral failure alone.

> Collaboration rarely distributes effort, recognition, or risk evenly.

Part I ends here with contribution grounded in lived asymmetry. Part II
turns to what groups build to survive fragility: structures that hold
collaboration together long enough for shared work to continue.
