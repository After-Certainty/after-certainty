# **Chapter 5**

## **The Structures That Hold Collaboration Together**

After a serious near-miss on a construction site, crews often adopt new
rituals without anyone calling them "culture change." A five-minute tailgate
briefing. A explicit sign-off before certain lifts. A rule that anyone can
stop work without penalty. None of these rituals eliminate risk. They
reduce the chance that risk enters the shared field unnoticed.

Collaborative groups develop similar structures: not because they love
process, but because fragility is easier to survive with scaffolding.

### **1. Rituals and predictable sequence**

**Rituals** turn ambiguous social situations into predictable sequences.
Standups, intake forms, handoffs, review meetings, and retrospectives are
rituals in this sense. Their power is not information transfer alone—
email can transfer information. Their power is **orientation**: everyone
knows when contribution is expected, in what form, and with what
authority.

Rituals fail when they become theater: the standup where no one listens,
the retrospective that never changes constraints. Failed rituals still
reveal hunger for structure. People want a recurring moment where partial
coherence can be rebuilt.

### **2. Facilitation and turn-taking**

**Facilitation** is specialized labor that keeps contribution possible
among people with uneven power and visibility. Good facilitators manage
time, elicit quiet voices, name decisions, and prevent premature closure.

**Turn-taking** norms—rounds, written comments before debate, paired
breakouts—reduce conversational dominance without requiring everyone to
be equally bold. They are especially important when expertise is uneven
but necessary: clinicians and families, engineers and designers, faculty
and students.

Facilitation is not neutral. Who facilitates, and whether they have
authority over outcomes, shapes what gets heard.

### **3. Shared language and documentation**

Groups invent **shared language**: terms for risk levels, definitions of
done, names for recurring failure modes. Shared language speeds partial
coherence. It also drifts. Documentation—decision logs, architecture
notes, care plans—anchors language to moments when the group meant
something specific.

Documentation is costly. Groups resist it until pain teaches them. The
pain usually arrives when key people leave and take context with them, or
when two functions discover they have been using the same word differently
for months.

### **4. Visible constraints and coordination norms**

Structures work best when **constraints are visible**: what is fixed, what
is negotiable, who can spend money, what safety means here. Visible
constraints reduce the need for heroic interpretation.

**Coordination norms** spell out how conflict is handled, how deadlines
slide, how escalation works. Norms do not need to be virtuous to be
useful. They need to be known.

### **5. What structures cannot do**

Collaborative practices do not eliminate fragility. They help groups
survive it.

Survival is not a small thing. A team that survives long enough to learn
often outperforms a team that collapses into blame at the first serious
wobble. But survival should not be marketed as transformation. Structures
can also be weaponized—used to simulate collaboration while reducing real
contribution to compliance. The chapters in Part III examine those
collapses.

> Collaborative practices do not eliminate fragility. They help groups
> survive it.
