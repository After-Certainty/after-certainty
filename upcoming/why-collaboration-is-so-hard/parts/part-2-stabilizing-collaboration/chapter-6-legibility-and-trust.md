# **Chapter 6**

## **Legibility and Trust**

A nonprofit board asks the executive director for "clearer metrics" on
program impact. The request sounds reasonable. Behind it is a board that
does not attend site visits, does not read long-form field reports, and
feels anxious about funding cycles. The director responds with dashboards:
outputs, reach, cost per beneficiary. The dashboards calm the board. They
also narrow what the organization optimizes for.

The collaboration between board and staff has not become more trusting.
It has become more **legible**—and legibility has partially substituted
for trust.

### **1. Visible work versus invisible work**

Organizations reward what they can see. **Visible work** maps cleanly to
artifacts and timelines: features shipped, cases closed, funds raised.
**Invisible work**—relationship repair, context-holding, risk
anticipation—does not.

Under pressure, groups often shift investment toward visible activity even
when invisible work is what prevents failure. Meetings multiply. Updates
proliferate. The collaboration **appears** more active while actual
coordination thins.

### **2. Why collaboration looks inefficient**

From outside a healthy collaboration, partial coherence can look messy:
debate, revision, pauses, side conversations. From inside, that mess may
be how the group tests judgments before committing shared resources.

When observers demand efficiency, they often mean **compress the visible
cycle**—fewer meetings, faster decisions, less talk. Compression can
raise throughput in the short term. It can also destroy the loops that
surface misunderstanding before it becomes expensive.

### **3. Pressure for premature clarity**

Anxiety produces **pressure for premature clarity**: define success now,
pick a metric now, end ambiguity now. Clarity is not the enemy. Premature
clarity is—because it forecloses learning in exchange for orientation.

Premature clarity often arrives as templates, mandatory fields, and
rigid workflows. Those tools can help. They can also signal that the
organization trusts process more than participants' judgment.

### **4. Narrating progress**

When trust is thin, groups require **narrated progress**: status updates,
percent-complete estimates, roadmap theater. Narration is a kind of
labor. It consumes time that could go into the work itself.

Narration is not always waste. It can rebuild shared orientation across
distance and turnover. It becomes coercive when narration is demanded
from contributors who already lack time, or when the narrative must be
optimistic to be acceptable.

### **5. Legibility substituting for trust**

A recurring pattern: **legibility substitutes for trust**. Instead of
investing in relationships, psychological safety, and credible repair after
conflict, the system demands measurable activity and explicit artifacts.

Legibility is cheaper to audit than trust. It also misaligns incentives.
People learn to optimize what is counted, not what is needed.

> Groups often prioritize what can be explained over what is actually
> helping.

Trust-heavy collaboration still uses metrics. The difference is sequence:
trust with legibility as a tool, rather than legibility as a replacement
for trust. The next chapter describes what stable collaboration feels like
from inside—not the idealized version, but the lived one.
