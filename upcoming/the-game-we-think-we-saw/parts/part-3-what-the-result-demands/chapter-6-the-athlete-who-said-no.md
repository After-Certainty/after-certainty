# Chapter 6

## The Athlete Who Said No

Simone Biles begins at the far end of the vault runway.

The apparatus is still. The judges are waiting. Her teammates stand together beyond the landing mat while the arena narrows toward the few seconds between the first step and the final landing.

She runs.

The sequence has been practiced often enough that it should no longer require ordinary thought. Feet accelerate toward the springboard. Hands meet the vault table. The body rises, turns, and finds the ground again. What spectators experience as flight is, for the gymnast, a chain of positions learned until one movement seems to summon the next.

But this time the chain breaks.

Biles had intended to perform a vault with two and a half twists. She completed only one and a half after losing her orientation in the air, landed awkwardly, and left the competition floor. Soon afterward, USA Gymnastics announced that she had withdrawn from the remaining events in the Olympic team final. Her teammates continued without her and won the silver medal.[^c6-tokyo-team]

From the stands, the mistake lasted only a few seconds.

Inside the movement, something more serious had disappeared.

Biles could still run. She could still jump. Her strength had not visibly left her. But the relationship between intention and location—the awareness that tells a gymnast where the body is while turning above the ground—was no longer reliable. She later described her mind and body as being out of sync and explained that the condition known as the twisties made it dangerous to perform skills she had completed for years.[^c6-twisties]

The audience could see the size of the moment.

Only the athlete could feel the condition of the body entering it.

### The Knowledge Inside the Movement

Every sport distributes knowledge unevenly.

A coach may understand the strategy better than any one player. A trainer may recognize a pattern of movement the athlete has learned to ignore. Teammates may see hesitation that the person performing the action cannot feel. Video may reveal a mechanical change invisible at full speed.

But some knowledge remains inside the performance.

The gymnast knows whether the air still feels organized. The pitcher knows whether the ball has begun leaving the hand differently. The quarterback knows whether the injured shoulder can make the throw the play requires. The tennis player knows what happens internally before walking into a room that others experience as routine.

This knowledge is not automatically accurate.

Athletes misread their bodies. They minimize injury, mistake fear for danger, or mistake danger for ordinary fear. They can want to compete badly enough that warning signs become easy to explain away. Chapter 5 began with precisely that problem: the athlete on the training table saying, “I’m good,” while everyone nearby has an interest in believing it.

Situated knowledge is not infallibility.

But neither is it merely another opinion.

The person performing the movement occupies a position no observer can fully reproduce. A camera may show where the body went. It cannot feel the absence of orientation before the body arrived there. A coach may know what the routine requires. The coach does not enter the air on the athlete’s behalf.

Knowledge matters differently when consequence is also distributed unevenly.

If the judgment is wrong, the coach loses an athlete from the lineup. The team may lose points or a medal. The audience loses the performance it expected to see.

The gymnast loses control of the body above the ground.

### The Authority of the Person at Risk

Authority in sports is often granted according to role.

The coach chooses the lineup. The official applies the rules. The physician grants clearance. The league determines eligibility. The athlete is expected to perform within the decisions those authorities have made.

Each role solves a real problem.

A team cannot function if every player independently chooses strategy. Medical judgment cannot depend entirely on whether an athlete wants to play. A league cannot allow participants to decide which obligations apply only when convenient.

Yet the legitimacy of these roles does not make the athlete’s judgment subordinate in every matter.

The athlete possesses two things that often appear separately elsewhere: unique knowledge and direct exposure to consequence.

A coach may have more expertise about the event. A physician may have more expertise about injury. A teammate may have a legitimate claim arising from shared preparation. But when the decision concerns whether this body can safely enter this action now, the person inhabiting the body possesses evidence no one else can entirely obtain.

That does not create absolute authority.

It creates presumptive authority.

The difference matters.

Absolute authority would mean the athlete’s account cannot be questioned. Presumptive authority means the account begins with weight because of where the athlete stands. Others may challenge it with strong evidence, medical findings, visible impairment, or a pattern of unreliable judgment. But they should not begin by treating the athlete’s knowledge as an obstacle to the institution’s preferred answer.

The burden should not always fall on the person at risk to prove that an invisible condition is real.

### What the Team Knows

The team’s claim cannot simply be dismissed.

Biles had not arrived in Tokyo as an individual visitor. She was part of a four-person team whose scores would be combined. Her routines affected Sunisa Lee, Jordan Chiles, and Grace McCallum. When she withdrew, the remaining gymnasts had to alter their assignments under Olympic pressure and perform routines they had not necessarily expected to contribute in that configuration. They did so and won silver.[^c6-teammates]

The team knew things Biles did not know alone.

It knew how her absence would change the order. It knew who would have to perform next. It knew the commitments everyone had made and the preparation built around an expected lineup. It knew that withdrawal would transfer pressure rather than eliminate it.

These were not imaginary costs invented to override her.

A refusal can be necessary and still burden other people.

That is one of the difficulties hidden by the language of individual choice. We sometimes speak as though respect for an athlete’s decision requires pretending the decision has no consequence beyond the athlete. But teams are systems of reliance. One person’s absence becomes another person’s responsibility.

Biles’s teammates were allowed to be surprised. They were allowed to feel pressure, disappointment, or fear. The legitimacy of her withdrawal did not require their experience to become effortless.

The question was not whether the team had a claim.

It was what kind of claim it had.

The team could ask for honesty. It could ask Biles to communicate what she knew as soon as she could. It could reorganize the competition and make future decisions about readiness.

It could not safely answer the question of whether she still possessed control in the air merely because it needed her score.

Collective dependence gives a team a legitimate interest in the athlete’s decision.

It does not give the team ownership of the athlete’s risk.

### The Performance Everyone Expected

Expectation becomes most powerful when it has already been earned.

Biles entered Tokyo as the reigning Olympic all-around champion and the central figure in a heavily favored American team. She had spent years performing skills other gymnasts did not attempt and had arrived at the Games carrying the public expectation that she would again dominate the competition.[^c6-expectations]

This history made her withdrawal harder to interpret.

A less accomplished gymnast might have been described as overwhelmed. Biles had already proven that she could perform under Olympic pressure. A less technically capable gymnast might have been told the routine exceeded her ability. Biles had built a career by exceeding the established boundaries of the sport.

The normal explanations did not fit.

So the withdrawal entered a moral argument.

Was she protecting herself or abandoning the team? Was the decision evidence of self-knowledge or a failure to meet the moment? Did mental well-being justify leaving an Olympic final? Had the meaning of toughness changed, or had the athlete failed to display it?

These questions gathered around the wrong assumption: that the withdrawal expressed how much Biles wanted the result.

The twisties were not simply reluctance intensified by pressure. They involved a disruption in the athlete’s ability to locate the body during complex airborne movement. Continuing would not have demonstrated that she cared more. It would have demonstrated that she was willing to place greater trust in a system she no longer trusted.[^c6-twisties]

Wanting the moment cannot make the movement available.

The stadium can make the stakes larger.

It cannot make the body more certain.

### Refusal Is Not the Opposite of Commitment

A refusal is often interpreted as the absence of whatever virtue participation would have displayed.

If playing demonstrates courage, declining must demonstrate fear.

If continuing demonstrates loyalty, stopping must demonstrate selfishness.

If carrying the burden demonstrates leadership, setting it down must demonstrate abandonment.

But actions do not receive meaning through opposition alone.

A person may stop because the goal no longer matters. A person may also stop because the goal matters enough not to turn a damaged capacity into a danger for everyone depending on it.

After leaving the team final, Biles remained with her teammates, encouraged them, and continued assessing whether she could safely compete in later events. She framed the decision as protecting both her safety and the team’s medal chances rather than continuing routines whose outcomes had become unpredictable, and USA Gymnastics publicly supported prioritizing her well-being.[^c6-usag-frame]

This does not mean every withdrawal automatically serves the team.

Athletes can avoid difficult situations and describe avoidance as self-protection. They can communicate late, shift preventable burdens onto others, or use private knowledge as a shield against accountability. The language of well-being can become as resistant to examination as the language of toughness.

The meaningful question is not whether the athlete said no.

It is what the refusal was responsive to.

Did the athlete identify a condition relevant to performance or safety?

Was the concern communicated honestly?

Did the athlete remain willing to engage with the consequences of the decision?

Was the refusal limited to what could not responsibly be done, or did it become a claim that all surrounding obligations had disappeared?

Biles did not declare that the competition no longer mattered.

She declared that she could not safely provide the performance everyone expected from her.

The distinction is small in language and enormous in practice.

### The Body the Audience Cannot Borrow

Spectators enter sports through identification.

They imagine taking the shot, throwing the pass, standing on the starting line, or hearing the crowd before the routine. This imagined participation is part of the pleasure. The athlete’s body becomes the place where the audience experiences a possibility unavailable to it.

But identification can become possession.

The spectator feels the significance of the event and assumes that feeling provides knowledge of what the athlete should do inside it. The fan has waited years for the competition. The ticket was expensive. The athlete represents a team, a country, or an entire era of expectation.

The audience experiences these stakes genuinely.

It does not experience the movement.

No amount of emotional investment can transfer the bodily consequence from the athlete to the observer. The spectator may be devastated by withdrawal and still remain physically untouched by the decision to continue.

This asymmetry is easy to forget because sports makes consequences appear shared.

“We won.”

“We lost.”

“We needed her.”

The language is part of belonging. Fans do carry disappointment, hope, identity, and memory. But the word we changes meaning near the edge of injury.

The audience can share the result.

It cannot share the landing.

### When the Refusal Concerns the Job Around the Game

Naomi Osaka’s refusal at the 2021 French Open did not concern whether she could swing a racket or move across the court.

It concerned what happened afterward.

Before the tournament, Osaka announced that she would not participate in the required post-match press conferences because of their effects on her mental health. After she skipped the news conference following her first-round victory, the tournament fined her $15,000. The four Grand Slam tournaments issued a joint statement describing media appearances as a contractual obligation and warning that repeated violations could lead to escalating sanctions, including default and possible suspension from future major tournaments. Osaka then withdrew from the event.[^c6-osaka-fo]

The dispute revealed a different boundary around athletic participation.

Osaka had not refused to compete. She had refused one part of the institutional package attached to competition. The tournament’s response made clear that, in its view, playing the match did not complete the athlete’s obligation.

There were legitimate reasons for that position.

Professional tennis depends partly on public attention. Reporters help carry the event beyond the court. Tournament rules are meant to apply consistently, rather than allowing the most famous or financially secure players to choose obligations that less powerful athletes must still meet. A player who accepts entry into a competition does not automatically gain the right to rewrite every condition unilaterally.

Osaka later acknowledged that tennis carries off-court commitments and clarified that her objection concerned the traditional press-conference format rather than the existence of sports journalism. She proposed limited mental-health absences from media duties without requiring athletes to disclose private medical details.[^c6-osaka-time]

Her case therefore resists a simple division between athlete and institution.

The tournament was not inventing the media requirement after she arrived. Osaka was not claiming that all obligations disappeared when they became unpleasant. She was arguing that one established obligation had a cost the institution’s normal process did not adequately recognize.

Her refusal made the architecture visible.

Until someone declined, the press conference could appear to be a natural extension of the match. Afterward, it became possible to see it as a separate demand—valuable to the sport, unequally experienced by athletes, and capable of being redesigned.

Refusal does not always prove that an obligation is illegitimate.

It often reveals that the obligation had been treated as inevitable before anyone had to justify it.

### The Right to Question the Refusal

Athlete-centered authority can become intellectually lazy when it asks others to stop examining a decision as soon as the athlete names a private concern.

Privacy matters. A person should not have to publish every symptom to earn protection. But institutions also make decisions under uncertainty. Coaches select rosters. Tournaments enforce common rules. Teams need enough information to distinguish temporary difficulty from unavailability.

There must therefore be room for questions.

Was another accommodation available?

Could the athlete have raised the concern earlier?

Does the refusal affect an obligation essential to fair competition?

Would granting the exception create an unequal burden for other participants?

Is the athlete’s account consistent with visible evidence and prior communication?

Questions are not automatically disbelief.

They become illegitimate when the athlete’s inability to satisfy observers is treated as evidence that the condition does not exist. They become coercive when the institution demands increasing personal disclosure until the athlete has suffered publicly enough to deserve the boundary.

The problem is not inquiry.

It is the assumption that the institution begins neutral while the athlete begins self-interested.

Both have interests.

The athlete may want to avoid pain, scrutiny, embarrassment, or responsibility. The institution may want the athlete to compete, appear, generate attention, or preserve established arrangements.

Neither perspective should be romanticized.

But only one person directly carries some consequences.

A fair process should recognize that asymmetry before asking the athlete to overcome it.

### The Exit That Changed the Team

Andrew Luck’s refusal came at a larger boundary.

He did not leave one game or one obligation. He left football.

In August 2019, shortly before the regular season, news spread during an Indianapolis Colts preseason game that Luck intended to retire. Some fans booed as he walked toward the tunnel. At the press conference that followed, the twenty-nine-year-old quarterback described years of cycling through injury, pain, and rehabilitation. He said the process had become unrelenting and had taken away his joy in the game.[^c6-luck]

The timing made the cost impossible to hide.

The Colts had built their plans around him. Teammates had trained expecting him to return. Coaches had designed an offense for a franchise quarterback. Fans had purchased tickets while imagining a season that now had to be reorganized weeks before it began.

Luck’s retirement was his decision.

Its consequences belonged to many people.

The boos expressed that collision in its rawest form. Some spectators did not experience the moment first as the end of a person’s injury cycle. They experienced it as the sudden removal of the future they had expected him to provide.

This does not make the reaction admirable.

It does make it intelligible.

A star athlete occupies an unusual moral position. The work is personal, but its meaning has been distributed. The player signs a contract, accepts compensation, and becomes the center of plans made by people who cannot easily replace the performance. To leave is not the same as walking away from an expectation no one had reason to hold.

Luck owed the team honesty.

He owed it whatever contractual consequences legitimately followed.

He did not owe football unlimited access to a life he could no longer inhabit without unacceptable cost.

The distinction does not remove the loss. It locates the boundary of the claim.

The organization could be harmed by his departure without acquiring the right to require his continuation.

### The Team Is Allowed to Continue

When an athlete says no, the surrounding institution must do more than decide whether the refusal was justified.

It must continue.

Another gymnast performs the routine. Another quarterback takes the snaps. A tournament draw moves forward without the withdrawing player. Teammates convert surprise into preparation because the competition does not pause until everyone has settled the meaning of the absence.

This continuation can feel like a judgment.

The athlete sees the replacement succeed and wonders whether the refusal revealed dispensability. The team struggles and the athlete’s absence becomes the easiest explanation. Commentators revisit the decision each time the result creates a new emotional need.

But continuation is not betrayal.

A team can respect an athlete’s boundary and still protect itself from dependency. Coaches can prepare replacements. Organizations can reconsider whether too much capacity has been concentrated in one person. Teammates can acknowledge the cost without converting it into accusation.

In Tokyo, Lee, Chiles, and McCallum competed after Biles withdrew and won silver. Their achievement should not be reduced to evidence that Biles was unnecessary, nor should it be used merely as proof that her decision was correct. It belonged to the gymnasts who performed under altered conditions.[^c6-teammates]

A refusal creates space others may have to fill.

What they do in that space is also theirs.

### Presumptive Authority

The athlete’s judgment should not be treated as final merely because it concerns the athlete.

But it should begin with a presumption.

That presumption rests on two asymmetries:

The athlete possesses knowledge others cannot fully access.

The athlete bears consequences others cannot fully share.

This is not enough to settle every dispute. A concussed player may be precisely the wrong person to determine readiness. A gymnast may underestimate a danger others can see. A tennis player may frame an ordinary obligation as uniquely harmful. A veteran may believe past excellence entitles them to choose participation without regard for teammates.

Presumptive authority does not eliminate counterevidence.

It determines how uncertainty is handled.

When evidence remains incomplete, who receives the benefit of the doubt?

The institution usually possesses procedural advantage. It controls selection, access, contracts, medical systems, public statements, and the language through which participation is judged. Without an intentional presumption toward the person carrying the risk, uncertainty will naturally resolve toward what the institution needs.

The athlete must then prove not only that the condition exists, but that it is serious enough to defeat the surrounding demand.

The more important the event becomes, the harder that proof becomes to supply.

A small injury may justify rest in October but not in the championship game. The body has not necessarily changed. The institution’s willingness to hear it has.

Presumptive authority resists that movement.

It says that the stakes may increase the cost of refusal, but they do not make private knowledge less relevant or bodily consequence less direct.

### The Return to the Beam

Biles did not compete in the Olympic all-around, vault, uneven-bars, or floor finals in Tokyo. She returned for the final women’s event, the balance beam, and won bronze.[^c6-beam]

The return complicated the story of withdrawal.

She had not become incapable of all gymnastics. She had not chosen between complete participation and complete departure. She found one event and one routine she could perform within the condition she was still managing.

That distinction matters because institutions often prefer binary categories.

Available or unavailable.
Committed or uncommitted.
Healthy or injured.
In or out.

Bodies do not always obey those boundaries.

A person may be unable to perform one action safely while remaining capable of another. An athlete may need to refuse today without knowing what will be possible tomorrow. The demand for a complete answer can force someone to claim more certainty than the body provides.

Biles’s return did not retroactively prove that every earlier withdrawal was correct. The bronze medal was not evidence entered in defense of her judgment.

It showed something else.

Refusal can be specific.

An athlete can say no to the action that has become unsafe while remaining in relationship with the sport, the team, and the possibility of returning.

### The Vindication We Should Not Require

Biles eventually returned to elite gymnastics. At the Paris Olympics in 2024, she won gold medals in the team competition, all-around, and vault, along with a silver medal on floor exercise.[^c6-paris]

The comeback changed how some people remembered Tokyo.

Withdrawal could now become part of a resilience story. The athlete had stepped away, recovered, returned, and won again. The later result made the earlier refusal easier to admire.

But this creates another danger.

A boundary should not need a triumphant sequel to become legitimate.

Had Biles never returned, she would still have been the person best positioned to report that she had lost reliable orientation in the air. Had Luck regretted retirement, the years of injury and rehabilitation would not become imaginary. Had Osaka never won another major title, the cost she described would not become less real.

We are drawn to vindication because it restores the familiar authority of winning.

The athlete said no, but look what the decision later produced.

The result arrives again to justify the method.

This book has already asked why winning should be allowed to perform that work. The same caution applies here. Refusal is not correct only when it preserves future excellence. Sometimes stopping protects a return. Sometimes it protects an ordinary life. Sometimes it changes nothing visible except that the person no longer crosses a boundary they could not safely cross.

The legitimacy of the decision must be evaluated from the knowledge and consequences available then.

Paris cannot go back to Tokyo and enter the air on Biles’s behalf.

### The Apparatus Remains

The vault stands in the same place after Biles leaves the floor.

Its measurements have not changed. The runway is still open. The judges remain seated. The team competition continues because sport cannot wait for every absence to become understandable.

From a distance, the apparatus looks almost ordinary.

The danger is difficult to see because it does not reside in the equipment alone. It exists in the relationship between the equipment and the body approaching it: speed, force, memory, orientation, and the trust that one movement will still call forth the next.

Everyone can see what the routine is worth.

Only the gymnast can feel whether the routine is still there.

That knowledge does not make the athlete sovereign over every consequence. Teammates still matter. Commitments still matter. Institutions may question, verify, accommodate, or reorganize. The athlete may be wrong.

But uncertainty does not erase position.

The person entering the air knows something the people waiting below do not. The person carries something they will not carry if the judgment fails.

This is why refusal can be an act of authority rather than its rejection.

The athlete does not control the game, the team, or the meaning others will assign to the absence. The athlete controls the final step toward a consequence no one else can accept in their place.

The competition continues. Another gymnast walks toward the apparatus. The scoreboard keeps accumulating results.

The absence remains visible because everyone expected the athlete to be there.

What remains invisible is the movement that did not happen, the landing no one had to watch, and the injury that never became necessary before the decision could be believed.

The audience can see the size of the moment.

Only the athlete can feel the condition of the body entering it.
