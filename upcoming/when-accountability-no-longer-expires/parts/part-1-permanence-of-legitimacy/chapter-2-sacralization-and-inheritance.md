# **Chapter 2**

## **Sacralization and Inheritance**

There is a moment every institution points back to.

A founding assembly.\
A revolution.\
A prophetic calling.\
A decisive break from the past.

In that moment, something important happens. Authority is justified.
Power is explained. Sacrifice is remembered. The story is told
carefully, often with reverence.

Over time, the details of the moment become less important than the role
the origin story plays.

The origin becomes unquestionable.

### **When origins stop being historical**

At first, origin stories are descriptive.

They answer reasonable questions:

- Why does this institution exist?

- What problem was it meant to solve?

- What values justified its authority?

But as legitimacy accumulates, origins begin to change roles.

They stop explaining authority and start **protecting it**.

When that happens, the origin is no longer treated as history.\
It is treated as **moral inheritance**.

Questioning it feels like ingratitude.\
Reexamining it feels destabilizing.\
Critiquing it feels dangerous.

This shift does not require belief in anything supernatural.

It requires only that the origin carry moral and emotional weight.

### **Sacralization without mysticism**

Sacralization is often misunderstood.

It does not require miracles, divine claims, or religious language. It
occurs whenever an origin story becomes morally untouchable—when it is
treated as too important to revisit honestly.

A constitution can be sacralized.\
A revolution can be sacralized.\
A founding CEO’s vision can be sacralized.\
A prophetic calling can be sacralized.

The common feature is not theology.

It is **moral insulation**.

Once an origin is sacralized, it no longer needs to defend itself. It is
assumed to be good, necessary, and beyond ordinary evaluation.

### **How inheritance replaces justification**

Sacralized origins do a specific kind of work.

They allow legitimacy to be **inherited rather than re-earned**.

Authority no longer needs to justify itself by present performance
alone. It draws credibility from proximity to the origin:

- Fidelity to founding principles

- Alignment with original intent

- Continuity with those who were there

In practice, this means authority flows forward even when conditions
change.

What mattered at the beginning continues to matter long after its
original justification has weakened or expired.

Legitimacy becomes something one receives by position, lineage, or
office—not something one must continuously reestablish.

### **Origin stories as moral shields**

Once sacralized, origins begin to do defensive work.

They are invoked to:

- End debate (“This is what we were founded on”)

- Reframe criticism (“That’s not who we are”)

- Redirect accountability (“We’ve always believed…”)

In these moments, the origin is no longer a reference point.\
It is a **shield**.

Harm is interpreted in light of the origin rather than the origin being
reexamined in light of harm.

This reversal is subtle. It often goes unnoticed by the people
participating in it.

But its effects accumulate.

### **Why this feels responsible**

From the inside, this inheritance often feels like care rather than
avoidance.

Protecting the origin:

- Honors sacrifice

- Preserves shared identity

- Prevents endless reopening of settled questions

- Creates continuity across generations

These are real goods.

Institutions that constantly relitigate their origins rarely survive.
Stability requires some form of closure.

The danger is not that origins are remembered.

The danger is that they become **immune to revision**, even when new
information, new harms, or new moral insights emerge.

### **Sacralization and permanence**

When sacralization combines with inherited legitimacy, authority
acquires a long memory and a long lifespan.

Failures do not automatically threaten legitimacy.\
Contradictions do not force reconsideration.\
Correction can occur without disturbing the foundation.

At this point, legitimacy is no longer something the institution holds
provisionally.

It is something it assumes.

This is how authority stops expiring.

### **What this sets in motion**

Sacralized origins explain why institutions can survive moral strain
without denying it.

They provide a moral reservoir that authority can draw from even when
present actions are contested.

But they also create a condition where harm can persist without forcing
a reckoning—because legitimacy has already been settled in the past.

The next chapter examines what happens once authority secured in this
way encounters harm.

Not spectacular harm.\
Not malicious harm.

But ordinary harm that arises when durable authority continues to act.
