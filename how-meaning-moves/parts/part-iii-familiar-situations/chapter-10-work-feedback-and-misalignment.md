# **Chapter 10 — Work, Feedback, and Misalignment**

Workplaces reward compression. Decisions have to land; feedback has to stick. Fast gets treated as capable, clear as effective. In that air, people expect talk to race from signal to action, ambiguity slows coordination, and delay reads as negligence.[^c10-march-simon-organizations] Pressure reshapes how meaning moves on the job.

## **Why Work Accelerates Compression**

Organizations exist to act. Deadlines, targets, scoreboards, and accountability all push talk toward quick reads. People compress meaning so work keeps moving. Interpretation finishes early because everyone needs a shared next step.

That efficiency is often necessary. It is also easy to break. When compression outruns contact, misalignment stops being a surprise and starts being the wallpaper.

Work relationships are a distinct kind of **connection**: mutual reliance, belonging, and safety under role—not the density of family, but real dependence nonetheless. When feedback or deadlines compress meaning too fast, people often hear judgment not only on output but on standing—whether they are trusted, whether they still belong. Misalignment frays **connection** quietly: coordination holds while candor withdraws; people stay “aligned” in behavior while privately revising what they believe about leaders and peers.

## **Feedback Under Signal**

Feedback carries heavy signal. It almost never lands as “neutral information.” It implies judgment, stakes, and comparison. Even careful feedback shows up with questions about competence, standing, and fit.

When that signal goes unnamed, listeners often hear threat. They defend, spin, or pull back—not because they hate input, but because meaning moved too fast to handle safely.[^c10-edmondson-fearless-organization] The words may be fair even when the pace and framing are not.

## **Why Feedback Feels Personal Even When It Is Not**

At work, feedback usually aims at behavior or output. Listeners often hear commentary on who they are. That is not mere thin skin; it is compression doing its job. Under pressure, people answer fast: *What does this say about me? Am I safe here? What do I do next?* Those questions fire before slower reflection gets a seat.[^c10-steele-whistling-vivaldi] Once identity feels touched, listening narrows.

## **Restraint Disappears in Performance Cultures**

Cultures built on performance often punish slowing down. Pause reads as waste, clarifying reads as defensiveness, and slowing feedback reads like indulgence while outcomes loom. So restraint—the force that keeps interpretation unfinished—erodes first. Talk jumps from signal to verdict. People react to what feedback *seems* to mean instead of what it actually says, and misalignment follows.

## **Why Clarification Often Backfires**

When feedback misfires, the reflex is to clarify: add detail, repeat the standard, offer examples. Sometimes that helps; often it does not. Clarification adds content, not contact, so trying to clarify does not guarantee that **Meaning gets clarified**. When a story has already hardened, more explanation gets sifted for proof of the old story. Certainty thickens instead of softening.[^c10-nickerson-confirmation-bias] When that hardening holds, **Meaning gets distorted**: what the speaker means as help can land as pressure on the listener.

## **Defensive Reactions as Speed, Not Resistance**

“Defensive” is often misread as refusal to learn. More often it is overload. When meaning arrives faster than it can be absorbed, people narrow their read to stay upright. They justify, minimize, or step back to regain footing. That is not always rejection of feedback; it is a safety response to speed.

## **How Misalignment Becomes Normal**

Over time, repeated compression turns crooked alignment into habit. People learn to expect misunderstanding. Managers learn not to expect welcome. Everyone lowers the bar and drives on. Work still happens while communication quietly frays. That is how places stay busy while turning brittle.[^c10-perrow-normal-accidents]

## **What Restraint Would Have Slowed**

Restraint at work does not erase performance pressure. It slows the jump from signal to judgment, leaves room to test a reading before it locks, and lets feedback stay informational long enough to be examined. Without restraint, feedback becomes verdict. With restraint, feedback can stay open—even when action still has to happen.

## **What Work Reveals About Communication**

Work highlights a basic tension: efficiency wants compression; understanding wants contact. Systems optimize for the first and hope the second follows. When it does not, misalignment gets blamed on individuals instead of on the pace everyone is asked to keep. At stake is often **professional connection** itself—whether people feel licensed to bring trouble early or trained to hide it until metrics slip. Naming that does not repair feedback, but it explains why feedback fails so predictably.

## **What Remains Possible at Work**

Feedback will never be perfectly safe. Misalignment will never vanish. What restraint can preserve is a chance to revisit meaning before it hardens into identity or judgment. At work, that chance is fragile and expensive. Without it, speed replaces contact, and communication collapses in plain sight.

> *Feedback fails when speed replaces contact—and work can still look aligned while candor quietly withdraws.*

### **The Aligned Surface**

::: {custom-style="Vignette Block"}
The feedback was brief and well prepared. He had examples, framed it constructively, and emphasized that it was not personal.

She listened carefully, nodding in the right places. She asked a clarifying question and wrote things down.

What settled was not confusion. It was a conclusion.

*This is how I’m being seen.
This is what matters now.*

Over the next few weeks, she adjusted. She stopped volunteering ideas that had not been requested, checked assumptions twice before speaking, and focused on what would land cleanly.

From the outside, things improved: fewer questions, faster delivery, less friction. In the next review, he noted the progress.

“You seem more aligned,” he said.

She agreed.

What neither of them named was what had quietly narrowed—or how much of the original work had disappeared in the process.
