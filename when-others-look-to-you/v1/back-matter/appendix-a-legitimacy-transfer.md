# **Appendix A — Legitimacy Transfer in Practice**

This appendix uses the same lenses—**harm**, **effectiveness**, **legitimacy**—on how authority changes hands. It is not a ranking of leaders, a partisan scorecard, or a verdict on any one institution. The aim is to show how legitimacy transfers, hardens, and can stay expanded after leadership changes hands.

Legitimacy transfer is not a political phenomenon. It is a structural one. Wherever authority changes hands at scale—in government, in healthcare, in religious institutions, in large organizations—the same pattern tends to appear: what one leader was permitted to do becomes the baseline the next leader inherits.

Crisis widens that baseline fast. Restraint can slow its expansion but may not undo it. Personalization can ride on inherited permissions without requiring new ones. Norms can return before hard limits do.

Three sequences below show that pattern across different institutions. They are not equivalent in stakes or in kind. They share a structure.

### **The Transfer Pattern**

At transfer, example-based legitimacy asks whether the new authority holder’s visible conduct matches what the role has represented—not just what was said when authority passed.

Procedural legitimacy asks whether the rules and roles that govern transfer are still functioning as real constraints or mostly as ceremony.

Office-based legitimacy asks what the position itself now carries: what permissions have accumulated in the role that move forward automatically with the title.

Sacralized legitimacy asks whether the transfer itself has become exempt from ordinary challenge—whether questioning who leads next sounds like attacking something the group is not supposed to reopen.

Each form ties accountability to authority a little differently. Each transfers a little differently too.

### **Government at Scale**

The U.S. presidency is designed around formal process. Terms end and restart. Law and separate branches are meant to limit what any one president can do. That design does not stop transfer; it makes transfer easier to see. At scale, powers used under one administration can become the expected baseline for the next. Permission can move forward faster than limits do, unless people actively renew those limits.

After September 11, emergency logic widened how much the president could do outside usual limits and made exceptional measures routine in the name of speed and security. Some measures were framed as temporary; several became lasting precedents. The main form stayed **procedural legitimacy**, but emergency permissions granted by process widened what the office was treated as allowed to do next—the direction runs from procedural toward office-based expansion.

The next phase emphasized procedural restoration and a slower, more careful tone. Some expanded powers were used more lightly; many were still available on paper. The lesson is not hypocrisy or bad faith. It is about design: holding back in use does not by itself roll back what the office already inherited. The emphasis stayed **procedural legitimacy** while practice meant tighter use of inherited powers without a full rollback of what the office had gained—office-based carryover with little procedural pull-back.

The following phase intensified personalization. Disagreement was framed as disloyalty; criticism of process, as a status fight. No entirely new constitutional design was required—the office already held wide permissions. **Office-based legitimacy** dominated: inherited office permissions were used to personalize power, and challenge started to sound like betrayal—the direction runs from office-based forms toward sacralization pressure.

The next phase renewed procedural language and familiar public expectations about how the office should behave. Professional process and formal restraint were stressed again. Inertia remained: many inherited authorities stayed with the office even as talk shifted toward “back to normal.” **Procedural legitimacy** renewed in language and process, but procedural restoration ran inside office permissions that only shrank slowly—procedural repair layered on top of office-based inertia.

Across these handoffs, crisis widens authority fast. Restraint can slow misuse but may not undo transfer. Personalization can ride on inherited permissions. Norms can come back before hard limits do. Expansion is easier than contraction unless people keep doing the work of limiting power over time.

### **Healthcare Networks**

A regional hospital network grants emergency exception authority during a capacity surge: faster purchasing, staffing overrides, centralized sign-off from a command post. Patient flow improves while the surge lasts. When the surge eases, the exception paths stay open for flexibility. Local teams now work under more centralized permission than before the emergency.

When the administrator who managed the surge moves on, the expanded permission structure moves with the role. The incoming administrator inherits a position that already includes what was granted under urgency. Nobody is required to justify keeping it—urgency has already done the work of normalizing it. Rolling it back would mean taking something away from the role itself, not just changing a policy. Local leaders who joined after the surge have no prior baseline to compare it against. For them, this is just how the network runs.

Procedural channels for questioning centralized decisions may still exist on paper. In practice, the office now carries permissions that make pushback costly: challenging the template can sound like resisting coordination the system depends on. Example-based legitimacy—trust built by watching a leader’s judgment in ordinary times—gives way to office-based legitimacy as the story of the surge recedes and what remains is the org chart.

Whether anyone actively works to narrow those permissions afterward depends on incentives that rarely line up. Boards and clinical leaders may prefer stability to another wrenching redesign. Frontline staff adapt around the new baseline because their work is urgent and local. By the time a governance body schedules a serious review, the exception paths have their own defenders and their own vocabulary of necessity. The transfer moment that mattered was the handoff of the administrator’s office: after that, rollback becomes an optional project competing with everything else.

The pattern here is not unique to healthcare. It is what office-based legitimacy looks like when transfer is smooth and the inherited permissions are never explicitly reviewed: authority moves forward faster than accountability does, and what was temporary becomes structural before anyone decided it should be.

### **Religious Institutions and Congregational Networks**

The baseline in most established religious communities combines procedural and example-based legitimacy. A founding pastor, a long-tenured rabbi, a senior elder who has led through formative moments—these figures accumulate authority through visible example over time. The role they hold and the person holding it become difficult to separate. When others look to them, they are looking to the office and the example simultaneously.

Transfer becomes complex precisely because of that fusion. When a long-tenured leader steps down or dies, what passes to the successor is a title, a role, and a set of expectations shaped by everything the prior leader made the role mean. The successor inherits the expanded expectations alongside the formal authority. Example-based legitimacy, which has to be re-earned in visible conduct, can quietly convert to office-based legitimacy—people following because of what the position represents rather than because of what they have witnessed the current holder do. If the founding example was compelling enough, the office can carry that weight for a long time after the behavior that built it has changed or disappeared.

Sacralization pressure is most visible at transfer in religious settings. When a succession is framed as providential—the right person for this moment, chosen by something beyond ordinary process—the legitimacy of questioning that transfer can become spiritually costly. Asking whether the named successor is the right fit can sound like questioning something the community is not supposed to reopen. Challenge is recoded not as disloyalty to a person but as disloyalty to the community’s shared story about itself and its direction.

That pressure does not require bad faith. Communities that have found genuine meaning in shared narrative often experience challenge to succession as genuinely threatening—because the narrative and the leadership are not fully separable in their experience. The structural effect is the same regardless: paths for correction narrow at exactly the moment transfer makes correction most necessary.

When congregations grow into networks—multiple campuses, affiliated communities, shared leadership structures—the transfer dynamics scale with them. Local communities may never have direct access to the senior leader whose example originally anchored the network’s legitimacy. They inherit a culture shaped by that example without having witnessed it. Office-based and sacralized legitimacy do more of the work at the edges of the network because example-based legitimacy cannot travel that far. What reaches local communities is the template—the story of what leadership here looks like—rather than the lived example that built it.

### **What the Sequences Share**

The three sequences differ in stakes, in sector, and in how explicitly legitimacy is named within each institution. Religious communities often name the sacred dimension of authority directly; healthcare and government institutions tend to leave it implicit. Named or unnamed, the structural effect on correction and accountability is similar. What the sequences share is the transfer dynamic: authority that expands under pressure tends to stay expanded. The next holder inherits the expanded position, not the pre-pressure baseline. And the people who join after the expansion have no prior condition to compare it against—for them, the inherited baseline is simply what the role is.

Legitimacy transfer is not wrong in itself. Continuity requires some transfer.

The risk appears when transfer becomes automatic while limits become optional.

Where authority can grow without people deliberately pulling it back, accountability lags. Over time, that lag is where harm piles up.
